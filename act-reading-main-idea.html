American Diploma · ACT · Reading: Key Ideas and Details

Main Idea and Summary Questions

How to find the main idea of a whole ACT Reading passage or a single paragraph: predict first, then beat the too-narrow, too-broad and half-right traps.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Main idea questions ask you to state what a passage (or one paragraph) is mainly about and what it mainly says. They belong to the Key Ideas and Details category, which is the largest reporting category on ACT Reading: 44–52% of the scored questions. The scored part of the test has 3 passages with 9 questions each, and almost every passage set includes at least one big-picture question about the whole passage and often one about a single paragraph.

Question formTypical stem (in our own words)What it really asks
Whole passage — main idea"The main idea of the passage is that:" / "The passage mainly focuses on:"The one claim or story that the WHOLE text develops
Whole passage — summary"Which of the following best summarizes the passage?"The main idea plus the route the text takes to reach it
One paragraph"The main point of paragraph 4 is that:" / "Paragraph 2 mainly focuses on:"Only that paragraph, not the whole passage
Group of paragraphs"Which of the following best summarizes paragraphs 2 and 3?"Both paragraphs, joined in the right relationship
Literary passage"The passage is mainly about:" / "Which statement best describes what happens in the passage?"What happens AND what it comes to mean (usually shown at the end)

Many ACT Reading stems end with a colon, so the correct choice completes the sentence. Read the stem and each choice together as one sentence.

Topic, detail or main idea?

The most useful thing you can learn for these questions is the difference between three levels. A topic is a subject (one or two words). A detail is one fact or example. A main idea is a full claim about the topic that the whole text supports.

LevelExample (passage about public drinking fountains)Can it be the answer?
Topic✗ "Public drinking fountains."No: it names a subject but says nothing about it
Detail✗ "Wealthy families paid for the fountains and decorated them."No: true, but it is one sentence of support
Too broad✗ "Cities should restore all of their historic buildings."No: the passage never goes that far
Main idea✓ "Fountains that seemed outdated are proving useful again."Yes: every paragraph supports it

A quick test: could each paragraph of the passage be a reason or example for this sentence? If yes, it is the main idea. If it fits only one paragraph, it is too narrow. If you could write a different book about it, it is too broad.

The method: predict before you look

  1. Read the blurb. The line in capitals above the passage tells you the passage type (humanities, social science, natural science, literary narrative) and often the topic.
  2. Label each paragraph in about three words as you read: [1] signs vanishing = loss, [2] painters + conversation, [3] printing wins: time/price …
  3. Look hard at the first and last paragraphs. In informational passages the author's claim is usually stated in paragraph 1 and returned to at the end. Watch for the sentence after "however", "yet", "but" or "I want to suggest": that is often where the author's own view begins.
  4. Write your own one-sentence main idea before you read the choices: topic + what the author says about it. Example: "Painted signs lost out for good reasons, but they carry something personal worth keeping."
  5. Match, then eliminate. Choose the choice closest to your prediction, then check that every part of it is true and that it fits the beginning, middle and end.
  6. Answer the whole-passage question last. You may answer the questions in a set in any order. After answering the detail questions you understand the passage better, so leave question 1 (often the big-picture one) until the end of the set.
توقّع الإجابة قبل ما تقرا الاختيارات

قبل ما تبص على A B C D، اكتب الفكرة الرئيسية بكلامك في جملة واحدة: الموضوع + الكاتب بيقول إيه عنه. بعد كده دوّر على الاختيار اللي بيقول نفس المعنى. الإجابة الصح لازم تنفع لأول القطعة وآخرها، مش لبراجراف واحد بس.

The four wrong-answer shapes

Wrong choices on main-idea questions are not random. Almost every one fits one of these shapes. Learn to name them and you will eliminate faster and with more confidence.

TrapWhat it looks like✗ Example (fountains passage)
Too narrowA true detail from one paragraph, offered as the main idea of the whole✗ "Some fountains were turned into kiosks or storerooms."
Too broadA big general claim about the whole subject or the whole world✗ "Water has always shaped the history of cities."
Half-rightThe first half matches the passage; the second half is invented or reversed✗ "Fountains fell silent when water reached homes and are now kept only as museums."
Reversed / off-topicUses the passage's words but says the opposite, or talks about something the passage barely mentions✗ "Planners believe fountains waste water."

Half-right choices are the most dangerous, because you agree with the first words and stop reading. Always read a choice to its final word. Too broad choices often contain words such as *all, always, history of, people in general, every*. Too narrow choices usually contain a specific name, number or example from the middle of the passage.

Paragraph main ideas

When the question names one paragraph, the main idea of the WHOLE passage is often a trap. Stay inside the paragraph.

  • Look first at the opening sentence, then at any sentence that begins with a contrast word (however, but, yet, in fact). Paragraph 4 of the practice passage begins "It would be wrong, however, to describe the old signs only with regret" — that sentence is the paragraph's point.
  • Ask what job the paragraph does in the passage: background, a problem, a counter-argument, an example, the conclusion. Its main idea fits that job.
  • Summaries of two paragraphs must include both paragraphs AND the link between them (then, but, because, as a result).
  • ✓ "Paragraph 4: the move to printed signs had real benefits." ✗ "Paragraph 4: painted signs are better than printed ones" (that is the opposite of what this paragraph is doing).

Main idea in a literary narrative

In fiction and memoir, the "main idea" is what happens and what it comes to mean for the narrator or character. Students often pick the most exciting moment, but an exciting moment is still a detail. The meaning is usually shown near the end: a change in feeling, a new understanding, a decision.

  • ✗ "The narrator's car breaks down on the desert road." (one event — too narrow)
  • ✗ "The narrator learns that all old things are valuable." (too broad — a moral the text never states)
  • ✓ "The narrator comes to see his father's old car differently after learning what it has meant to the family." (event + meaning + change)

Worked examples

Read this short excerpt (shorter than a real ACT passage, so we can focus on the method). Predict the main idea yourself before reading the examples.

[1] For centuries, public drinking fountains were among the most generous buildings a city could offer. Wealthy families paid for them, decorated them with carved stone and inscriptions, and placed them where travellers and workers passed. A cup of clean water was free to anyone who stopped. [2] As piped water reached private homes in the last century, many fountains fell silent. Their taps were removed, their basins filled with dust, and some were turned into kiosks or storerooms. City officials saw little reason to repair buildings that no longer seemed necessary. [3] Recently, however, several cities have begun restoring their fountains, not as museums but as working sources of water. Planners point to hotter summers, to the cost and waste of plastic bottles, and to the simple pleasure of a shaded place to stop. The old fountains, it turns out, were not outdated; they were waiting for a problem they could help to solve.

Prediction: "Fountains lost their use when homes got water, but they are becoming useful again." Notice that the last sentence states it almost directly.

Worked example 1 — whole-passage main idea

Question: The main idea of the excerpt is that public drinking fountains:

  • A) were decorated with carved stone by wealthy families.
  • B) lost their purpose when water reached homes but are proving useful again today.
  • C) fell out of use when water reached homes and are now preserved as museums.
  • D) show that cities must restore every historic building they own.

Answer: B. It covers all three paragraphs: generous past (1), decline (2), new usefulness (3). It matches our prediction.

  • A is wrong — Too narrow: one detail from paragraph 1.
  • C is wrong — Half-right: the first half is true, but paragraph 3 says "not as museums" — the second half is reversed.
  • D is wrong — Too broad: the excerpt is about fountains, not every historic building, and "must" is far stronger than anything stated.

Worked example 2 — paragraph main idea

Question: Paragraph 2 mainly focuses on:

  • A) the reasons planners want to restore the fountains.
  • B) the ways some fountains were turned into kiosks.
  • C) the generosity of the families who built the fountains.
  • D) the decline of the fountains after piped water reached private homes.

Answer: D. Paragraph 2 opens with the cause (piped water) and then lists the results: silent taps, dust, new uses, no repairs. D is the paragraph's job: describing the decline.

  • A is wrong — Wrong paragraph: the planners' reasons are in paragraph 3.
  • B is wrong — Too narrow: kiosks are one example inside the paragraph.
  • C is wrong — Wrong paragraph: the families appear in paragraph 1.

Worked example 3 — summary

Question: Which of the following best summarizes how the excerpt develops?

  • A) It describes a problem, proposes three solutions, and chooses the best one.
  • B) It argues that piped water was a mistake and that homes should use public fountains instead.
  • C) It traces the fountains from a valued past, through neglect, to a practical return.
  • D) It compares fountains in several cities and ranks them by their decoration.

Answer: C. A summary must follow the route of the text: past value → neglect → return. C does exactly that, in the right order.

  • A is wrong — Wrong structure: there is no problem-then-solutions pattern; the reasons in paragraph 3 explain the restoration, they are not competing solutions.
  • B is wrong — Reversed and too extreme: the author never criticizes piped water.
  • D is wrong — Off-topic: no ranking or comparison of cities appears.

Now a short literary excerpt. Remember: event + meaning.

For years I was embarrassed by my father's car. It was older than I was, its blue paint faded to the colour of washed jeans, and it coughed each time he started it. I asked him to drop me at the corner, not at the school gate. One winter evening it broke down on the desert road, and we waited two hours for help. He did not complain once. Instead he told me how he had bought it with his first year's salary, and how he had driven my mother to the hospital in it the night I was born. The next morning, I asked him to drop me at the gate.

Worked example 4 — literary main idea

Question: The excerpt is mainly about the narrator:

  • A) changing his feelings about his father's car after learning what it has meant to his family.
  • B) waiting two hours for help when the car broke down on the desert road.
  • C) learning that he should never judge anything by its appearance.
  • D) hiding his father's car from his school friends for many years and continuing to do so.

Answer: A. The excerpt moves from embarrassment (beginning) to a story about the car's history (middle) to a changed request ("drop me at the gate"). A includes the change and its cause.

  • B is wrong — Too narrow: the breakdown is the event that starts the change, not the whole story.
  • C is wrong — Too broad: a general moral ("never … anything") the text does not state.
  • D is wrong — Half-right: he did hide the car, but the last sentence shows he stopped. The end of the choice reverses the ending.
Classic traps
  • The favourite detail. A vivid fact or the most exciting event feels important, but it is still a detail. Ask: does every paragraph support it?
  • The half-right ending. The choice starts perfectly and changes one thing at the end ("…and are now kept as museums"). Read to the last word.
  • The big moral. Choices with *all, always, never, everyone, the history of* usually claim more than the passage does.
  • The wrong level. Answering a paragraph question with the passage's main idea, or a passage question with one paragraph's idea.
  • Forgetting the turn. Many passages present a common view and then disagree with it ("It is easy to treat this as progress. I want to suggest…"). The main idea is the author's view, not the view the author rejects.
  • Missing the ending in fiction. The meaning of a story is usually shown in the final paragraph; a choice that ignores the ending is too narrow.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Label each paragraph in about three words while you read; say the main idea in one sentence at the end.
  • Skip the whole-passage question, answer the detail questions, then come back to it.
  • Predict, then match: topic + what the author says about it.
  • Test each choice against the first AND the last paragraph.
  • Eliminate by naming the trap: too narrow, too broad, half-right, reversed.
أشهر 3 فخاخ في الـ Main Idea

Too narrow = تفصيلة واحدة من براجراف واحد. Too broad = كلام عام كبير عن الدنيا كلها مش عن القطعة. Half-right = أول الجملة صح وآخرها غلط، فاقرا الاختيار لحد آخر كلمة. الإجابة الصح هي اللي كل براجراف في القطعة يقدر يكون دليل عليها.

Practice

Now try a full humanities passage (about 700 words) with 9 questions, most of them main-idea, paragraph-point and summary questions. It is untimed, but aim for about 10 minutes. Label each paragraph as you read and predict before you look at the choices.

Remember
  • Key Ideas and Details = 44–52% of scored ACT Reading questions; main-idea questions are part of it.
  • Main idea = topic + what the author says about it, supported by every paragraph.
  • Predict in one sentence before reading the choices, then match.
  • Eliminate too narrow, too broad, half-right and reversed choices; read every choice to the last word.
  • For a paragraph question stay inside that paragraph; in fiction include the ending and what it means.
