Writer's Goal: Choosing Content That Meets a Purpose

How to answer "Which choice most effectively…" and "Suppose the writer's primary purpose had been…" questions by matching the exact goal words and judging what the essay really does.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Writer's-goal questions belong to Production of Writing, the reporting category that is 38–43% of the ACT English score. They test whether you can choose the content that does a stated job. The stem tells you the job; your task is to find the one choice that does exactly that job — not the one that is simply true, correct or attractive.

They come in two main forms:

FormTypical stem (in our own words)Where it appears
Goal for one part"The writer wants to emphasise / show / give a specific example of … Which choice best accomplishes this goal?" or simply "Which choice most effectively …?"On an underlined word, phrase or sentence anywhere in the essay
Goal for the whole essay"Suppose the writer's primary purpose had been to … Would this essay accomplish that purpose?"Usually the last question of an essay

In the first form, all four choices are usually grammatical and on the general topic. Three of them do a slightly different job. In the second form, two choices say Yes and two say No, each with a reason; you must judge the essay and then the reason.

اقرا المطلوب بالظبط

في أسئلة Which choice most effectively… كل الاختيارات جمل سليمة. علّم على الكلمات اللي بتقول الهدف (specific / emphasise / picture / contrast / introduce / conclude) واسأل عن كل اختيار: هو بيعمل الهدف ده بالظبط؟ مش: هو جملة حلوة؟

The method for "Which choice most effectively …"

  1. Underline the goal words in the stem, for example: "specific sense of HOW MANY," "emphasise how HARD," "show TRUST in the writer's skill."
  2. Split two-part goals. "Admit the changes AND show the market feels the same" needs both halves. A choice that does only one half is wrong.
  3. Test every choice against the goal words only. Ask "Does this show trust?" — not "Is this true? Is this a good sentence?"
  4. Look for the exact focus. A choice can match the topic but miss the job: liking the shop is not trusting the writer; a long trip is not a hard trip.
  5. Read the sentence before and after. The right choice must also fit the paragraph; if it contradicts the next sentence, it is wrong.

Goal words and what the right choice contains

Goal in the stemThe right choice …Typical wrong choices
most specific / most detailed / most precisehas names, numbers, exact places, concrete actionsvague words: many, some, various, a lot, near, things
help the reader picture / visualisegives a concrete image, often a comparison or sensory detailstates the idea in abstract words ("it was small")
emphasise [a quality]shows that exact quality with evidenceshows a near-quality (long ≠ hard; popular ≠ trusted)
show a contrast with what came beforecontains an opposite ideaadds a new idea in the same direction
give an example / illustrategives one concrete case of the claimrepeats the claim in general words
introduce the paragraph / the essaynames the topic the next sentences developtoo broad, too narrow, or off-topic
conclude / return to the openingcloses the main idea, often echoing the first paragrapha new topic or a small new detail
be most relevant to the paragraph's focuslinks directly to what the paragraph is abouttrue facts about something nearby

Choosing the detail that meets a stated purpose

Most goal questions are about details. Think of the goal as a filter: let through only the detail that serves it.

  • Goal: show how dedicated the volunteers were.
  • ✓ volunteers, who did not miss a single week even during exams, (dedication: never missing)
  • ✗ volunteers from several different grades (variety, not dedication)
  • Goal: give the most specific location of the collection point.
  • ✓ beside the main gate, next to the guard's room (two exact landmarks)
  • ✗ somewhere on the school grounds (vague)
  • Goal: show that the principal was persuaded by the evidence.
  • ✓ After studying our chart of the week's totals, she agreed (links her decision to the data)
  • ✗ She smiled warmly and agreed (kindness, not evidence)
  • Goal: emphasise how hard the journey was.
  • ✓ men and animals sometimes went several days between wells
  • ✗ The trip was long. (length, not hardship)

Two-part goals are where many strong students lose marks. If the stem says "to show both the size of the crowd and its mood," a choice with a number but no feeling, or a feeling but no number, is wrong.

  • Goal: show both the size of the crowd and its excitement.
  • ✓ More than five thousand fans jumped and sang for the whole second half. (size + excitement)
  • ✗ More than five thousand fans filled the stadium. (size only)
  • ✗ The fans were thrilled. (excitement only)
الهدف اللي ليه جزئين

لو السؤال طالب حاجتين (both … and …)، لازم الاختيار يعمل الاتنين. اختيار بيعمل نص الهدف بس يبقى غلط حتى لو كلامه صح. ولو مطلوب specific دوّر على أرقام وأسماء وأماكن محددة، مش كلمات زي many أو some.

The method for "Suppose the writer's primary purpose had been …"

  1. Read the hypothetical purpose carefully. Note the topic (bicycle repair, factory recycling) AND the kind of essay (explain steps, argue, compare, tell a personal story, give a history). Note the scope: one person's experience, or a general explanation?
  2. Say in one sentence what the essay really does, for example: "A teenager tells how one summer in his uncle's shop taught him to work with his hands."
  3. Compare. If the topic, the kind of essay and the scope all match, the answer is Yes. If any one differs, it is No. The word "primary" matters: a topic that appears in one paragraph is not the primary purpose.
  4. Choose the reason that describes the essay accurately. Cross out reasons that are false about the essay, and reasons that treat a small detail as the main point.
Hypothetical purposeReal essayDecision
explain the steps of repairing a bicyclea personal story about a summer in a repair shopNo — story, not a how-to explanation
describe a family member's craft in a personal essaythe writer describes her grandfather's carpentry workshop and the chair he made for herYes — personal and about his craft
argue that cities should build rooftop gardensneighbours describe how they built one rooftop gardenNo — a personal account, not an argument about cities
explain how paper is recycled in factoriesa student council's paper-collection project at one schoolNo — a school project, not factory processes

On these questions, the right answer is often No, because the stated purpose is usually close to the essay but not the same. Do not guess No automatically, though: check the match each time.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — specific detail

Passage: Wildlife officers on the Red Sea coast count the dugongs that feed on sea grass near the shore. __The animals are hard to see.__

The writer wants to give a specific reason why counting the dugongs is difficult. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) The animals are shy and can stay underwater for several minutes at a time.
  • C) The dugong is a distant relative of the elephant.
  • D) Many tourists hope to see a dugong.

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: True but vague — it gives no specific reason.
  • B) ✓ Correct: Two specific reasons the animals are hard to count.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: An interesting fact, but about the animal's family, not about counting.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Off-topic: tourists, not counting.

Worked example 2 — emphasise a quality

Passage: Every Thursday, __volunteers__ carried the full recycling boxes to the collection point.

The writer wants to emphasise how dedicated the volunteers were. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) a group of volunteers
  • C) volunteers from several different grades
  • D) volunteers, who did not miss a single week even during exams,

Answer: D

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Says nothing about dedication.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Adds a word but no quality.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: A near-quality: variety, not dedication.
  • D) ✓ Correct: Never missing a week, even during exams, is proof of dedication.

Worked example 3 — two-part goal

Passage: After the final whistle, __the fans left the stadium.__

The writer wants to show both how many fans there were and how happy they were. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) thousands of fans poured out of the stadium, singing and waving flags.
  • C) the fans were delighted.
  • D) more than forty thousand fans left the stadium.

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Neither part of the goal.
  • B) ✓ Correct: Both parts: thousands (number) + singing and waving flags (happiness).
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Happiness only — no number.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Number only — no happiness.

Worked example 4 — primary purpose

Passage: (Imagine an essay in which a student describes her grandfather's carpentry workshop: the smell of wood, the tools on the wall, and the chair he built for her tenth birthday.)

Suppose the writer's primary purpose had been to write a personal essay about a family member's craft. Would this essay accomplish that purpose?

  • A) Yes, because it describes the grandfather's workshop and a piece of furniture he made for the writer.
  • B) Yes, because it explains the history of carpentry in Egypt.
  • C) No, because it focuses on the writer's own hobby.
  • D) No, because it gives instructions for building a chair.

Answer: A

  • A) ✓ Correct: The essay is personal and is about the grandfather's craft — topic, kind and scope all match, and the reason is accurate.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Right decision, false reason: the essay has no history of carpentry.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: False: the craft is the grandfather's, not the writer's.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: False: the essay describes a chair; it gives no instructions.
Classic traps
  • True but not the job: a correct, pleasant sentence that does a different job from the stated goal.
  • Near-quality: variety instead of dedication, liking instead of trust, long instead of hard.
  • Vague choice for a "specific" goal: many, some, various, a lot, near the gate.
  • Half a two-part goal: a choice that does only one of the two things asked.
  • Opposite of the goal: "My first patches were fine" when the goal is to show difficulty.
  • Small detail as the main point in "Suppose…" questions: one paragraph about patching tyres does not make a how-to essay.
  • Right decision, wrong reason: Yes/No is correct but the reason describes the essay falsely.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Underline the goal words before you read the choices.
  • Cross out every choice that does a different job, however good it sounds.
  • For "specific," look for numbers, names and exact places; for "picture," look for a concrete image.
  • For two-part goals, tick each part separately.
  • Answer "Suppose…" questions last, after summarising the whole essay in one sentence.

Practice

Read the two essays and answer the 10 questions. Each question states a goal; choose the option that meets it. Question 10 asks about the second essay as a whole.

Remember
  • Match the exact goal words — not just a true, nice sentence.
  • "Specific" = numbers, names, exact places; "picture" = a concrete image.
  • Two-part goals need both parts.
  • "Suppose…": compare topic, kind of essay and scope with what the essay really does; then check the reason is true.
  • A detail mentioned once is never the essay's primary purpose.
