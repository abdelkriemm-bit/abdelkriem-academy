Checkpoint 6 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Fiction Writing Basics: Everything for Paper 2 Writing

Every element of a Paper 2 story — character, setting, plot, imagery, dialogue, tense and point of view — plus sentence starters, the G.C.S.P.P. planning technique, how to write an opening, a continuation or a full story, and two strong samples.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is Paper 2 writing?

Paper 2 of Cambridge Primary Checkpoint English (0058/02) is the Fiction paper. It lasts 1 hour and is worth 50 marks. Section A is Reading (25 marks) and Section B is Writing (25 marks). The suggested time is 30 minutes for each section. In Section B you write a piece of fiction: a story, or part of a story.

The writing task is usually linked to the theme of the reading text you have just read. You are not copying that text. You are writing something new with your own ideas. The question often gives you a few bullet points or ideas to think about. Use them: they tell you what the examiner hopes to see.

Kind of taskWhat you are asked to doWhat the examiner looks for
Story openingWrite only the beginning of a story (for example, characters setting out to help someone).A strong first line, a clear setting, a main character we care about, a problem that starts to appear, and a hook at the end. You do NOT finish the story.
Story continuationCarry on the story from the reading text, using your own ideas.The SAME characters, setting, point of view, tense and mood as the extract. The story moves forward logically from where it stopped.
Complete story on a themeWrite a whole story about a given idea (for example, a place that matters to a character and something that happens there).A clear beginning, build-up, problem, climax and ending, all linked to the theme.
Description inside a storyDescribe a setting or a character as part of a narrative.Vivid detail using the senses, figurative language and strong verbs, while the story still moves.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0.
  • An answer of 21-60 words can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example, a diary or a letter when the task asks for a story), Creation of texts can be capped at 1 and Structure of texts at 3 (sometimes 2).
  • If the task says ‘opening’, write an opening. If it says ‘continue’, continue. Answer the task you are given, not the one you prepared.

2. How your story is marked (25 marks)

The examiner gives you five separate marks and adds them together. The marks are the same on Paper 1 and Paper 2, but in Paper 2 they are about storytelling.

CriterionMarksWhat it means in a storyWhat a top answer does
Creation of texts5Is it a real, engaging story that fits the task and the reader?Answers every part of the task, keeps the right genre, uses a consistent narrator, and makes the reader want to read on.
Vocabulary and language3Are your words chosen carefully?Precise verbs and adjectives, imagery (similes, metaphors, personification), words that build the mood.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7Is the story well built?Clear paragraphs, a strong opening, a build-up, a climax and a satisfying ending (or a hook, for an opening). Paragraphs link smoothly. The pace changes where it should.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation & grammar)7Are the sentences correct and varied?Simple, compound and complex sentences, different sentence starters, the past tense kept steady, speech punctuated correctly, punctuation used for effect.
Word structure (spelling)3Are the words spelled correctly?Common and harder words spelled accurately, including plurals, past-tense endings and prefixes/suffixes.

Look at the numbers. Structure and Grammar and punctuation are worth 14 of the 25 marks together. A simple story told in well-organised paragraphs with accurate, varied sentences will beat a clever idea written in one long, messy paragraph.

3. All the elements of a story

These are the tools a story writer uses. You will not use every one in a 30-minute story, but you should know them all, because the reading questions in Section A also ask about them.

ElementWhat it meansExample
CharactersThe people, animals or creatures in the story. Keep it to two or three in the exam.Omar, his grandad and the pigeons.
Direct characterisationThe writer TELLS us what a character is like.Laila was brave and kind.
Indirect characterisationThe writer SHOWS us through speech, thoughts, effect on others, actions and looks (STEAL).Laila stepped in front of the younger boy and said, “Leave him alone.”
SettingWhere and when the story happens.A noisy rooftop in Shubra on a windy Friday afternoon.
ImageryDescription that makes the reader see, hear, smell, taste or feel.The smell of hot bread drifted out of the bakery.
SimileA comparison using ‘like’ or ‘as’.The paint peeled like old orange skin.
MetaphorA comparison that says something IS something else.The classroom was a beehive.
PersonificationGiving human actions or feelings to a thing.The wind tugged at my sleeve.
OnomatopoeiaA word that sounds like the noise.Clack. Crash. Hiss.
AlliterationWords close together starting with the same sound.The silent, silver sea.
PlotThe events of the story, in order.Opening → build-up → problem → climax → ending.
Conflict / problemThe difficulty the character must face.The violin must be returned, but the children are not allowed to travel alone.
ThemeThe deeper message.Responsibility · kindness · courage.
ForeshadowingA small hint about what will happen later.Neither of them noticed the man in the next carriage.
SuspenseThe ‘what happens next?’ feeling.Something moved behind the curtain. Then it stopped.
DialogueThe words characters say, inside speech marks.“We have to take it back,” Nour said.
Point of view (POV)Who tells the story: first person (I) or third person (he/she/they).First person: I climbed the steps. Third person: Youssef lifted the case.
TenseStories are normally told in the past tense.He walked, she whispered, they had gone.

4. Plot: the story mountain

Most good stories climb a ‘mountain’. The tension rises to the top (the climax) and then comes down to the ending.

StageWhat happensParagraphs in a 30-minute story
1. Opening (exposition)Introduce the main character and the setting. Hook the reader.1 paragraph
2. Build-up (rising action)Something starts to happen. Small hints and details.1–2 paragraphs
3. ProblemThe difficulty or surprise appears.1 paragraph (can be short)
4. ClimaxThe most exciting or emotional moment. Slow it down.1–2 paragraphs
5. Ending (resolution)The problem is solved or the character changes. Finish with a strong last line.1 paragraph
Pace earns structure marks

Do not spend half your story on breakfast. Move quickly through ordinary moments (‘After school, I hurried home.’) and slow down at the important moment, with short sentences, details and feelings. This change of speed is called pace, and examiners reward it.

5. The G.C.S.P.P. technique — plan before you write

Before you write a single sentence, spend three or four minutes breaking down the task with G.C.S.P.P. Each letter is a decision. If you make the decisions first, you will not change your mind in the middle of the story.

LetterDecisionQuestions to ask yourself
G — GenreWhat kind of story is it?Adventure, mystery, realistic fiction, fantasy, horror? The genre decides your mood and vocabulary.
C — CharactersWho is in it?Who is the main character? Who else? (Two or three is enough.) What is each one like? How are they connected?
S — SettingWhere and when?If the task gives a setting, use it. If not, choose one that suits the genre and that you can describe well.
P — Point of viewWho tells it?First person (I) feels close and personal. Third person (he/she) lets you show several characters. For a continuation, use the SAME POV as the extract.
P — PlotWhich part of the story am I writing?The whole story? Only the opening? A continuation? Plan the events for that part only.
Worked example: G.C.S.P.P. on a task
  • Task: ‘Write a story about a place that is special to a character and something surprising that happens there.’
  • G — Realistic fiction (an everyday, believable story).
  • C — Omar (11, quiet, misses his grandad) · Grandad (on the phone only) · the pigeons.
  • S — The roof of a block of flats in Shubra, Cairo, on a windy Friday.
  • P — First person (I), so the reader feels Omar’s feelings.
  • P — Whole story: Omar feeds the pigeons → misses Grandad → finds a loose board → a tin box with a note and a ring → phones Grandad → the tower ‘feels like ours’.
  • The sample story in section 13 is written from exactly this plan.
Continuation tasks: lock in five things
  • Same characters, with the same personalities and names.
  • Same setting (unless the story clearly moves somewhere).
  • Same point of view: if the extract says ‘she’, do not suddenly write ‘I’.
  • Same tense: if the extract is in the past tense, stay in the past tense.
  • Same mood: if the extract is tense and creepy, do not turn it into a comedy.
  • Start exactly where the extract stopped. Do not repeat what already happened.

6. Opening, continuation or full story: how each one works

The biggest mistake in Paper 2 is writing the wrong ‘amount’ of story. Read the task word by word and decide which of these three you are writing.

Story openingStory continuationComplete story
How much plot?Only the start: introduce the character, the setting and the first sign of a problem.The next part: what happens after the extract, up to a climax and usually an ending.All five stages of the story mountain.
Where most words goSetting and character description, plus a short scene with dialogue.Action and events that grow out of the extract.Shared across the stages, with the most detail at the climax.
How to endWith a hook: a question, a surprise, a hint of danger. Do NOT solve the problem.With a clear resolution, or a deliberate cliffhanger if the task allows it.With a satisfying ending: the problem solved, a lesson learned, or a circular ending.
Biggest dangerRushing to the end of the whole story.Changing names, POV, tense or mood.Spending too long on the beginning and rushing the climax.
  1. Opening — plan in four steps: (1) a hook first sentence · (2) a paragraph of setting using three senses · (3) the main character shown through STEAL · (4) the first hint of the problem, ending on a hook.
  2. Continuation — plan in four steps: (1) note the characters, setting, POV, tense and mood of the extract · (2) start at the exact moment it stopped · (3) build to a climax with suspense · (4) end in a way that fits the story so far.
  3. Complete story — plan in five steps: one line for each stage of the story mountain, and one word for the theme you want the ending to show.

7. Sentence patterns and sentence starters

If every sentence starts with ‘I’ or ‘He’ or ‘The’, the story sounds flat. These eight starters make your writing lively and earn Grammar and punctuation marks. Notice that most of them are followed by a comma before the main clause.

StarterPatternPlain sentenceWith the starter
-ing starterBegin with an -ing verb to show action happening.Mariam ran to the gate.Racing past the swings, Mariam reached the gate first.
to starterBegin with ‘to + verb’ to show purpose.He climbed on the chair to see.To see over the wall, he climbed onto the wobbly chair.
Prepositional phraseBegin with a place or time phrase.The cat slept.Under the warm car, the cat slept peacefully.
OnomatopoeiaBegin with a sound word.The door shut loudly.Bang! The door slammed behind them.
-ed starterBegin with an -ed word that describes a state.The bike was old and was left outside.Forgotten in the rain, the old bike rusted slowly.
Dependent clauseBegin with as, when, although, because, if, before, after…She heard a noise and stopped.As she reached the top step, she heard a noise.
Quotation (speech)Begin with something a character says.Dad told us to hurry.“Hurry up, the bus is leaving!” Dad shouted.
AdverbBegin with an adverb of manner or time.He opened the box slowly.Slowly, carefully, he opened the box.

Sentence types. Use all three kinds, and choose them for effect:

TypeWhat it isExampleBest used for
SimpleOne main clause.The light went out.Shock, tension, a dramatic moment.
CompoundTwo main clauses joined by and, but, or, so.The light went out, and the room fell silent.Linking two connected actions.
ComplexA main clause plus a dependent clause.When the light went out, the room fell silent.Description, explanation, showing what causes what.

8. Dialogue: how to punctuate speech

A few lines of speech bring a character to life and move the plot forward. But speech that is punctuated wrongly costs Grammar and punctuation marks. Learn these rules.

  1. Put speech marks around the exact words spoken only: “I’m lost,” said Nour.
  2. Start the spoken words with a capital letter: Grandad said, “Feed them first.”
  3. Put the punctuation that ends the speech INSIDE the speech marks: “Run!” “Are you sure?” “I know,”
  4. If the speech comes first and the sentence continues, end the speech with a comma (not a full stop), then a small letter: “It’s late,” she said.
  5. If the reporting clause comes first, put a comma before the speech marks open: Youssef said, “Let’s go.”
  6. If one sentence of speech is split, the second part starts with a small letter: “If we hurry,” said Nour, “we can catch him.”
  7. Start a NEW LINE every time a new person speaks.
  8. Keep dialogue short. Two or three exchanges are enough. Speech should show character or move the story — not fill space with ‘Hi.’ ‘Hi.’ ‘How are you?’
WrongCorrectRule
“We have to go.” She said.“We have to go,” she said.Comma inside the speech marks · small letter for ‘she’.
He shouted “stop”!He shouted, “Stop!”Comma before the speech · capital letter · ! inside.
“Where are you” asked Mum?“Where are you?” asked Mum.The question mark belongs to the speech.
“I’m tired,” said Adam. “Me too,” said Laila. (on one line)Put Laila’s words on a new line.New speaker, new line.
Better than ‘said’

whispered, muttered, called, shouted, cried, gasped, giggled, sighed, snapped, begged, replied, explained, admitted, announced. Choose the verb that shows HOW the words were said — but do not change it every time. ‘Said’ is fine sometimes.

9. Punctuation for effect

Good writers use punctuation to control how the reader reads. These are the uses you should know and use:

  • Ellipsis ( … ) — to show a pause or suspense, information left incomplete, or a speaker being interrupted: ‘The door opened slowly…’ / “I thought I saw…”
  • Single dash ( – ) — for emphasis at the end of a sentence, or to show an interruption in speech: ‘He thought of the old man’s face – the panic in it.’ / “Look, he’s – ”
  • Pair of dashes — to add extra information in the middle of a sentence: ‘The tower – built before I was born – leaned a little to the left.’
  • Brackets — for extra information: ‘My cousin (who never stops talking) was silent for once.’
  • Colon ( : ) — to introduce a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information: ‘Then Youssef saw it: a battered violin case.’
  • Exclamation mark — for shock, strong feeling or a shout. Use it in speech, and use it rarely in narration.
  • Question mark in narration — to share the character’s worry with the reader: ‘Who had left it there?’

Endings to avoid
  • ‘And then I woke up. It was all a dream.’ It throws away the whole story.
  • ‘The End.’ written instead of a real last sentence.
  • Stopping suddenly because time ran out. Leave five minutes for your ending.

13. Strong sample 1 — a complete story

The Rooftop (realistic fiction, complete story)316 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Write a story about a place that is special to a character and something surprising that happens there. You could think about: why the place matters to the character · what the character finds or what happens · how the character feels at the end.

Every afternoon, I climbed the forty-two steps to the roof of our building in Shubra. Up there, above the honking cars and the shouting vegetable sellers, stood the wooden pigeon tower that Grandad had built before I was born. It was my favourite place in the whole of Cairo.

Since Grandad had moved to Alexandria to live with my aunt, the roof felt different. The paint on the tower was peeling like old orange skin, and the pigeons cooed softly, as if they were asking where he had gone. I fed them every day, just as he had shown me, but it never felt quite right.

One windy Friday, something strange happened. As I scattered the seeds, I noticed that a board at the bottom of the tower was loose. It rattled in the wind. Clack. Clack. Carefully, I knelt down and pulled it away.

Behind it was a small tin box, grey with dust.

My hands trembled as I lifted the lid. Inside, there was a folded note and a tiny silver ring: the kind that fits around a pigeon’s leg. I unfolded the paper. In Grandad’s wobbly handwriting, it said, “For Omar, when the roof is his.”

I sat on the warm concrete for a long time. Below me, the city carried on with its noise, but I could only hear my own heartbeat. Grandad had known. He had known that one day I would look after his birds on my own.

That evening, I phoned him.

“Did you find it, habibi?” he asked, and I could hear that he was smiling.

“I found it,” I whispered. “I’ll take care of them, Grandad. I promise.”

The next morning, I slipped the silver ring onto the leg of the youngest pigeon. When it rose into the pink sky and circled the rooftops of Shubra, the tower no longer felt like Grandad’s. It felt like ours.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: every idea in the task is covered — why the roof matters (paragraphs 1–2), the surprise (paragraphs 3–5) and Omar’s feelings at the end (paragraphs 6–10). The first-person narrator stays consistent, and the theme of family and responsibility is shown, not lectured.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise verbs (scattered, knelt, trembled, slipped, circled) · a simile that suits the setting (peeling ‘like old orange skin’) · personification of the pigeons ‘asking where he had gone’ · onomatopoeia ‘Clack. Clack.’
  • Structure of texts 7/7: a full story mountain — opening with the setting, a build-up showing that Omar misses Grandad, the problem/surprise, a slowed-down climax with the note, and a resolution. The one-line paragraph ‘Behind it was a small tin box…’ creates a pause. The circular ending (‘It felt like ours.’) links back to Grandad’s tower in paragraph 1. Time phrases (Every afternoon, One windy Friday, That evening, The next morning) link the paragraphs.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: a variety of sentence types — simple (‘Grandad had known.’), compound and complex (‘As I scattered the seeds, I noticed…’) · varied starters (prepositional ‘Up there…’, adverb ‘Carefully,’, dependent clause ‘Since Grandad had moved…’) · a colon introducing extra information (‘a tiny silver ring: the kind…’) · dialogue punctuated correctly with a new line for each speaker · the past perfect used accurately for earlier events (‘had built’, ‘had moved’).
  • Spelling 3/3: harder words correct — favourite, trembled, handwriting, concrete, whispered, circled, youngest.
  • Word count: inside the 250–350 range — long enough to develop the story, short enough to write and check in 30 minutes.

14. Strong sample 2 — a story opening

Compare this with sample 1. An opening does NOT solve the problem. It sets up the characters, the setting and the problem, then stops on a hook.

The Violin Case (adventure, story opening)271 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Write the opening of a story in which two characters set out on a journey to return something important to its owner.

The train to Fayoum was already pulling away when Youssef saw it: a battered black violin case, lying alone on the seat opposite.

“Someone’s forgotten it,” whispered his little sister, Nour. She pressed her nose against the window and searched the crowded platform. “Look! That old man in the grey coat – he’s waving at us!”

It was too late. The train shuddered, groaned and rolled out of Ramses Station, and the old man shrank on the platform like a full stop at the end of a sentence.

Youssef lifted the case onto his knees. It was heavier than he expected, and it smelled of wood polish and dust. A brass label was screwed to the handle. In tiny letters, it read: Hassan Fawzi, Shakshouk Village, Lake Qarun.

“We have to take it back to him,” Nour said at once. She always decided things at once.

Youssef frowned. Shakshouk was at the far edge of the lake, nearly two hours beyond their aunt’s house. They had forty pounds, one bottle of water and a bag of biscuits. Mama had said, very clearly, “Straight to Aunt Samia’s. No adventures.”

Outside, the city melted away. Fields of bright green clover slid past, and white egrets lifted lazily from the canals. Youssef stared at the case. Somewhere inside it, he imagined, was a song that belonged to someone else.

He thought of the old man’s face – the panic in it.

“All right,” he said slowly. “But we tell no one until we’ve found him.”

Nour grinned. Neither of them noticed the man in the next carriage, who had been watching the violin case since Cairo.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: this is clearly an opening — the characters, the setting (a train from Ramses Station to Fayoum) and the problem (returning the violin) are set up, and the journey has not happened yet. The two characters are different: Nour decides ‘at once’, Youssef thinks carefully.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: a fresh simile (the old man shrinking ‘like a full stop at the end of a sentence’) · strong verbs (shuddered, groaned, melted away, slid past, lifted lazily) · the senses (the smell of wood polish and dust, green clover, white egrets).
  • Structure of texts 7/7: the first sentence hooks the reader with a colon reveal · the paragraphs move from discovery → decision → difficulty (money, Mama’s rule) → the setting outside → Youssef’s choice. The last line is foreshadowing: a mysterious watcher creates a hook for the rest of the story.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: speech punctuated correctly, including a reporting clause before speech (‘Mama had said, very clearly, “Straight to…”’) · a single dash for emphasis in speech (‘– he’s waving at us!’) and for emphasis (‘– the panic in it’) · varied sentence lengths, including the short sentence ‘She always decided things at once.’ for character · consistent past tense and third-person POV.
  • Spelling 3/3: battered, opposite, shuddered, imagined, carriage, polish, egrets — all accurate.
  • STEAL is used without being forced: Nour’s speech and actions show she is impulsive, while Youssef’s frown and thoughts show he is careful.

16. Practice

  1. Read the task: ‘Write the opening of a story about a child who discovers a hidden room in an old building.’ Is this an opening, a continuation or a complete story? What must you NOT do?

    Show answer
    It is an opening. You must not tell the whole story or solve the mystery of the room. Introduce the child, describe the building, show the discovery of the room, and end on a hook (for example, a sound from inside the room).

  2. Make a G.C.S.P.P. plan for: ‘Write a story about a character who has to be brave to help someone else.’

    Show answer
    G — adventure or realistic fiction. C — Salma (10, shy, afraid of water) and her little brother Ali. S — a beach in Ras El Bar in summer. P — third person (she). P — whole story: (1) Salma refuses to swim · (2) Ali’s ball floats away and he follows it · (3) he is pulled out by a wave · (4) climax — Salma grabs a body board and paddles to him, heart pounding · (5) ending — wrapped in towels, Ali says, “You swam!” and Salma realises fear does not have to win.

  3. Punctuate this speech correctly: where is my bag asked Hana i left it here a minute ago

    Show answer
    “Where is my bag?” asked Hana. “I left it here a minute ago.” (Question mark inside the speech marks · capital letters for ‘Where’ and ‘I’ · the second sentence of speech gets its own speech marks.)

  4. Rewrite with a different sentence starter each time: ‘The boy walked into the room. The boy saw a box. The boy opened it.’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: Pushing the door open, the boy stepped into the dusty room. In the far corner, a wooden box waited on the floor. Slowly, he lifted the lid. (-ing starter, prepositional phrase starter, adverb starter.)

  5. The extract you read is in the third person and the past tense: ‘Adam crept along the corridor. The torch flickered.’ A student continues: ‘I am really scared now and I run back.’ What two mistakes have they made?

    Show answer
    They changed the point of view from third person (Adam) to first person (I), and they changed the tense from past (crept, flickered) to present (am, run). Correct: ‘Adam was really frightened now, and he ran back.’

  6. Change this ‘telling’ sentence into ‘showing’: ‘Laila was nervous before the race.’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: Laila retied her shoelaces for the third time, and her stomach twisted like a wet towel.

  7. Which is the better ending, and why? (a) ‘Then I woke up and it was a dream.’ (b) ‘I placed the old key back in Grandad’s drawer. Some secrets, I decided, were worth keeping.’

    Show answer
    (b). It gives a real resolution, shows that the character has changed, and links back to the story. Ending (a) cancels everything that happened and loses Creation and Structure marks.
