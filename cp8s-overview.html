Stage 8 Science: The Tests and How to Prepare

How Cambridge Lower Secondary science tests work — two 45-minute papers, question styles, mark-scheme words and results — and how to use this Stage 8 course to prepare.

In the curriculum
  • This course covers the whole Cambridge Lower Secondary Science Stage 8 curriculum (framework code 0893).
  • Thinking and Working Scientifically: models, planning, carrying out enquiries, analysing and evaluating (8TWSm, 8TWSp, 8TWSc, 8TWSa)
  • Biology: joints and muscles, blood, breathing, diet, respiration, ecosystems (8Bs, 8Bp, 8Be)
  • Chemistry: atoms, purity, concentration, chromatography, word equations, energy changes, reactivity, salts (8Cm, 8Cp, 8Cc)
  • Physics: speed, forces, moments, pressure, diffusion, light and colour, magnetism (8Pf, 8Ps, 8Pe)
  • Earth and space: Earth's magnetic field, resources, climate, galaxies and asteroids (8ESp, 8ESc, 8ESs)
  • Science in Context (8SIC) is taught through the lessons but Cambridge says it is not directly assessed.

Written by Mohamed Abdelkriem for students in British and international schools, and for their parents. Read this page first: it explains the tests you will meet, how they are marked, and how to use the rest of the course.

Which test, and when?

The Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint Science test has the code 0893 (the same code as the curriculum framework) and two papers, 0893/01 and 0893/02. It is set and marked by Cambridge. Very important for Stage 8 students: the Checkpoint test is taken at the end of Stage 9, and since May 2023 it assesses Stage 9 only. (The older tests with code 1113 belonged to the previous curriculum, so do not worry if you see old papers with that code.)

So why study Stage 8 in Checkpoint style? Because Stage 9 builds on Stage 8: you cannot understand Stage 9 respiration, atoms or forces without the Stage 8 ideas. And in Stage 8 your school is likely to give you end-of-year tests in the same style as Checkpoint. Cambridge makes Lower Secondary Progression Tests for Stages 7, 8 and 9. They are marked by your teachers in school, and schools can use them at any time in the year. This course prepares you for those Stage 8 tests and builds the base for Checkpoint at the end of Stage 9.

Questions on each strand can appear on both papers: there is biology in Paper 1 and in Paper 2, and the same for chemistry, physics, and Earth and space. Thinking and Working Scientifically skills appear all through both papers — a question about blood may ask you to name a variable, read a graph or spot an anomalous result.

The front cover of a Checkpoint paper tells you to answer all questions, to write in the space provided, to use a black or dark blue pen (an HB pencil is allowed for diagrams and graphs), not to use correction fluid or an erasable pen, and to show all your working. The number of marks for each part is shown in square brackets, for example [2].

How to use your 45 minutes

50 marks in 45 minutes gives you about 54 seconds per mark (45 × 60 = 2700 seconds; 2700 ÷ 50 = 54). Use the marks as a clock: by about 18 minutes you should have answered roughly 20 marks' worth.

  1. Read the stem (the information at the start of each question). Later parts often depend on it.
  2. Look at the marks. [1] = one fact or one word. [2] = two separate points, or a point and a reason. [3] = three points, or a calculation with formula, working and unit.
  3. Underline the command word: name, state, describe, explain, suggest, calculate, predict, compare.
  4. Answer every part. A blank space can never score; a sensible answer often can.
  5. If you are stuck, mark the question, move on, and come back at the end.
  6. Keep the last 3–5 minutes to check units, the number of ticks, and that each [2] answer has two points.
Command wordWhat it meansExample answer
Name / StateGive a short answer, no reason needed"plasma"
DescribeSay what happens or what the data shows"As the temperature increases, the time decreases."
ExplainSay what happens AND why (use because / so)"The arm bends because the biceps contracts and pulls the forearm up."
SuggestUse your knowledge in a new situation; more than one answer may be right"The thermometer may not have been read at eye level."
CalculateWork out a number; show working and give the unit"speed = 120 ÷ 40 = 3 m/s"
PredictSay what you think will happen, using science"The magnet will pick up more paperclips, because more coils make a stronger field."

Question styles you will meet

Real Cambridge Lower Secondary science papers use the styles below. Every lesson page in this course practises them.

StyleWhat it looks likeExam tip
Label a diagram"Label parts A, B and C of the cell."Use the exact science word; a line must point clearly to the part.

Understanding the mark scheme

Mark schemes have four columns: Question, Answer, Marks, Further information. These signs and words tell the examiner what to accept. Knowing them helps you write answers that get every mark.

Word or signWhat it meansWhat it means for you
/Separates different answers that each earn the same mark.Answer A / answer B: either one is fine — do not write both if you are unsure.
( )Words or units in brackets do not have to be written."(red) blood cells": "blood cells" alone would do, but writing more is safer.
underlined wordThat exact word is needed for the mark.If "evaporation" is underlined, "drying" earns nothing.
AcceptAnother answer the examiner will also give the mark for.Different correct wordings are fine — the science must be right.
Do not acceptA wrong answer, or one that contradicts a correct one.Writing a correct and a wrong answer together loses the mark.
IgnoreExtra words that are not relevant: no mark, but no penalty.Unrelated extras do not lose marks — but they waste time.
NoteExtra information for the examiner.—
ecf (error carried forward)A mistake in an early part is not punished again in a later part.Always finish later parts using your earlier answer, even if you are unsure of it.
ora (or reverse argument)The answer can be said the other way round."Copper is less reactive than zinc" = "zinc is more reactive than copper".

Three more helpful rules: a science word spelled the way it sounds is usually given the mark ("haemoglobbin" is clearly haemoglobin); grammar is not marked, only the science; and marking points are independent — you can get the second mark even if you miss the first. "Show your working" means write the formula and the numbers you used: if the final answer is wrong, the working can still earn marks.

Worked examples: how marks are given

Worked example 1 — a 1-mark naming question Question: Name the part of the blood that carries oxygen. [1]

Answer writtenMarkWhy
red blood cells1Correct.
red cells1"(blood)" would be in brackets in the mark scheme — not needed.
red blood cells and white blood cells0White blood cells do not carry oxygen: the wrong extra contradicts the right answer.
blood0Too vague: the question asks for the part of the blood.

Worked example 2 — a 2-mark explanation Question: Explain why the knee needs two muscles to bend and straighten the leg. [2]

  1. Count the marks: [2] means two separate points.
  2. Point 1: muscles can only pull (contract); they cannot push.
  3. Point 2: so one muscle pulls to bend the leg and the other pulls to straighten it — they are an antagonistic pair.
  4. Full answer: "Muscles can only pull, not push, so one muscle (the hamstring) contracts to bend the knee and the other (the quadriceps) contracts to straighten it."

Common mistake: "One muscle pushes and one pulls." This is wrong science and scores 0.

Worked example 3 — a calculation and error carried forward Question: A cyclist travels 600 m in 120 s. (a) Calculate the speed. Give the unit. [2] (b) How far does she travel in 300 s at the same speed? [1]

  1. (a) speed = distance ÷ time = 600 ÷ 120 = 5 m/s. One mark for the working or the number, one mark for the unit.
  2. (b) distance = speed × time = 5 × 300 = 1500 m.
  3. Suppose a student wrote 600 ÷ 120 = 6 m/s in (a) — the number is wrong. In (b) they write 6 × 300 = 1800 m. They used their own (a) answer correctly, so with ecf they still get the mark in (b). Never leave (b) blank because you doubt (a).

Worked example 4 — or reverse argument (ora) Question: Zinc reacts with dilute acid but copper does not. What does this tell you about the reactivity of zinc and copper? [1]

  • "Zinc is more reactive than copper." — 1 mark.
  • "Copper is less reactive than zinc." — also 1 mark (ora: the same idea said the other way round).
  • "Zinc is reactive." — 0 marks: there is no comparison.

How results are reported

For Checkpoint, Cambridge marks the papers and changes the raw marks into a Checkpoint score from 0 to 50, for the whole subject and for each strand: Thinking and Working Scientifically, Biology, Chemistry, Physics, and Earth and Space. Results for smaller parts (sub-strands) and for each question are given as raw marks. Each score falls in a band:

Thinking and Working Scientifically (TWS)

TWS is the skills strand. It is reported as a strand of its own, and it is mixed into almost every question. At Stage 8 it has four parts. The page Thinking and Working Scientifically teaches all of them in detail; here is the big picture with one example of each.

PartWhat you do at Stage 8Example
Models and representations (8TWSm)Explain what an analogy is and use one; use symbols and formulae."The heart is like a pump." Writing speed = distance ÷ time, or H2O for water.
Purpose and planning (8TWSp)Decide if a hypothesis is testable, predict using science, plan investigations, write risk assessments."More coils will make the electromagnet stronger, because…" Not every investigation can be a fair test.
Carrying out enquiry (8TWSc)Classify and use keys, choose equipment, measure accurately, repeat readings, judge secondary sources for bias, record results.Reading a measuring cylinder at eye level; noticing that an advert from a company may be biased.
Analysis, evaluation and conclusions (8TWSa)Describe trends, find anomalous results, say if predictions were accurate, make conclusions with limitations, suggest improvements, present data."As the coils increase from 10 to 60, the paperclips increase from 3 to 18. The result at 40 coils is anomalous."

Common mistakes
  • Writing one point for a [2] question. Two marks need two separate ideas.
  • Giving a number without a unit, or with the wrong unit (m/s for speed, N for force).
  • Writing two answers "to be safe" — a wrong one cancels the right one.
  • Describing data with no numbers. Quote values from the table or graph.
  • Using everyday words ("the muscle gets small") instead of science words ("the muscle contracts").
  • Leaving a later part blank because an earlier part was hard — ecf can still give you the mark.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Most questions start with a short story: a student doing an investigation, a table of results or a diagram. Read it twice.
  • Almost every question mixes a topic with a TWS skill: variables, predictions, trends, anomalies, improvements.
  • Calculations: write the formula first, then substitute, then the answer with its unit.
  • Graphs: label both axes with units, use more than half the grid, plot crosses exactly, draw a smooth line or curve of best fit.
  • "Explain" questions: one marking point per idea — use "because" and "so".

Practice

Remember
  • Checkpoint Science (0893) is taken at the end of Stage 9; Stage 8 end-of-year tests use the same style.
  • Two papers, 45 minutes and 50 marks each; all strands and TWS can appear on both.
  • About 54 seconds per mark: let the [marks] tell you how much to write.
  • Mark-scheme language: / = alternatives, ( ) = not needed, underlined = exact word, ecf, ora.
  • Show working and units; never write a right and a wrong answer together.
