Key Ideas and Details

Main idea, details, sequence, cause–effect, comparisons and inferences on ACT Reading, with worked examples, trap analysis and a full literary narrative practice passage.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Key Ideas and Details is the largest category on ACT Reading: 44–52% of the scored questions. With 27 scored questions, that is roughly 12 to 14 questions on every test. These questions check whether you understood what the passage says and what it clearly means. They appear in every passage type, including the literary narrative (fiction or memoir) that is always one of the three scored passages.

Sub-typeTypical stem (in our own words)What you do
Main idea / central idea"The passage mainly focuses on:" / "The main idea of paragraph 4 is that:"Summarize the whole passage (or paragraph) in one sentence
Detail"According to the passage, …" / "The narrator states that …"Find the sentence; the answer is a paraphrase of it
Sequence"Which of the following events happens first?"Put events in TIME order, not reading order
Cause–effect"According to the passage, X happened because:"Find the reason the text gives (because, so, since, as a result)
Comparison"Compared with A, B is described as:"Find the description of BOTH things and compare
Inference"It can most reasonably be inferred that …" / "The narrator's statement most strongly suggests …"Take ONE small step beyond the words, never two

The method, step by step

  1. Read the blurb and know who the narrator is. In literary passages, ask: Who is telling the story? Is he a child in the story or an adult looking back? Many Key Ideas questions depend on this.
  2. Track the plot as you read. For a narrative, your 3-word map becomes a short timeline: [1] sent to uncle, [2] sweeps 3 weeks, [3] watches, [4] gets wheel, [5] fails, [6] note, [7] succeeds, [8] pen, [9] adult lesson.
  3. Find the line before you choose. For every detail, cause–effect or comparison question, go to the paragraph and read the sentence before, the sentence and the sentence after.
  4. Predict in your own words. Write a 3–6-word answer on your scrap paper before looking at the choices.
  5. Match meaning, not words. The right answer usually paraphrases the text. A choice that copies the passage's exact words but changes one relationship (cause, order, comparison) is a classic trap.
  6. For inferences, test the step. Ask: "Does the text make this almost certain?" If you need to imagine extra facts, eliminate it.
  7. For main-idea questions, check the ends. The correct choice fits both the beginning and the end of the passage; in memoir, the end often reveals the meaning.

Sequence, cause–effect and comparison: the three relationship questions

Many wrong answers on Key Ideas questions contain true facts from the passage but connect them the wrong way. Learn to check the relationship, not just the facts.

RelationshipSignal words in the passageThe trap to watch for
Sequence (time)then, later, by March, the next day, before, after, years later, nowReading order ≠ time order: memoirs often start in the present and flash back
Cause–effectbecause, so, since, as a result, that is why, which madeReversed cause and effect: the effect is offered as the cause
Comparisonlike, unlike, more than, nothing like, instead of, whileHalf-right: one side of the comparison is correct, the other is invented

Worked examples

Read this short memoir excerpt (shorter than a real ACT passage, so we can focus on the method). Then study each question, the answer, and why each wrong choice is wrong.

[1] We moved to Cairo in January, in the middle of the school year, which my mother said was the worst possible time and my father said was the only possible time. On my first morning at the new school, the teacher placed me at a desk beside a boy named Tarek, who looked at my old-fashioned pencil case and said nothing at all. [2] For a week, Tarek and I shared the desk like two strangers sharing a bus seat. Then, during a mathematics lesson, he dropped his ruler, and I picked it up before he could. He looked surprised. The next day, a small bag of sunflower seeds appeared on my side of the desk. Neither of us mentioned it. [3] By March we were walking home together. I learned later that Tarek had also arrived in the middle of a school year, two years before me, and that nobody had picked up anything for him.

Worked example 1 — main idea

Question: The excerpt mainly focuses on:

  • A) the narrator's difficult relationship with his new teacher.
  • B) a friendship that grows slowly from a small act of kindness.
  • C) the disagreement between the narrator's parents about moving.
  • D) the problems of changing schools in the middle of the year.

Answer: B. The excerpt moves from silence (paragraph 1) to a small kindness and a gift (paragraph 2) to walking home together (paragraph 3). B covers all three paragraphs.

  • A is wrong — Off-topic: the teacher appears only once, to seat the narrator.
  • C is wrong — Too narrow: the parents' disagreement is one sentence of background.
  • D is wrong — Too broad: the excerpt is about one friendship, not the general problems of changing schools.

Worked example 2 — sequence

Question: Which event happened immediately before the bag of sunflower seeds appeared on the narrator's side of the desk?

  • A) The teacher seated the narrator beside Tarek.
  • B) The narrator learned about Tarek's own arrival at the school.
  • C) The narrator picked up Tarek's ruler.
  • D) The two boys began walking home together.

Answer: C. Paragraph 2: the ruler is picked up "during a mathematics lesson", and "the next day" the seeds appear. Look for the time signal: "the next day" links the two events directly.

  • A is wrong — Happened before, but not immediately before: a week passed in between.
  • B is wrong — Happened much later ("I learned later") — the trap of reading order vs time order.
  • D is wrong — Happened afterwards, "by March".

Worked example 3 — comparison

Question: The narrator's parents differed in their views of the January move in that:

  • A) his mother thought it was badly timed, while his father thought there was no other choice.
  • B) his mother wanted to move to Cairo, while his father did not.
  • C) both parents thought it was the best possible time to move.
  • D) his father worried about the new school, while his mother worried about the city.

Answer: A. "My mother said was the worst possible time and my father said was the only possible time." Only possible = there was no other choice. A states both sides correctly.

  • B is wrong — Not stated: the text gives their views of the timing, not of Cairo itself.
  • C is wrong — Reversed for the mother and not true for the father: "only possible" is not "best".
  • D is wrong — Invented: neither worry is mentioned (outside-the-text trap).

Worked example 4 — inference

Question: The last sentence of the excerpt most strongly suggests that Tarek:

  • A) disliked all new students until the narrator arrived.
  • B) had been lonely when he first arrived and valued the narrator's small kindness.
  • C) had told the teacher to seat the narrator beside him.
  • D) had dropped his ruler on purpose to test the narrator.

Answer: B. Tarek also arrived mid-year and "nobody had picked up anything for him." One small step: he knew how it felt to be ignored, which explains his surprise and his gift.

  • A is wrong — Too extreme: "all new students" goes far beyond the text.
  • C is wrong — Two steps away: nothing suggests Tarek spoke to the teacher.
  • D is wrong — Pure speculation: the passage says only that he "dropped his ruler"; inventing a motive is not inference.
Classic traps
  • Right words, wrong relationship. The choice uses the passage's own words but reverses cause and effect or changes the order of events.
  • Reading order vs time order. Memoirs jump between the past and the present; build a timeline before answering sequence questions.
  • Half-right comparisons. Check BOTH halves of a comparison choice against the text.
  • Over-inference. "Most reasonably inferred" means one small step. Extreme words (all, never, completely, always) usually signal two steps too many.
  • Main idea = favourite detail. In fiction, students often choose the most exciting moment. The main idea must include the ending and the meaning.
  • Confusing the narrator with the author, or the young narrator with the adult narrator. "I am a structural engineer now" is the older narrator looking back; his feelings then and now may differ.
Fast strategy for test day
  • For literary narrative: identify the narrator, the time frame, and the turning point while you read.
  • Detail and cause–effect questions: go back to the line every time; never answer from memory.
  • Predict a short answer before reading the choices.
  • Sequence: number the events 1–4 on your scrap paper using the time signals.
  • Inference: choose the most cautious choice that the text strongly supports.
القطعة الأدبية

في الـ literary narrative اسأل نفسك 3 أسئلة وإنت بتقرا: مين اللي بيحكي؟ بيحكي وهو صغير ولا وهو كبير بيفتكر؟ وإيه اللحظة اللي غيرت القصة؟ أغلب أسئلة الـ main idea والـ inference إجابتها في آخر باراجراف.

Practice

Now try a full literary narrative passage (about 750 words) with 9 Key Ideas and Details questions. It is untimed, but aim for about 10 minutes. Build a timeline as you read, and go back to the text for every answer.

Remember
  • Key Ideas and Details = 44–52% of scored ACT Reading questions — the biggest category.
  • Every detail answer is a paraphrase of a sentence you can point to.
  • Check the relationship: time order, cause–effect and comparison traps use true facts connected the wrong way.
  • An inference is one small, safe step beyond the text, never a guess.
  • In literary narrative, know who is telling the story and from what point in time.
