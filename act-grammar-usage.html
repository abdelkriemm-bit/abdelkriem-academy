American Diploma · ACT · English: Conventions

ACT Grammar and Usage

Subject–verb agreement, pronoun case and agreement, who/whom, verb forms, adjectives and adverbs, comparisons, idioms and confused words — with worked items and an ACT-style quiz.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

Usage questions test whether each word has the right form: a verb that agrees with its subject, a pronoun in the right case, the correct past participle, an adverb where an adverb is needed, the right preposition after a word. These errors often sound natural in everyday speech, which is exactly why the test uses them. This page gives you a rule and a quick check for each one.

What this question type tests

Usage belongs to Conventions of Standard English, which is 38–43% of the 40 scored English questions (about 15–17 questions, together with punctuation and sentence structure). The question looks like any other ACT English question: an underlined word or phrase and four versions, usually with NO CHANGE first. When the vertical scan shows that the choices differ in the form of one word (is/are, he/him, went/gone, careful/carefully, larger/largest, of/for), you are looking at a usage question.

The method is always the same: find the word the underlined portion depends on (its subject, its noun, its antecedent, the word before the preposition), then apply the rule.

1. Subject–verb agreement

A singular subject takes a singular verb (is, was, has, goes); a plural subject takes a plural verb (are, were, have, go). The test hides the subject. Cross out everything between the subject and the verb, then check.

Rule✗ Wrong✓ Right
Ignore phrases between subject and verb (of…, in…, with…)The walls of the oven holds the heat.The walls of the oven hold the heat.
each, every, one, either, neither, everyone = singularEach of the blocks were numbered.Each of the blocks was numbered.
A + B joined by and = pluralMy brother and my cousin plays football.My brother and my cousin play football.
along with / as well as / together with do not add to the subjectThe coach, along with the players, are here.The coach, along with the players, is here.
either…or / neither…nor: the verb agrees with the nearer subjectNeither the students nor the teacher are ready.Neither the students nor the teacher is ready.
Inverted order (there is/are, here…, a prepositional phrase first): find the subject after the verbThere is three reasons for this.There are three reasons for this.
The number of = singular; a number of = pluralThe number of visitors have grown.The number of visitors has grown.
A clause or a gerund as subject = singularReading long novels help my vocabulary.Reading long novels helps my vocabulary.
اتفاق الفاعل والفعل

امسح أي كلام بين الفاعل والفعل (زي of the oven أو in the box) وبعدين اسأل: الفاعل مفرد ولا جمع؟ Each وEvery وOne دايمًا مفرد حتى لو بعدها جمع.

2. Pronoun case: I or me? he or him?

Subject formsObject formsPossessive
I, he, she, we, they, whome, him, her, us, them, whommy/mine, his, her/hers, our/ours, their/theirs, whose
  • Cover the other person. "He taught Karim and I" → "He taught I" ✗ → "He taught Karim and me" ✓.
  • After a preposition use the object form. ✓ between you and me ✗ between you and I; ✓ for her and me.
  • Comparisons with than/as: finish the sentence. ✓ She is taller than I (am). ✓ He helped you more than (he helped) me.
  • Reflexive pronouns (myself, himself) only when the subject does the action to itself or for emphasis. ✓ I hurt myself. ✗ Karim and myself fixed it. ✓ Karim and I fixed it.

3. Who or whom?

Who is a subject (like he); whom is an object (like him). Turn the clause into a short sentence and test with he/him:

  • "My grandfather, ___ repaired radios…" → he repaired radios → who.
  • "The doctor ___ we visited…" → we visited him → whom.
  • "To ___ should I send the form?" → send it to him → whom.
  • "The girl ___ I think won the prize…" → ignore "I think": she won → who.

4. Pronoun agreement and clear reference

  • A pronoun must agree in number with the noun it replaces (its antecedent). ✗ The company raised their prices. ✓ The company raised its prices. ✗ The Great Temple faces east, and the engineers made sure that they still did. ✓ …that it still did.
  • The pronoun must clearly point to one noun. ✗ When Ali met Hassan, he was nervous. (Who was nervous?) ✓ Ali was nervous when he met Hassan.
  • Do not switch person in the middle of a passage. ✗ If one studies daily, you will improve. ✓ If you study daily, you will improve.
  • its / it's and their / they're / there are tested here too: its = belonging to it; it's = it is.

5. Verb tense and verb forms

  • Keep the tense consistent with the rest of the paragraph unless the time really changes. ✗ Yesterday we visit the museum and saw the mummies. ✓ Yesterday we visited the museum and saw the mummies.
  • Past perfect (had + past participle) = the earlier of two past actions. ✓ By the time the water arrived, the workers had taken the last block away.
  • Present perfect (has/have + past participle) = from the past until now, often with since or for. ✓ Since 2020 she has written three books.
  • Conditionals: ✓ If I had known, I would have helped. ✗ If I would have known…
  • would have / could have / should have, never would of. ✓ We should have left earlier.

Irregular past participles are a favourite trap: after has/have/had you need the third form, not the simple past.

6. Adjectives vs adverbs

  • Adjectives describe nouns: a careful worker, a quick reply.
  • Adverbs describe verbs, adjectives and other adverbs, and often end in -ly: she worked carefully; an extremely quick reply.
  • ✗ The blocks were cut very careful. ✓ The blocks were cut very carefully.
  • ✗ The new design is incredible efficient. ✓ The new design is incredibly efficient. ("incredibly" describes the adjective "efficient")
  • After linking verbs (be, seem, look, feel, taste, sound, become) use an adjective: ✓ The soup tastes good. ✓ I feel bad. ✗ I feel badly about it (when you mean your feeling).
  • good = adjective; well = adverb (and the adjective for health): ✓ She sings well. ✓ He did a good job.

7. Comparatives vs superlatives

Comparing…Use✓ Example
two things-er / moreOf the two temples, the Great Temple is larger.
three or more-est / mostIt is the largest of the three pyramids.
never both—✗ more larger, most largest, more better
countable nounsfewerfewer cars, fewer mistakes
uncountable nounslessless traffic, less water
between two / among three or morebetween / amongbetween the two cities; among the five students

Also check that a comparison compares like with like: ✗ Cairo's population is larger than Alexandria. ✓ Cairo's population is larger than Alexandria's (or than that of Alexandria).

8. Idioms and prepositions

Some words must be followed by one particular preposition. There is no rule to work out — you learn them. When the vertical scan shows only the preposition changing, say each choice in your head and pick the standard one.

Lie ولا Lay؟

lie يعني "يتمدد/يرتاح" ومالهاش مفعول: He would lie down. أما lay يعني "يحط حاجة" ولازم بعدها مفعول: Lay the book on the table. خلي بالك: lay كمان هي الماضي بتاع lie، فبعد would أو to استخدم lie.

Worked examples

Each example is part of an ACT-style essay. The underlined portion is shown in bold.

Worked example 1. "The collection of old maps in the university library attract researchers from all over the world."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) attracts
  • C) are attracting
  • D) have attracted

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. Cross out "of old maps in the university library": the subject is "collection" (singular) → "attracts".
  • A, C and D are all plural forms; they agree with "maps" or "library", the nearest nouns, which is the trap.
  • Trap name: the nearest-noun trap.

Worked example 2. "By the time the guests arrived, my mother had already went to the airport to collect my aunt."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) has already went
  • C) had already gone
  • D) already gone

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. The going happened before the arrival, so we need the past perfect: had + past participle. The participle of go is gone.
  • A uses the simple past "went" after "had".
  • B has the same participle error and the wrong tense (present perfect in a past story).
  • D has no helping verb, so the sentence has no complete verb.

Worked example 3. "The committee chose two students, Mona and I, to represent the school."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) me
  • C) myself
  • D) mine

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. "Mona and ___" renames "two students", the object of "chose". Test: the committee chose me → object form.
  • A is the subject form.
  • C is reflexive, but the subject (the committee) is not the same as the object.
  • D is possessive.

Worked example 4. "Many young engineers are capable to design buildings that use far less energy."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) capable in designing
  • C) capable of designing
  • D) capable for design

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. The idiom is "capable of + -ing".
  • A uses "to", B uses "in", and D uses "for" — all non-standard after "capable".
  • Trap name: idiom error; it only sounds wrong if you have learned the pair, so learn the table above.

Worked example 5. "Of all the dishes at the festival, the koshari from the small stall near the gate was the more popular."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) most popular
  • C) popularest
  • D) most popularest

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. "Of all the dishes" compares many items, so the superlative "most popular" is needed.
  • A is a comparative, used only for two items.
  • C and D are not correct English forms; long adjectives use most, and you never combine most with -est.
Classic traps
  • Nearest-noun trap: the verb is made to agree with a noun in a phrase between the subject and the verb.
  • Sounds-polite trap: "between you and I", "Karim and myself" — they sound formal but are wrong.
  • Simple past after had: had went, had took, has began.
  • Adjective where an adverb is needed: cut very careful, incredible fast.
  • Superlative for two: "the best of the two" → the better of the two.
  • Wrong preposition: different with, capable to, consist from.
  • Twin words: affect/effect, than/then, lie/lay — check the part of speech.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Verb choices? Cross out the phrase in the middle and find the real subject.
  • Pronoun choices? Cover the other person, or replace with he/him.
  • After had/has/have? Use the third form (gone, written, taken).
  • Describing a verb or an adjective? Adverb. Describing a noun or after a linking verb? Adjective.
  • Two things → -er/more; three or more → -est/most.
  • Only the preposition changes? Say each one; choose the standard idiom.
Who ولا Whom؟

حوّل الجملة لجملة قصيرة وجرّب he و him. لو he مناسبة استخدم who، ولو him مناسبة استخدم whom. مثال: My grandfather, ___ repaired radios → he repaired → who.

Practice

Two ACT-style essays with 6 questions each. The numbers in brackets match the question numbers. For more practice on agreement, pronouns and verb forms, see the grammar lessons in the SAT course.

Remember
  • Find the true subject: cross out the middle phrase; each/every/one = singular.
  • Subject form: I, he, who. Object form (after verbs and prepositions): me, him, whom. Cover the other person to test.
  • After has/have/had use the past participle: gone, written, taken, begun.
  • Adverbs describe verbs and adjectives; adjectives describe nouns and follow linking verbs.
  • Two → -er/more, three+ → -est/most; learn the idioms (capable of, different from) and the twins (affect/effect, than/then, lie/lay).
