American Diploma · ACT · English: Knowledge of Language

Word Choice and Tone

Precision, formal vs casual register, consistent tone, idioms and vague vs specific wording — five checks, worked items and a 10-question ACT-style essay.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Word-choice and tone questions belong to Knowledge of Language, the ACT English reporting category that is 18–23% of the English score. Knowledge of Language is about using words precisely and appropriately: the exact word for the meaning, a style that fits the essay, and wording that stays consistent from start to finish. (Concision — cutting unnecessary words — is part of the same category; see the Concision page.)

These questions look like any other ACT English item: an underlined word or phrase and four choices, usually with NO CHANGE first. Sometimes there is no stem; sometimes you see stems such as "Which choice best maintains the style and tone of the essay?", "Which choice is the most precise?", or the NOT form, "Which of the following alternatives would NOT be acceptable?"

The difficulty: wrong choices are often real English words that almost fit. You must judge meaning, register and idiom, not just grammar. This page gives you five checks.

اقرا الكلمة في جملتها

في أسئلة word choice الاختيارات كلها ممكن تكون كلمات صحيحة لوحدها، بس واحدة بس بتدّي المعنى الدقيق وبتناسب أسلوب المقال. اسأل: الكلمة دي معناها مظبوط هنا؟ رسمية زي باقي المقال؟ الحرف اللي بعدها (of / on / to) صح؟

Check 1: Precision — the exact meaning

The right word means exactly what the writer intends. Many wrong choices are words with a similar look or a related meaning.

PairMeaningExample
affect / effectaffect = verb (to influence); effect = noun (a result)Rain affects the harvest. Rain has an effect on the harvest.
accept / exceptaccept = receive, agree; except = not includingShe accepted every invitation except one.
imply / inferimply = suggest (speaker); infer = conclude (listener)Her smile implied approval; I inferred that she agreed.
eminent / imminenteminent = famous; imminent = about to happenAn eminent scientist warned that a storm was imminent.
distinctive / distinguisheddistinctive = easy to recognise; distinguished = respecteda distinctive smell / a distinguished professor
respectively / respectfullyrespectively = in the order given; respectfully = with respectAli and Sara came first and second, respectively.
continual / continuouscontinual = repeated with breaks; continuous = without stoppingcontinual interruptions / a continuous line
economic / economicaleconomic = about the economy; economical = saves moneyeconomic growth / an economical car
principal / principleprincipal = main, head of school; principle = a rule or beliefThe principal explained the principle.
adapt / adoptadapt = change to fit; adopt = take as your ownThe school adopted a new timetable and students adapted to it.

Precision is also about logic in context. A word may be correct alone but wrong in the sentence: "The scientists guessed the results after three years of careful measurement" — "guessed" contradicts "careful measurement"; "confirmed" is precise.

Check 2: Register — formal vs casual

Most ACT essays are written in a clear, neutral-to-formal style, the style of a good magazine or textbook. Slang and chatty speech are wrong in that style, even when the meaning is right.

pretty cool, awesome, super neatremarkable, impressive, striking

But formal does not mean pompous. The ACT also rejects choices that are too grand or too dramatic for a plain essay: "The yeast greedily devours the sugars" in a science essay, "The committee's illustrious and magnificent decision" in a report. The best choice matches the essay — not always the fanciest word.

رسمي بس مش متكلّف

المقال على الـ ACT غالباً أسلوبه رسمي بسيط. شيل أي كلام عامي زي stuff / kind of / awesome / dude. بس كمان متختارش كلمة تقيلة ومبالغ فيها لو المقال بسيط. الصح هو اللي شبه باقي المقال.

Check 3: Consistent tone with the essay

Tone is the writer's attitude: serious, enthusiastic, warm, neutral, humorous. The right choice keeps the tone the essay already has. A personal narrative can be warmer and use "I" and simple everyday words; an informational essay is neutral; an argumentative essay is confident but fair. Before you answer, read two or three sentences around the underline and ask, "How does this writer sound?"

  • ✗ (in a respectful article about a scientist) Her research was a total game-changer.
  • ✓ Her research transformed the field.
  • ✗ (in a warm personal story) My grandmother's kitchen exhibited a pervasive aroma of cardamom.
  • ✓ My grandmother's kitchen always smelled of cardamom.

Check 4: Idioms — the right small word

An idiom is a fixed combination, often a verb or adjective + preposition. There is no logic rule; you must know the pair. Here are combinations that appear often in test-style sentences:

Method: read the phrase with each preposition in your head. If one sounds strange, it probably is. If all sound possible, think about meaning: "agree with a person, agree to a plan, agree on a decision."

Check 5: Vague vs specific

When the question asks for precision or detail, a vague word loses. Vague: a certain temperature, some people, a lot, things, stuff, good, nice, very big. Specific: 25 to 30 degrees Celsius, the city's three hospitals, 40 per cent, a 12-metre wall. Choose the specific option when the sentence is meant to inform, unless it adds information that is off-topic (that is a Relevance problem).

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — precision

Passage: After studying the soil samples for two years, the team __guessed__ that the old lake had dried up about 5,000 years ago.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) hoped
  • C) concluded
  • D) imagined

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "Guessed" suggests no evidence — it contradicts two years of study.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: "Hoped" is about wishes, not evidence.
  • C) ✓ Correct: "Concluded" means reached a judgement from evidence — exactly what the study did.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: "Imagined" means invented in the mind; again no evidence.

Worked example 2 — register

Passage: The city council's report describes the new park in detail. It will include a running track, a library kiosk, and __a ton of__ shaded seating areas.

Which choice best maintains the style and tone of the essay?

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) loads of
  • C) several
  • D) a whole bunch of

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Casual expression in a formal report.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Casual.
  • C) ✓ Correct: Neutral and formal, matching "report" and "in detail."
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Casual and wordy.

Worked example 3 — idiom

Passage: Many students in my class are interested __about__ robotics, so our teacher started an after-school club.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) in
  • C) for
  • D) with

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "Interested about" is not standard English.
  • B) ✓ Correct: The idiom is "interested in."
  • C) ✗ Wrong: "Interested for" is not standard.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: "Interested with" is not standard.

Worked example 4 — tone (too dramatic)

Passage: The museum's new guide explains how the mummies were prepared. Each body was dried with natron, a natural salt, and then __the ghastly, horrifying corpse was__ wrapped in linen.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) the gruesome dead thing was
  • C) it was
  • D) the creepy body was totally

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Overly dramatic language that clashes with a calm, informational guide.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Dramatic and vague ("thing").
  • C) ✓ Correct: Neutral, clear and concise — "it" clearly means the body.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Casual and dramatic ("creepy," "totally").
Classic traps
  • The look-alike: distinguished for distinctive, imminent for eminent, affect for effect.
  • The fancy trap: the longest, most impressive word is not automatically right — it must match the meaning and the tone.
  • The drama trap: emotional or exaggerated words in a neutral essay.
  • The slang trap: a choice with the right meaning but casual words (a bunch of, kind of, stuff).
  • The preposition trap: capable to, interested about, different than, prefer … than.
  • The vague trap: "a certain," "some," "things" when the sentence wants information.
  • The context trap: a word that is fine alone but contradicts nearby details (guessed after years of study).
Fast strategy for test day
  • Decide the essay's tone in the first paragraph (formal? personal? neutral?) and keep it in mind.
  • Cross out any choice with slang first — it is almost never right in an ACT essay.
  • For similar-looking words, say each meaning in a few words and test it in the sentence.
  • For idioms, read the whole phrase aloud in your head; trust standard pairs you have learned.
  • If two choices remain, choose the one that is more precise and fits the tone — not the one that sounds more impressive.
  • On a NOT question, three choices are acceptable; look for the one with a wrong meaning, wrong idiom or wrong register.

Practice

Read the essay and answer the 10 questions. The essay is informational, so its tone is clear and fairly formal.

Remember
  • Precise = the exact meaning in this context; watch look-alike words.
  • Register: no slang in a formal essay — but no over-dramatic words either.
  • Keep the tone the essay already has.
  • Learn the common idioms: capable of, depend on, interested in, different from, prefer A to B.
  • Specific beats vague when the sentence is meant to inform.
