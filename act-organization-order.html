American Diploma · ACT · English: Production of Writing

Organisation: Order and Transitions

Transition words by logic family, sentence placement, paragraph order, and introduction, conclusion and transition sentences — with a method, worked items and a 10-question ACT-style essay.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Organisation questions belong to Production of Writing, the reporting category that is 38–43% of the ACT English score. Production of Writing covers topic development (see the Relevance page) and organisation, unity and cohesion — putting ideas in a logical order and linking them so the reader can follow.

On the test, organisation appears in five forms:

FormTypical stem (your own words)
Transition wordsAn underlined "However," "Therefore," "For example," with NO CHANGE and three other transitions.
Sentence placement"For the sake of logic and cohesion, Sentence 3 should be placed: where it is now / before Sentence 1 / after Sentence 1 / after Sentence 4."
Adding a sentence in the right place"The writer wants to add the following sentence … The sentence would most logically be placed after: …"
Paragraph order"For the sake of logic and cohesion, Paragraph 3 should be placed: …" (asked at the end of the essay)
Introduction, conclusion and transition sentences"Which choice most effectively introduces the essay / concludes the paragraph / provides the best transition to the next paragraph?"

When an essay has a sentence-placement or paragraph-order question, the sentences or paragraphs are numbered in the passage, like (1), (2), (3) or [Paragraph 1]. That number is your signal to think about order.

الكلمات الصغيرة بتكشف الترتيب

في أسئلة ترتيب الجمل دوّر على الكلمات اللي بترجع لحاجة قبلها: this / that / these / he / she / they / such / also / the + اسم اتذكر قبل كده. الجملة اللي فيها الكلمات دي لازم تيجي بعد الحاجة اللي بتشاور عليها.

Transition words: the logic families

A transition word tells the reader how two ideas are related. The ACT gives you four transitions that each mean something different, and only one fits the logic. Learn the families:

Logic familyUse it when the second idea…Words
Additionadds another point of the same kindalso, in addition, moreover, furthermore, besides
Contrastgoes against or differs from the firsthowever, but, yet, in contrast, on the other hand, whereas
Concession / unexpected resulthappens despite the first ideaeven so, nevertheless, still, nonetheless, despite this, although
Cause → effectis the result of the firsttherefore, as a result, consequently, thus, so, for this reason
Exampleis an example of the firstfor example, for instance, such as, to illustrate
Restatement / clarificationsays the first idea again more clearlyin other words, that is, in short
Emphasismakes the first idea strongerin fact, indeed, above all, especially
Similarityis like the firstsimilarly, likewise, in the same way
Time / sequencecomes later in time or in orderthen, next, later, eventually, finally, meanwhile, afterwards
Conclusionsums upin the end, ultimately, overall

Method: cover the transition. Read the sentence before and the sentence with the blank. Say in your own words how they connect ("the second is the result," "the second is surprising after the first"). Then pick the family, then the word.

  • ✓ The ferry was full; as a result, we waited for the next one. (effect)
  • ✓ The ferry was full; even so, the captain let three more passengers on. (unexpected)
  • ✗ The ferry was full; for example, we waited for the next one. (waiting is not an example of being full)
  • ✗ The museum is free on Fridays; however, many students visit then. (no contrast — use "as a result")

Two ACT tricks: (1) If two choices are in the same family ("therefore" and "thus"), neither can be the only right answer, so both are usually wrong. (2) Sometimes no transition is needed; if a choice removes it, check whether the logic is clear without one.

عائلات الـ transitions

قبل ما تبص على الاختيارات، قول بالعربي العلاقة بين الجملتين: عكس؟ نتيجة؟ مثال؟ إضافة؟ ترتيب زمني؟ وبعدين اختار الكلمة من العيلة الصح. لو لقيت كلمتين بنفس المعنى في الاختيارات، غالباً الاتنين غلط.

Sentence placement: a step-by-step method

  1. Read the sentence to be placed and circle its "pointing" words: this, that, these, such, also, then, the + noun already introduced, he/she/they, a time word (later, first).
  2. Find what each pointing word points to. "Railways changed that" points to a situation; "Some had two minute hands" points to clocks just mentioned.
  3. Check the neighbours. The sentence must follow what it refers to and must fit before what comes next.
  4. Test your choice by reading the three sentences in a row. If the flow is smooth and every pronoun has a clear partner, you are right.
  5. Remember "where it is now" is often correct. Check it first; do not move a sentence unless there is a problem.
Clue in the sentenceWhat it tells you
this / these / such + nounthe idea must appear just before
he, she, they, itthe person or thing must be named before
also, another, tooa first one must come before
for examplethe general claim comes before
first / then / finally / later / by 1900follow the time order
a new name with a full description ("a farmer named Ali")Ali is being introduced — so it is early

Paragraph order

Paragraph-order questions work like sentence placement on a larger scale. Look at the first sentence of the paragraph in question and the last sentence of the one before. Look for:

  • time order (1840s before 1883);
  • problem before solution, cause before effect;
  • an idea introduced before it is discussed ("this method" needs the method first);
  • general before specific, and the conclusion last.

Introductions, conclusions and transition sentences

JobWhat the right choice doesWrong choices usually…
Introduce the essay or paragraphNames the specific topic the sentences after it developare too broad ("Clocks come in many shapes"), off-topic, or only about one small detail
Conclude the paragraphSums up or completes the paragraph's pointstart a new topic or repeat a detail
Conclude the essayReturns to the main idea or opening image; may connect to todayadd a new fact, or are general statements about the world
Transition between paragraphsMentions the idea before AND points to the idea afteronly look back or only look forward

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — transition word

Passage: The new metro line cut the journey from Heliopolis to downtown from an hour to twenty minutes. __However,__ many families who had driven to work started taking the train.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) As a result,
  • C) For example,
  • D) In other words,

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: No contrast: a faster journey and more passengers go together.
  • B) ✓ Correct: Faster trips caused families to switch — cause → effect.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Families switching is not an example of a shorter journey.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: The second sentence does not restate the first.

Worked example 2 — sentence placement

Passage: (1) My uncle opened his first shop in Mansoura in 1990. (2) Within ten years, he had added a printing service and a second branch. (3) It sold only notebooks and pens. (4) Today he owns six shops across the Delta.

For the sake of logic and cohesion, Sentence 3 should be placed:

  • A) where it is now.
  • B) before Sentence 1.
  • C) after Sentence 1.
  • D) after Sentence 4.

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "It" would seem to refer to the second branch, and the time order would jump back from "within ten years" to the beginning.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: "It" would have nothing to refer to — the shop has not been mentioned yet.
  • C) ✓ Correct: "It" = his first shop. The order becomes: the first shop (1990) → what it sold → ten years later → today. Pointing word and time order both work.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: "It" would refer to the six shops (plural), and the essay would end going back in time.

Worked example 3 — transition sentence between paragraphs

Passage: Paragraph A explains how sand dunes move a few metres each year in the Western Desert. Paragraph B begins: "__________ Engineers now plant rows of hardy shrubs to hold the sand in place."

Which choice provides the best transition into Paragraph B?

  • A) Sand is made of tiny pieces of rock.
  • B) Because moving dunes can bury roads and farms, people have looked for ways to stop them.
  • C) The Western Desert is very large.
  • D) Some dunes are taller than buildings.

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Off-topic fact — no link to stopping dunes.
  • B) ✓ Correct: Looks back (moving dunes) and forward (ways to stop them) — exactly what a transition sentence must do.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Too general; no link to the shrubs.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: A detail about dunes that does not lead to the solution.
Classic traps
  • Same-family twins: two choices mean the same (therefore / thus) — usually both wrong.
  • The automatic "However": students pick a contrast word without checking for a real contrast.
  • Moving a sentence that is fine: "where it is now" is often correct — check it first.
  • Orphan pronoun: a placement that leaves "it," "this" or "they" with nothing to point to.
  • Too-broad introduction: "Clocks come in many shapes and sizes" is about clocks, not the essay's topic.
  • One-way transition: a sentence that only repeats the old paragraph or only starts the new one.
  • New-topic conclusion: a final sentence that opens a new subject instead of closing the essay.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Transition words: cover the choices, name the relationship, then choose the family.
  • Sentence placement: circle the pointing words (this, it, also, then) and find their partners.
  • When you see numbered sentences, keep their questions in mind as you read — the problem is often obvious on first reading.
  • Leave paragraph-order questions until you have read the whole essay.
  • For introductions and conclusions, first say the essay's main idea in one sentence; then find the choice that matches it.

Practice

Read the essay and answer the 10 questions. Sentences in Paragraphs 2 and 3 are numbered for the placement questions. Question 10 asks about the essay as a whole.

Remember
  • Name the logic first (contrast, cause, example, addition, time), then pick the transition word.
  • Pointing words (this, it, they, also, such) must come after the thing they point to.
  • Order clues: time, problem before solution, general before specific, idea introduced before discussed.
  • Introductions name the specific topic; conclusions close it; transitions look back and forward.
  • "Where it is now" and NO CHANGE are real answers — check them first.
