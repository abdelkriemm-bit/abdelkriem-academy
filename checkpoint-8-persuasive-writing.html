Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Persuasive Writing — Writing Pack

How to persuade a reader to act in Paper 1: formats, DAFOREST techniques, PEEL paragraphs, counter-arguments, calls to action and a strong 350+ word sample speech.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Persuasive writing tries to convince the reader to think something, believe something or — most importantly — do something. Opinion writing wants the reader to understand and agree with your view. Persuasive writing pushes further and wants the reader to act: vote, sign, join, buy, change, stop. In Paper 1 it can come as a speech, a letter, an article, an email or a leaflet, so this pack shows you one structure (introduction → PEEL paragraphs → counter-argument → call to action) that works for all of them, plus the persuasive toolkit DAFOREST.

1. What is persuasive writing?

QuestionAnswer
PurposeTo persuade: change the reader's mind and make them take action.
AudienceNamed in the task: students in assembly, parents, the head teacher, the local council, newspaper readers. Every choice you make (tone, examples, call to action) depends on them.
ToneConfident, passionate and urgent — but still polite and controlled. Formal for officials, semi-formal for students.
VoiceFirst person (I, we) and direct address (you). Inclusive 'we' and 'our' make the reader feel part of the cause.
Where it appearsPaper 1 Non-fiction, Section B Writing. Task words: persuade, convince, encourage, urge, campaign, appeal.
Exam factWhat it means for you
PaperPaper 1 Non-fiction (Stage 8 Progression Test and Checkpoint 0861 have the same format).
Paper length1 hour 10 minutes for the whole paper: Section A Reading (25 marks) + Section B Writing (25 marks).
Writing timeAbout 35 minutes is suggested for Section B (some 2023 papers said 30 minutes).
Number of tasksOne extended writing task. There is a planning space. Planning is not marked, but it saves marks.
This genrePersuasive writing (letter, speech, article, leaflet) is a framework text type that is likely to appear. Watch the verb in the task: 'persuade', 'convince' or 'encourage' means this genre.

Formats: the task decides the layout

FormatLayout and openingTypical ending
SpeechGreet the audience (Good morning, Mr Ahmed, teachers and fellow students). Strong spoken hook.Call to action + Thank you for listening.
LetterDear Mr/Mrs… (Yours sincerely) or Dear Sir or Madam (Yours faithfully). Say why you are writing in paragraph 1.Call to action + I look forward to hearing from you.
ArticleHeadline, optional subheadings. Indirect hook, then your claim.A punchy call to action addressed to all readers.

CategoryMarksBandsWhat the top band needs in THIS genre

Marking rules you must know
  • The examiner uses 'best fit': each category is judged on its own, starting from the lowest band.
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. Between 21 and 60 words can score no more than 7 marks in total — so always finish a full piece.
  • Writing the wrong text type is expensive: in recent mark schemes Creation is capped at 1 and Structure is capped (at 3, or 2 if the content is also wrong).
  • Your plan is not marked, but students who plan for 4–5 minutes almost always score higher in Structure.

FeatureWhat it doesExample

The persuasive toolkit: DAFOREST and more

ToolMeaningExample
D — Direct addressSpeak to the reader as 'you'You have the power to change this.
A — AlliterationSame first sound for a memorable phrasefresh food, fresh air and fresh ideas
F — FactsStatements that can be provedOur city regularly reaches 40 °C in summer.
O — OpinionsYour view or an expert's, stated confidentlyIt is undeniable that students need shade.
R — Rhetorical questionsQuestions that make the reader think — no answer expectedDo we really want to look out at rows of hot metal?
E — Emotive languageWords that create strong feelingsa dusty, abandoned wasteland
S — StatisticsNumbers that add authorityThree out of four students say they have nowhere shady to sit.
T — Triplets (rule of three)Ideas in threes for rhythmhealthier, calmer and happier
AnecdoteA short real-life storyLast June, a Year 7 student fainted in the heat at break time.
Inclusive languagewe, us, our — builds unityTogether, we can build something special.
HyperboleDeliberate exaggeration for effectIt is the most important vote of our school lives.
ConditionalsShow consequencesIf we choose concrete now, we will regret it for years.
ImageryHelps the reader picture your ideaPicture lemon trees swaying above a bench.
Counter-argument + rebuttalAdmit the other side, then defeat itWhile parking matters, the street already has space.
Use tools naturally

Examiners reward techniques that are used for a purpose, not a list of tricks squeezed into every line. Five or six tools used well (one or two in each paragraph) are far better than fifteen that make the writing sound forced.

3. How to structure persuasive writing

ParagraphWhat to writeExample sentence
Title / greetingHeadline for an article. Greeting for a speech or letterGood morning, teachers and fellow students.
IntroductionHook → background (what is happening and why it matters) → claim / thesis statementI am here to ask you to vote for the garden.
Body 1 — PEELYour first strong pointFirst, a garden would make our school a better place to learn.
Body 2 — PEELYour second pointSecondly, we need shade more than we need tarmac.
Body 3 — PEELYour strongest, most emotional pointMost importantly, a garden would teach us responsibility.
Counter-argument + rebuttalState the other side fairly, then show why your side is still strongerSome of you will say… However, …
Conclusion + call to actionSum up the points, restate the claim in new words, tell the reader exactly what to doSo on Thursday, tick 'Garden'. Thank you for listening.
PEEL: the persuasive paragraph
  • P — Point: a topic sentence that gives one clear argument. (First, a garden would make our school a better place to learn.)
  • E — Evidence: a fact, statistic, example, expert view or anecdote that supports it. (Research shows that students who spend time among plants feel calmer and concentrate better.)
  • E — Explain: show how the evidence proves your point and why it matters to THIS audience. Add a persuasive tool here: a rhetorical question, emotive words, a triplet. (Think what that would mean here: Science lessons where we grow the beans we study…)
  • L — Link: a final sentence that links back to your main claim or leads into the next paragraph. (A car park offers none of this.)

Build up: put your arguments in an order that grows in strength, or start strong and finish with the most emotional point. Use 'ladder' connectives: First… Secondly… What is more… Most importantly… Above all…

How to start: five hooks

  1. Picture this: Close your eyes for a moment and picture the empty patch of land behind our sports hall.
  2. Shocking fact: Every single day, our school canteen throws away enough food to feed an entire class.
  3. Rhetorical question: When was the last time you walked to school without breathing in a cloud of exhaust fumes?
  4. Anecdote: Last June, a Year 7 student fainted from the heat while queueing for lunch. She was not the first.
  5. Bold statement: Next Thursday, we will make the most important decision our school has faced in years.

How to end: five calls to action

  1. Repetition: Vote green. Vote for the garden. Vote for a school we can all be proud of.
  2. Specific request: Please sign the petition at the library desk before Friday, and ask your parents to sign it too.
  3. Choice: The choice before us could not be clearer: act now, or accept the consequences later.
  4. Future image: Imagine walking through these gates in five years' time and seeing what we built together.
  5. Formal letter: I urge you to reconsider this decision, and I look forward to hearing from you.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Introduction and claim

  • It has become increasingly clear that we can no longer ignore [issue].
  • The truth is that [issue] grows more urgent with every passing day.
  • I hold the firm conviction that [action] is essential.
  • If we are serious about [goal], the logical choice is to [action].

Point and evidence

  • One compelling reason to support this is that…
  • Research has shown that…
  • Just look at [example], which proves that…
  • Ask any [student/parent/teacher] and they will tell you that…

Explain (with a persuasive tool)

  • This clearly shows that…, so it is impossible to ignore.
  • Think about what this would mean for…
  • Can we, in good conscience, allow…?
  • Surely no reasonable person could deny that…

Counter-argument and rebuttal

  • Some people claim that… However, …
  • Of course, it is true that… but…
  • Critics may argue that…, yet the evidence shows…

Conclusion and call to action

  • The choice before us could not be clearer: …
  • Let us act with determination and…
  • Do not wait for someone else to act. …
  • Join us. Sign today. Make the difference.

5. Strong sample answer

Persuasive speech for a school assembly485 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school has an unused area of land behind the sports hall. Students and staff will vote on whether it should become a car park or a school garden. Write a speech to be given in assembly persuading students and teachers to vote for the garden. In your speech you should: • explain how a garden would benefit students • respond to people who want a car park • encourage your audience to vote.

Good morning, Mrs Hassan, teachers and fellow students.

Close your eyes for a moment and picture the empty patch of land behind our sports hall. What do you see? Dust. Broken chairs. A rusty football goal that nobody has used for years. Next Thursday, we will all vote on its future: it can become a car park, or it can become a school garden. I am standing here today to ask you to vote for the garden, because it is the only choice that gives something back to every single one of us.

First, a garden would make our school a better place to learn. Research from around the world shows that students who spend time among plants feel calmer and concentrate better in lessons. Think about what that would mean here: Science lessons where we grow the beans we study, Art lessons where we sketch real flowers instead of pictures on a screen, and quiet break times far from the noise of the playground. A car park offers none of this. A garden would bring every subject to life.

Secondly, we need shade far more than we need tarmac. Every summer, temperatures in our city climb above forty degrees, and that corner of the school turns into a frying pan. Last June, a Year 7 student fainted from the heat while queueing for lunch. Tarmac would make the area even hotter; it would soak up the sun all morning and give the heat back all afternoon. A few lemon trees and some climbing plants could create a shady space where students can actually sit and eat. Do we really want to look out of our classroom windows at rows of hot metal when we could be looking at green leaves?

Most importantly, a garden would teach us responsibility. Each class could look after its own vegetable bed, from planting the seeds to harvesting the crop. Imagine Year 8 selling their own tomatoes and mint at the school fair and giving every pound to charity. That is the kind of memory that stays with a student for life — and nobody has ever treasured the memory of a parking space.

Some of you will say that our teachers need somewhere to park, and that is true. Nobody wants them to spend twenty minutes searching for a space every morning. However, the road beside the main gate already has room for more cars, and the school could easily arrange for staff to use it. Parking problems can be solved in other ways. A lost chance to build a garden cannot.

So, when you pick up your voting slip next Thursday, remember what is at stake. A car park will give us space for cars. A garden will give us fresh air, fresh food and fresh ideas. Vote green. Vote for the garden. Vote for a school that we can all be proud of.

Thank you for listening.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation 5/5 — the stance is clear from the claim at the end of paragraph 2 and never weakens. All three bullets are covered and developed: benefits (three PEEL paragraphs), people who want a car park (counter-argument and rebuttal), encouraging the vote (conclusion). The speech speaks directly to its real audience of students and teachers.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — emotive, precise and memorable words for a spoken audience: rusty, frying pan, treasured, at stake. Alliteration and a triplet in 'fresh air, fresh food and fresh ideas'.
  • Structure 7/7 — speech format: greeting, spoken hook ('Close your eyes…'), and 'Thank you for listening'. Each body paragraph is a full PEEL paragraph, e.g. paragraph 3: Point 'a garden would make our school a better place to learn' → Evidence 'Research… calmer and concentrate better' → Explain with a list of examples for THIS school → Link 'A car park offers none of this. A garden would bring every subject to life.'
  • Structure — arguments build in strength with ladder connectives (First, Secondly, Most importantly). The counter-argument and rebuttal come just before the conclusion. The call to action echoes the 'car park or garden' choice from the opening, giving a strong circular shape.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — deliberate minor sentences for effect (Dust. Broken chairs.). Rhetorical questions with correct question marks. A colon introducing an explanation (…vote on its future: it can become a car park, or…) and a colon introducing a list (…what that would mean here: Science lessons…, Art lessons…). A semicolon joining two related independent clauses (Tarmac would make the area even hotter; it would soak up the sun…). A single dash for emphasis in paragraph 5. Triple repetition in the call to action (Vote green. Vote for… Vote for…). Modal verbs (would, could, can) and a conditional sense of consequence.
  • Persuasive toolkit used naturally — Direct address (you), Facts (forty degrees), Opinion (the only choice), Rhetorical questions, Emotive language, Triplets, plus an anecdote (the Year 7 student), imagery (lemon trees), inclusive language (we, our) and repetition.
  • Spelling 3/3 — ambitious words spelt correctly: concentrate, temperatures, harvesting, responsibility, treasured, arrange.

Timing plan for Section B (about 35 minutes)

MinutesWhat to do

4–7Plan three PEEL points (weakest → strongest), one piece of evidence each, one counter-argument with a rebuttal, and your call to action. Choose 5–6 DAFOREST tools and note where they go.

6. Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it costs marksFix
No call to action — the piece just stopsThe purpose (to make the reader act) is not achievedEnd by telling the reader exactly what to do, when and how
Shouting: CAPITAL LETTERS, '!!!' and insultsUnsuitable tone loses Creation and Vocabulary marksStrong words, calm control, one exclamation mark at most
Points without evidenceArguments not developedPEEL: every point needs evidence and an explanation
Forgetting the audience named in the taskWeak writer–reader relationshipUse their name/role and examples that matter to them
Ignoring the other sideThe argument looks weakOne counter-argument + a firm rebuttal
Forcing a technique into every sentenceSounds unnaturalOne or two tools per paragraph, chosen for effect
Wrong format (no greeting in a speech, no 'Dear' in a letter)Wrong text type can cap Creation and StructureCheck the format features before you start writing
Rhetorical questions with no question markGrammar & punctuation errorsRead each question aloud and check the ending
Checklist before you finish
  • The format features are correct (greeting and 'Thank you for listening' / Dear… Yours… / headline).
  • My stance is clear from the first paragraph and stays consistent.
  • The introduction has a hook, background and a clear claim.
  • Each body paragraph follows PEEL — Point, Evidence, Explain, Link.
  • One counter-argument is admitted and answered with a rebuttal.
  • I have used at least five persuasive tools naturally (DAFOREST and more).
  • Sentence variety: short punchy sentences, rhetorical questions, a triplet, a conditional.
  • The conclusion ends with a clear, specific call to action.

Practice tasks

  1. Task 1: Many students throw away plastic water bottles every day. Write a letter to your head teacher persuading him to install water refill stations in the school. Write the opening paragraph and a PEEL plan.

    Show answer
    Opening: Dear Mr Khaled, I am writing on behalf of the Year 8 Eco Club to ask you to install water refill stations around our school. Every day, hundreds of plastic bottles end up in our bins, and I believe this simple change could transform our school's impact on the environment. PEEL plan: P1 Point — waste (Evidence: our club counted over 300 bottles in one day. Explain: that is tens of thousands a year. Link: refill stations would stop this). P2 Point — health (students drink more water when it is free and cold). P3 Point — reputation and money (families save money. The school leads by example). Counter: cost of the machines → cost shared with the parents' association, and savings on cleaning. Close: call to action — 'I urge you to approve this proposal before the summer term, and I look forward to hearing from you. Yours sincerely, …'

  2. Task 2: Name the persuasive technique in each sentence: (a) Together, we can make our streets safe again. (b) This plan is cheap, simple and effective. (c) Would you want to breathe this air every morning? (d) Nine out of ten residents support the new park.

    Show answer
    (a) Inclusive language (we) and emotive language. (b) Triplet (rule of three). (c) Rhetorical question with direct address. (d) Statistic.

  3. Task 3: Write a rebuttal to this counter-argument: 'A longer lunch break would waste learning time.'

    Show answer
    Some people claim that a longer lunch break would waste valuable learning time. However, students who have had time to eat properly and rest return to lessons more focused, so the learning that does happen in the afternoon is far more effective. Surely thirty minutes of real rest is worth more than an hour of tired, distracted students.

  4. Task 4: Improve this weak call to action: 'So that is why I think we should have a library. Bye.'

    Show answer
    So, the choice is clear: a library would give every student a quiet place to read, learn and dream. Sign our petition at the reception desk before Friday, tell your parents about it, and help us turn an empty room into the heart of our school. Thank you for listening.
