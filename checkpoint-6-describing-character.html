Checkpoint 6 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Describing a Character (STEAL)

How to create a believable, memorable character with the STEAL method — Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions and Looks — plus show-don’t-tell, imagery for characters, sentence frames, short character sketches and a full sample story.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is character description?

Every story needs a character the reader cares about. Characterisation is the way a writer shows what a character is like: their personality, their feelings and how they change. In Paper 2 (Fiction), your characters help you earn Creation of texts marks (an engaging, believable story) and Vocabulary and language marks (well-chosen, imaginative words).

There are two ways to describe a character:

TypeWhat the writer doesExample
Direct characterisationTELLS the reader what the character is like.Karim was shy but brave.
Indirect characterisationSHOWS the character through what they say, think and do, how others react and how they look. The reader works it out.Karim gave a tiny wave. Later, while everyone else froze, he walked straight across the pitch to help.

Indirect characterisation is almost always stronger in a story, because the reader discovers the character for themselves. A small amount of direct description is fine (‘He was small for his age’), but the personality should be shown.

In the exam

In the exam, about 30 minutes is suggested for the whole writing task (Paper 2, Section B, 25 marks). Many strong answers are about 250-350 words. Your character description must live inside a story: show the character through STEAL while the events move forward.

2. Features: the STEAL method

STEAL is the tool for showing a character. Each letter is one way of revealing personality.

LetterWhat it isQuestion to askExample
S — SpeechWhat the character says and HOW they say it.Is the voice kind, harsh, quiet, loud, funny, nervous?“Hello,” he murmured, so quietly that only the front row heard him.
T — ThoughtsWhat the character thinks or feels inside.What are they worried about, hoping for, hiding?Keep calm, he told himself. It’s only the first day.
E — Effect on othersHow other people react to the character.Do people laugh, fall silent, feel safe, keep away?The boys stood in a silent circle, staring at the new boy.
A — ActionsWhat the character does, including small habits.What does this action show about them?He folded his jumper into a pillow and gently lifted Ziad’s foot onto it.
L — LooksAppearance: face, clothes, body language, expressions.Which ONE detail tells us most about them?Glasses that kept sliding down his nose.
You do not need all five every time

A quick sketch of a minor character needs only two (for example Looks + Actions). Your main character should get all five across the whole story — not all crammed into the first paragraph. Spread them out: Looks when they first appear, Speech and Thoughts in the build-up, Actions and Effect on others at the climax.

3. Show, don’t tell

Instead of naming a feeling or a personality trait, show the evidence. The reader then ‘sees’ the character.

4. Imagery for characters

Imagery (similes, metaphors and personification) makes a character unforgettable. Choose a comparison that fits the personality you want to show.

DetailPlainWith imageryWhat it suggests
VoiceHe had a loud voice.His voice boomed like a drum across the yard.Powerful, confident, maybe frightening.
EyesShe had bright eyes.Her eyes sparkled like the Nile at sunrise.Lively, happy, full of energy.
MovementHe moved quickly.He darted between the players like a fish through reeds.Quick, clever, hard to catch.
HandsHis hands were old.His hands were a map of wrinkles and old scars.A long, hard-working life (metaphor).
SmileShe smiled.Her smile switched on like a lamp in a dark room.Warm, comforting.
TemperHe got angry easily.His temper was a kettle, always close to boiling.Quick to anger (metaphor).

5. How to build a character — step by step

  1. Decide one main impression. Before writing, choose two or three key traits (for example: shy, clever, secretly brave). Every STEAL detail should support them.
  2. Choose one stand-out look. Not a passport description (height, hair colour, eye colour…). One detail that says something: glasses that keep slipping, a patched shirt, a whistle always round his neck.
  3. Give the character a voice. One or two lines of speech that sound like THEM. A shy character murmurs. A bossy one gives orders.
  4. Show a habit or action. A small, repeated action is easy to remember and can return at the end (Karim pushing up his glasses).
  5. Let others react. How the other characters treat your character tells the reader a lot — and it can change during the story.
  6. Let us inside their head. One or two thoughts, especially at the most difficult moment.
  7. Show change at the climax. The best stories reveal a hidden side of the character, or show them changing: the shy boy is brave, the bully is kind, the selfish girl shares.
Part of the storyWhere STEAL goes
OpeningLooks (one stand-out detail) + a first line of speech or a habit.
Build-upThoughts and Effect on others: what the character worries about, how others treat them.
ClimaxActions: what the character DOES when it really matters. This is where the personality is proved.
EndingEffect on others has changed, or the habit returns with a new meaning (a circular ending).

6. Building blocks: mix-and-match sentences

STEALFrameFilled in
LooksThe first thing anyone noticed about [name] was [detail].The first thing anyone noticed about Uncle Sayed was his enormous moustache.
Looks + Actions[Name] had [looks], and moved with [body language].Laila had paint on her fingers and moved with quick, impatient steps.
SpeechWhen [name] spoke, it came out [tone]: “[words].”When Coach Hamdy spoke, it came out like thunder: “Again!”
ThoughtsInside, [name] felt [feeling], though [he/she] tried to hide it.Inside, Youssef felt sick with worry, though he tried to hide it.
Effect on othersWhenever [name] walked into a room, [reaction].Whenever Teta walked into a room, the arguments stopped.
Actions[Name] would often [habit], a small habit that showed [trait].Adam would often straighten his pencils, a small habit that showed how much he hated mess.
Closing imageWhat stayed with me was [detail], like [image].What stayed with me was his laugh, like a door opening onto sunshine.

7. Short character sketches to study

Each sketch uses only some STEAL tools. Spot which ones before you read the label.

  • The old fisherman (Looks, Actions, Effect on others): Amm Mahmoud’s skin was brown and creased like an old leather bag. Every evening, he sat on an upturned bucket by the harbour, mending his nets with fingers that never stopped moving. The young fishermen always asked him about the weather, and they never sailed if he shook his head.
  • The strict coach (Looks, Speech, Effect on others): Coach Hamdy wore the same red tracksuit every day and a silver whistle that never left his neck. “Again,” he would say, after every mistake. Nobody argued. Even the tallest boys stood up straight when that whistle flashed in the sun.
  • The nervous competitor (Thoughts, Actions): Salma read her speech card for the hundredth time. What if I forget every word? The card was crumpled and damp in her hand. When her name was called, she stood up so fast that her chair fell over.
  • The bully who is not what he seems (Speech, Actions, Thoughts): “Move,” Tamer snapped, shoving past the younger boys at the canteen. He bought three sandwiches. Later, behind the sports hall, he gave two of them to his little brother. Nobody can know, he thought.
  • The kind shopkeeper (Speech, Actions, Effect on others): Amm Fathy’s shop was tiny, but it was never empty. “Pay me tomorrow, my son,” he would say, waving a hand. He knew every child’s name. On the coldest nights, the street cats waited by his door, because they knew he would come out with a bowl of milk.

9. Strong sample story

The New Boy (realistic fiction, complete story)334 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Write a story about a new student who arrives at a school. Show what the new student is like through what they say, do and think, and how other people react to them.

Karim arrived in Class 6B on a grey Tuesday in October. He was small for his age, with a neatly ironed shirt, a school bag that was far too big for him and glasses that kept sliding down his nose. He pushed them up every few seconds, as if he were checking that the world was still there.

“Everyone, this is Karim. He has just moved here from Aswan,” said Mr Samir.

Karim gave a tiny wave. “Hello,” he murmured, so quietly that only the front row heard him.

At break, Ziad, who thought he was the funniest boy in the school, copied the way Karim pushed up his glasses. A few boys laughed. Karim’s ears turned red. Keep calm, he told himself. It’s only the first day. He sat alone on the bench by the fence and opened a small notebook. His pencil moved quickly, and he did not look up once.

Then, during the football match, there was a horrible crack. Ziad lay on the ground, clutching his ankle and groaning. Everyone froze. Somebody shouted for a teacher, but nobody moved closer.

Except Karim.

He walked straight across the pitch, knelt beside Ziad and spoke in a calm, steady voice. “Don’t move it. My father’s a nurse. Breathe slowly, like this.” He folded his jumper into a pillow and gently lifted Ziad’s foot onto it. His hands did not shake at all.

By the time Mr Samir arrived, Ziad had stopped crying. The boys stood in a silent circle, staring at the new boy as if they were seeing him for the first time.

The next morning, there was a crowd around Karim’s desk. He had opened his notebook. Inside were drawings of every player from the match, each one perfect, and on the last page was Ziad, in the middle of a kick, looking like a champion.

“Can you draw me?” Ziad asked, a little shyly.

Karim pushed his glasses up his nose and smiled. “Only if you stop copying me.”

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: the task is answered in full — Karim’s personality is shown through what he says, does and thinks, and through how others react. The story has a clear character change in the other boys’ opinion of Karim, which makes it engaging and satisfying.
  • STEAL — Looks: the oversized bag and the glasses ‘that kept sliding down his nose’ (paragraph 1). Speech: the shy ‘murmured’ hello (paragraph 3) contrasts with the calm, steady instructions at the climax (paragraph 7). Thoughts: ‘Keep calm, he told himself’ (paragraph 4). Effect on others: the boys laugh at first (paragraph 4), then stand ‘in a silent circle’ (paragraph 8) and crowd around his desk (paragraph 9). Actions: drawing alone, then kneeling to help Ziad (paragraphs 4 and 7).
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise verbs (murmured, clutching, knelt, folded) · a revealing comparison for a habit (‘as if he were checking that the world was still there’) · imagery at the end (‘looking like a champion’) · body language instead of feeling words (‘Karim’s ears turned red’, ‘His hands did not shake at all’).
  • Structure of texts 7/7: opening (arrival and looks) → build-up (teasing and loneliness) → problem (the injury) → the two-word paragraph ‘Except Karim.’ as the turning point → climax (Karim helps) → resolution (the drawings). The glasses habit opens and closes the story: a circular ending with a joke that shows Karim’s new confidence.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: a relative clause inside commas for extra information (‘Ziad, who thought he was the funniest boy in the school, copied…’) · varied starters (‘At break,’ ‘Then,’ ‘By the time…’, ‘The next morning,’) · direct thought shown without speech marks · dialogue punctuated correctly with a new line for each speaker · past and past perfect tenses used accurately.
  • Spelling 3/3: ironed, murmured, clutching, groaning, jumper, champion, shyly — all correct.

10. Common mistakes and checklist

MistakeBetter
A ‘passport’ description: ‘He was tall. He had brown hair and brown eyes.’One telling detail: ‘He was so tall that he ducked under every doorway, as if apologising for his size.’
Only telling: ‘She was kind and helpful.’Show it with an action: ‘She stayed behind to help the caretaker stack the chairs.’
All five STEAL tools in the first paragraphSpread them across the story.
Too many characters, all described a littleTwo or three characters · describe the main one most.
Speech that sounds like anyoneGive each character their own voice: shy, bossy, funny, gentle.
The character does something that does not fit their personality, with no reasonIf a character changes, show WHY (a challenge, a discovery).
Character checklist
  • I decided my character’s two or three key traits before writing.
  • I used one stand-out Looks detail, not a list.
  • My character speaks in a voice that suits them.
  • I showed at least one Thought.
  • Other characters react to my character (Effect on others).
  • My character’s Actions at the climax prove what they are really like.
  • I showed feelings through the body, not just feeling words.
  • I used one simile or metaphor for the character.

11. Practice

  1. Why does the author write that Karim pushed up his glasses ‘as if he were checking that the world was still there’?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show a nervous habit, so we understand Karim is anxious on his first day · to make him seem careful and thoughtful · to create a memorable, slightly funny picture of him · to set up the habit that returns in the last line (circular ending) · to make the reader feel sympathy for him.

  2. Why does the author put ‘Except Karim.’ in a paragraph on its own?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to make Karim’s action stand out as the turning point · to create a pause and suspense before we see what he does · to show the contrast between Karim and everyone else, who froze · to surprise the reader, because the shy boy is the one who acts.

  3. Find one example of each STEAL tool in the sample story.

    Show answer
    Speech: “Hello,” he murmured. Thoughts: Keep calm, he told himself. Effect on others: the boys stood in a silent circle, staring. Actions: he folded his jumper into a pillow. Looks: glasses that kept sliding down his nose.

  4. Change these ‘telling’ sentences into ‘showing’ sentences: (a) Omar was lazy. (b) Aunt Nadia was generous.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: (a) Omar’s homework lay untouched while he scrolled through videos for the third hour. (b) Aunt Nadia never visited without a bag of sweets for every child in the building.

  5. Write a two-sentence sketch of a grumpy neighbour using only Speech and Effect on others.

    Show answer
    Possible answer: “Keep that ball away from my car!” Mr Gamal barked from his balcony. Every child in the street grabbed the ball and disappeared in seconds.

  6. Practice task: ‘Write a story about a character who surprises everyone.’ Write a short plan with STEAL notes.

    Show answer
    G.C.S.P.P.: realistic fiction · Hassan (quiet, always at the back of the class) and his classmates · a school talent show in Giza · third person · whole story. STEAL — Looks: oversized blazer, eyes on the floor · Speech: one-word answers · Thoughts: ‘Nobody expects anything from me’ · Effect: classmates forget he is there · Actions: secretly practises the oud every night. Plot: (1) talent show announced, Hassan does not sign up · (2) a performer is ill · (3) the teacher asks for a volunteer · (4) climax — Hassan walks onto the stage and plays, the hall falls silent · (5) ending — people now greet him in the corridor, and he looks up when they do.
