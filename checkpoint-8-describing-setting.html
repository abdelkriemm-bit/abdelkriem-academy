Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Describing a Setting

How to describe a place so the reader sees, hears and feels it: mood, zooming in, senses, strong verbs and figurative language, with building-block sentences and a 350+ word sample story.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is a setting description?

The setting is the time and place where a story happens: a village house at sunset, a crowded market in the morning, a school corridor at night. A setting description makes the reader see, hear, smell and feel that place, and — most importantly — it creates a mood (atmosphere) that suits the story.

In the exam, setting description is not a separate task: it lives inside your Paper 2 story (Section B Writing, 25 marks, about 35 minutes). Many tasks include a bullet such as 'describe the place' or 'describe where the character is', and even when they do not, a vivid setting is one of the quickest ways to earn marks for Vocabulary and language and Creation of texts. The reading section of Paper 2 also often asks how a writer creates atmosphere in a description, so this skill helps you twice.

Purpose of a setting descriptionExample idea
To create a mood or atmosphereA silent, dusty house makes the reader feel sad or uneasy.
To show time passing or changeThe village looks different after five years — new buildings, dirty canal.
To reflect a character's feelingsGrey rain on the day a boy loses the final (pathetic fallacy).
To build tension before an eventFlickering lights and a dark corridor before something strange happens.
To make the story believableReal, specific details: the call to prayer, a donkey cart, a microbus.

2. Features of a strong setting description

FeatureExample (original)
One clear mood chosen before you writeNostalgic and a little sad: faded paint, empty rooms, a smell of dried mint.
Wide view → close detail (zooming in)The whole village… the lane… the blue door… the missing brass knocker.

Verbs, not just adjectives

Weak description piles up adjectives: 'The old, dirty, quiet, sad house.' Strong description gives the place actions: 'Paint peeled from the door in long, curling strips, and every step complained underfoot.' The verbs (peeled, complained) do the describing.

3. How to build a setting description — step by step

  1. Choose one mood. Warm, tense, eerie, peaceful, busy, lonely, nostalgic? Every detail you pick must support it. If the mood is eerie, do not describe a cheerful ice-cream van (unless you want a deliberate contrast).
  2. Start wide. One sentence that places the reader: where are we and what does it look like from a distance? (The microbus left me at the edge of the village, where the asphalt gave up and the dust began.)
  3. Zoom in. Move closer to one or two telling details — a door, a window, an object that tells a story (the missing brass knocker shows the house is already being emptied).
  4. Add senses. Sound, then smell, then touch or temperature. Taste only if it is natural (salt in the air at the beach, dust in the mouth in a sandstorm).
  5. Use one excellent comparison. One fresh simile or metaphor in a paragraph is stronger than five ordinary ones.
  6. Let a character move through the place. Description is strongest when it is part of the action: the character walks, touches, notices, remembers.
  7. Show change. Light changes, weather changes, the place changes over time — this gives your description a structure, not just a list.
  8. End with an echo of the mood. Close on a small, specific detail rather than explaining the feeling ('It made me feel sad').

How setting fits into the whole story (whole story, 5–7 paragraphs):

ParagraphWhat to writeSetting job
1. OpeningArrive in the place. Wide view. First hookEstablish the mood immediately
2. Build-upMove closer. Telling details. A character meets the main characterShow change / contrast. Give clues to the conflict
3. Rising actionThe character explores. Memories or tension growSenses deepen the mood. One strong image

4. Building blocks: mix-and-match sentences

Use these frames to build a description fast in the exam. Pick three or four categories — you rarely need all of them — and adapt them to first person (I) or third person (he, she).

CategoryFrameExample
OrientationI stood at [place], taking in [the scene] for the first time in years.I stood at the end of the lane, taking in Grandfather's house for the first time in five years.
Visual detailIn front of [me/her], [visual detail] stretched out, impossible to ignore.In front of me, the canal stretched out, green and lazy, impossible to ignore.
SoundThe only sound was [sound 1], broken now and then by [sound 2].The only sound was the hum of the fridge, broken now and then by a car horn far below.
SmellThe air smelled of [smell], sharp and unmistakable.The air smelled of diesel and roasted corn, sharp and unmistakable.
Touch / temperatureWhen [small action], [I/she] felt [touch] against [skin].When the door opened, I felt cool, stale air against my face.
ComparisonIn the [light], the [object] looked [comparison].In the orange light, the unfinished building looked like a burnt match.
Closing echoAs [I/she] turned away, [final detail] stayed in [my/her] mind — [echo].As I turned away, the palm tree stayed in my mind — a green flag that refused to come down.

Mood word banks
  • Peaceful: gentle, drifting, golden, murmur, still, soft breeze, lapping water.
  • Eerie: flickering, hollow, creaking, motionless, shadowy, stale air, a silence that watches.
  • Busy: jostling, clatter, shouting, honking, swirling, crowded, a river of people.
  • Nostalgic: faded, worn smooth, familiar, dusty, half-forgotten, the smell of…, as it had always been.

Timing in the exam. Section B Writing has about 35 minutes. Use them like this:

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph. Choose your mood and the three senses you will use.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). Keep checking: is every detail building the same mood?
30–35Proofread: speech punctuation, capital letters, apostrophes, spelling of your ambitious words, tense slips and missing words.

6. A strong sample story

Sample story — The Palm by the Canal (realistic fiction, setting-led)462 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: You have read an extract about a man who returns to the town where he grew up. Write a story about a character who goes back to a place that is important to them. In your story you should: describe the place and how it has changed, show how the character feels, and explain what happens there and how the story ends.

The microbus coughed once and abandoned me at the edge of the village, where the asphalt gave up and the dust began.

Five years had passed since I had last walked this road. Back then, it had seemed endless; now, it took me less than ten minutes. The canal still ran beside it, slow and green, but plastic bottles bobbed where I remembered ducks. On the far bank, a half-finished red-brick building stood among the fields, its iron rods poking at the sky like the fingers of someone who had forgotten what they wanted to say.

Grandfather's house waited at the end of the lane. Its blue door had faded to the colour of an old bruise, and the paint peeled away in long, curling strips. Somebody had already taken the brass knocker. Inside, the air was cool and stale, heavy with the smell of dust and dried mint. Sunlight fell through the shutters in thin gold bars, and tiny specks of dust drifted inside them as if they were swimming.

"You came," said Grandfather. He was sitting on a packing box in the middle of the empty room, smaller than I remembered, his white galabeya folded neatly over his knees. "The buyers arrive tomorrow. I wanted someone to see it before it stops being ours."

We walked through the house together. In the kitchen, the clay oven was cold and cracked. On the stairs, each step complained under our feet. On the roof, however, nothing had changed at all: the pigeon tower still leaned towards the fields, and beyond it the date palm rose above everything, tall and stubborn, its fronds rattling softly in the evening breeze.

I leant over the parapet and ran my hand along the trunk. There, half-hidden beneath the rough bark, were two letters carved years ago by a small boy with a borrowed knife: A.M.

"You thought I never noticed," Grandfather chuckled.

As the sun sank, the whole village changed. The canal turned to copper. The unfinished building became a black shape against an orange sky, and the call to prayer rolled across the rooftops, joined by the barking of a distant dog and the clatter of a donkey cart. For a few minutes, everything looked exactly as it had five years earlier.

Grandfather put his hand on my shoulder. "Houses can be sold," he said quietly. "Roots are a different matter."

The next morning, before the buyers arrived, we went downstairs with a spade and an old tin can. Near the foot of the palm, where its roots reached towards the canal, we dug up a tiny green shoot that had sprouted from a fallen date, and I carried it home on the microbus, held carefully in my lap all the way to Cairo.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: all three bullets are fully developed — the place and how it has changed (paragraphs 2–3: plastic bottles instead of ducks, the new building, the faded door). The character's feelings shown, not told (the missing knocker, the carved initials, the hand on the shoulder). What happens and the ending (the roof, the sunset, the shoot taken home). The theme of returning and belonging runs through to the final image.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise nouns (galabeya, parapet, pigeon tower, clay oven, fronds). Strong verbs (coughed, abandoned, bobbed, poking, complained, rattling). Fresh figurative language — personification of the microbus and the stairs, the simile of the iron rods 'like the fingers of someone who had forgotten what they wanted to say', the metaphor 'the canal turned to copper'. Four senses: sight, sound (call to prayer, clatter, rattling), smell (dust and dried mint), touch (rough bark).
  • Structure of texts 7/7: the description zooms from wide (the village road) to close (the door, the knocker, the dust in the sunlight, the initials). Contrast between 'back then' and 'now' gives the description a shape. The sunset paragraph is the emotional climax, where the setting itself changes. The ending links back to the opening microbus — a circular structure — and the shoot gives a hopeful final image.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: varied sentence types — a long complex opening sentence, simple sentences for impact ('Somebody had already taken the brass knocker.', 'The canal turned to copper.'), compound-complex sentences in the sunset paragraph. Varied starters (prepositional phrases 'On the far bank', 'In the kitchen', 'On the roof', subordinate clause 'As the sun sank'). Punctuation: a semicolon joining two related clauses ('Back then, it had seemed endless; now, it took me less than ten minutes.'), colons for explanation and emphasis ('nothing had changed at all:' / 'a borrowed knife: A.M.'), accurate speech punctuation, commas after fronted adverbials. Past and past perfect used accurately ('had passed', 'had faded').
  • Word structure (spelling) 3/3: ambitious words spelt correctly (abandoned, parapet, unfinished, carefully, chuckled, sprouted). Possessives correct (Grandfather's house). No homophone errors (there/their, where/were).

Why does the author use…? (answers you can write about this sample in a reading question)

  1. Why does the author use personification in 'The microbus coughed once and abandoned me'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to make the old, unreliable microbus seem alive and tired (imagery). To show the narrator feels left alone or unwelcome as soon as he arrives ('abandoned'). To create a slightly sad, lonely mood from the very first line. To hook the reader with an unusual, vivid opening.

  2. Why does the author use the simile 'like the fingers of someone who had forgotten what they wanted to say'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to help the reader picture the thin iron rods sticking up from the unfinished building. To suggest the building is incomplete and purposeless. To show the narrator's feeling that the village has changed in an awkward, unwelcome way. To create a sad, uncertain mood.

  3. Why does the author use the semicolon in 'Back then, it had seemed endless; now, it took me less than ten minutes.'?

    Show answer
    To join two independent clauses that are closely related: both are about the length of the road, so joining them helps the reader see at once how much the narrator has grown and how the place feels different.

  4. Why does the author use the colon in 'with a borrowed knife: A.M.'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to introduce extra information (exactly what was carved). To create emphasis on the initials, the most important detail of the scene. To introduce an explanation of the 'two letters' mentioned earlier in the sentence.

  5. Why does the author use the metaphor 'The canal turned to copper'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show the colour of the sunset reflected on the water. To show the dull, dirty canal becoming beautiful for a moment. To show how the setting changes as the day ends. To create a warm, nostalgic mood at the emotional climax. A short simple sentence also gives the image impact.

  6. Why does the author list the sounds 'the call to prayer… the barking of a distant dog and the clatter of a donkey cart'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to make the setting realistic and specific. To appeal to the sense of hearing so the reader feels present. To show the village's traditional life still continues. To build a peaceful, nostalgic atmosphere. The onomatopoeic 'clatter' adds sound imagery.

7. Common mistakes and checklist

MistakeBetter
A long list of adjectives: 'a big, old, dark, scary house'Choose one or two and add a verb: 'a dark house that leaned over the street'
Only sightAdd at least two more senses: sound, smell, touch
Mixing moods with no reason (scary corridor + cheerful birds)Every detail supports the chosen mood
Clichés: 'as white as snow', 'it was a dark and stormy night'A fresh comparison from the real place: 'as white as a new galabeya'
Description that stops the story for a whole pageWeave description into action: the character walks, touches, notices
Telling the feeling at the end: 'It made me feel very sad.'End on a detail that carries the feeling: 'Somebody had already taken the brass knocker.'
Tense slips: 'The sun sets and the sky was orange'Keep the past tense: 'The sun set and the sky turned orange.'
Setting checklist
  • I chose one clear mood, and every detail supports it.
  • I moved from a wide view to close, specific details.
  • I used at least three senses.
  • I used precise nouns and strong verbs, not just adjectives.
  • I used one or two fresh comparisons (simile, metaphor or personification).
  • The setting changes or reacts at the key moment (light, weather, sound).
  • I ended on a specific image that echoes the mood, not on 'it made me feel…'.
  • My tense and point of view stay the same throughout.

8. Practice

  1. Practice task: 'You have read an extract set in a busy fish market. Write a story about a character who visits a place full of people and noise.' Bullets: describe the place · something unexpected happens · how the story ends. Write a short plan.

    Show answer
    Mood: busy and lively, turning tense. G.C.S.P.P.: realistic fiction. Hamza (12, sent to buy fish alone for the first time) and a fishmonger with a booming voice. Anfoushi fish market, Alexandria, early morning. First person. Whole story. Plan: (1) wide view — gulls, wet stone, ice, shouting prices. (2) zoom in — silver fish on ice, the smell of salt and diesel. (3) Hamza loses the money in the crowd. (4) climax — the market noise becomes a wall, he panics. (5) the fishmonger noticed the money fall and kept it for him. (6) ending — the same market, now friendly. He goes home with the fish and a new friend.

  2. Rewrite with three senses and one comparison: 'The kitchen was nice.'

    Show answer
    Example: The kitchen smelled of cumin and frying onions, and steam curled from the pot like a lazy cat. A radio murmured on the windowsill, and the tiles were warm under my bare feet.

  3. Rewrite the cliché: 'It was a dark and stormy night.'

    Show answer
    Example: Rain hammered the tin roof, and the streetlight outside flickered like a tired eye.

  4. Write two sentences describing the same street, first peaceful and then threatening.

    Show answer
    Peaceful: Morning light spilled across the empty street, and a single bicycle ticked lazily past the bakery. Threatening: The streetlights buzzed and died one by one, and the empty street seemed to hold its breath.

  5. Add pathetic fallacy: a boy has just failed an important exam. Describe the walk home.

    Show answer
    Example: Grey clouds sagged low over the rooftops, and a thin, cold drizzle soaked through his blazer. Even the traffic seemed to crawl, as if the whole city had lost the will to hurry.

  6. Why does a writer zoom in from a wide view to a small detail? Give two effects.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: it guides the reader's eye like a camera, so the description is organised. It makes the small detail feel important, so the reader notices it. It builds curiosity or tension as the view narrows. It often reveals the character's feelings through what they choose to notice.
