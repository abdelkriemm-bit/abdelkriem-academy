ACT Colons: Lists, Explanations and the Traps

When ACT English accepts a colon — after a complete sentence to introduce a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information — where it is never allowed, and how it differs from the semicolon and the dash, with worked examples and an ACT-style quiz.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

The colon (:) is a pointing mark: the first part of the sentence makes a promise, and the colon says “here it is”. ACT English tests one main condition — there must be a complete sentence before the colon — and a few favourite wrong uses. This lesson teaches the four jobs of the colon, where it is never allowed, and how to tell it apart from the semicolon and the dash.

What this question type tests

Colon questions belong to the Conventions of Standard English category, which is 38–43% of the 40 scored English questions. A portion of the essay is underlined and the choices differ in punctuation, usually with NO CHANGE first. A colon may be the right answer (for example, to introduce a list or an explanation), or it may be the trap (a colon after “are”, “including” or “such as”).

The main rule: a complete sentence before the colon

A colon comes after a complete sentence (independent clause). What comes after the colon can be almost anything that the first part points to: one word, a phrase, a list or another complete sentence. So the test always looks at the part before the colon.

The colon check

Cover the colon and everything after it. Can the part before it stand alone as a full sentence and make sense with a period? Yes → a colon is possible. No → the colon is wrong. Then check that what follows really is the list, explanation, emphasis or extra information that the first part points to.

The four jobs of the colon

JobWhat follows the colon✓ Example
1. Introduce a listthe items that the sentence mentionsShe packed three things: bread, cheese, and olives.
2. Introduce an explanationthe reason or the details of what was just saidThe trip was cancelled: the road to the oasis had flooded.
3. Give emphasisone word or short phrase saved for the endThere was only one thing he feared: silence.
4. Add extra informationa fact, a name, a question or a quotation that the first part announcesThe project began with one question: how much of the music survived?

1. Lists. The sentence before the colon usually contains a word that announces the list: “three things”, “the following”, “two tools”.

  • ✓ The craftsmen work with three materials: tin, coloured glass, and wire.
  • ✓ Bring the following: a pencil, an eraser, and a calculator.
  • ✗ The craftsmen work with: tin, coloured glass, and wire. (“The craftsmen work with” is not complete; the colon cuts the preposition from its object.)
  • ✗ The three materials are: tin, coloured glass, and wire. (The colon cuts the verb from its complement.)
  • ✓ The three materials are tin, coloured glass, and wire. (No punctuation is needed at all.)

2. Explanations. After a complete sentence, a colon can introduce a second complete sentence that explains or proves the first. Here the colon also works as a way to join two sentences.

  • ✓ The work is difficult: a single mistake can crack the glass.
  • ✓ I finally understood why she hid the notebook: it was a diary of our family.
  • ✗ The work is difficult, a single mistake can crack the glass. (comma splice)

3. Emphasis. The sentence builds up, and the colon saves the key word for the end.

  • ✓ After months of training, she had one goal: gold.
  • ✓ The village lacked only one thing: clean water.

4. Extra information. The first part announces something — a question, a name, a quotation, a rule — and the colon delivers it.

  • ✓ The sign on the door gave a simple rule: no phones after eight.
  • ✓ My grandfather always repeated the same advice: “Measure twice, cut once.”

Where a colon is never allowed

Never put a colon…✗ Wrong✓ Right
after a verb (is, are, was, include, contain, want…)The winners were: Omar and Laila.The winners were Omar and Laila.
after a preposition (of, to, with, for, in, by…)The box was full of: coins and rings.The box was full of coins and rings.
after such as, including, like, for exampleWe need tools such as: hammers and saws.We need tools such as hammers and saws.
after an incomplete start or a dependent clauseBecause the road flooded: the trip ended.Because the road flooded, the trip ended.
between a subject and its verbThe best part of the trip: was the boat ride.The best part of the trip was the boat ride.
in pairs, around extra informationMy uncle: a carpenter: fixed the door.My uncle, a carpenter, fixed the door.
between items of a listRosetta: Tanta: and MansouraRosetta, Tanta, and Mansoura
followed by and/but (“: and”)The work was slow: and the singers were old.The work was slow, and the singers were old.

A useful rescue: if the words before the list are incomplete, you can either delete the colon or make the first part complete by adding words such as “the following” or “three things”. On the ACT, the choices will usually offer the version with no colon.

  • ✗ The menu included: soup, rice, and fish.
  • ✓ The menu included soup, rice, and fish.
  • ✓ The menu included three dishes: soup, rice, and fish.
الكولون في جملة واحدة

الكولون (:) لازم قبله جملة كاملة تنفع تقف لوحدها. بعده بييجي واحد من أربعة: قائمة، أو شرح، أو تأكيد على كلمة، أو معلومة زيادة. ممنوع بعد فعل زي are و include، وممنوع بعد حرف جر زي of و with، وممنوع بعد such as و including.

Colon vs semicolon vs dash

The three marks look similar, and the ACT often puts them together in one set of choices. Each one has its own conditions:

MarkBefore itAfter itJob
Colon (:)a complete sentenceanything: word, phrase, list or sentenceintroduces a list, explanation, emphasis or extra information
Semicolon (;)a complete sentencea complete sentence (or, by the SAT/ACT extra rule, the next item of a list whose items contain commas)joins two closely related sentences
Single dash (—)usually a complete sentencethe emphasised ending or an interruptionemphasis or an interruption
Pair of dashes (— … —)the rest of the sentencethe rest of the sentenceextra information in the middle; must close with a second dash
  • ✓ We needed three things: maps, water, and patience. (list → colon)
  • ✗ We needed three things; maps, water, and patience. (a semicolon needs a complete sentence after it)
  • ✓ The bridge was closed; we took the ferry. (two sentences → semicolon works)
  • ✓ The bridge was closed: engineers had found a crack. (the second sentence explains the first → colon also works)
  • ✓ He had trained for one moment — the final lap. (single dash for emphasis)
  • ✓ Her handwriting — small, neat, and always in blue ink — filled every page. (pair of dashes = extra information)
  • ✗ Her handwriting — small, neat, and always in blue ink: filled every page. (mixed pair; a colon can never close extra information)

Key difference to remember: a semicolon needs a complete sentence on both sides; a colon needs a complete sentence only before it. So when what follows is a list or a short phrase, the semicolon is out and the colon (if the first part is complete) is in. And a colon is never used in a pair.

إزاي أفرّق بين الثلاثة

السيميكولون: جملة كاملة قبله وجملة كاملة بعده. الكولون: جملة كاملة قبله بس، وبعده أي حاجة بتشرح أو بتعدّد. الشرطتين: معلومة زيادة في نص الجملة، ولازم تتقفل بشرطة تانية، مش بكولون ولا بفاصلة.

Worked examples

Each example is part of an ACT-style essay. The underlined portion is shown in bold.

Worked example 1. “The volunteers brought everything the clinic needed; bandages, blankets, and bottled water.”

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) needed: bandages,
  • C) needed, such as: bandages,
  • D) needed bandages,

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. “The volunteers brought everything the clinic needed” is a complete sentence, and the colon introduces the list of what was needed.
  • A uses a semicolon before a list; a semicolon needs a complete sentence after it.
  • C puts a colon straight after “such as”.
  • D has no punctuation, so “needed bandages” runs together and the sentence no longer makes sense.

Worked example 2. “The three subjects on Sunday's timetable are: physics, history, and art.”

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) are; physics,
  • C) are physics,
  • D) are, physics,

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. “The three subjects on Sunday's timetable are” is not complete: the verb “are” needs its complement. No punctuation goes between a verb and its complement.
  • A puts a colon after the verb “are”.
  • B uses a semicolon after an incomplete start.
  • D puts a comma between the verb and its complement.

Worked example 3. “The old ferry had one serious problem, its engine stopped whenever it rained.”

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) problem its
  • C) problem: and its
  • D) problem: its

Answer: D.

  • D is correct. “The old ferry had one serious problem” is complete, and the second part explains the problem. A colon after a complete sentence introduces an explanation.
  • A is a comma splice: two complete sentences joined by a comma alone.
  • B is a run-on.
  • C puts “and” after the colon, which is never correct.

Worked example 4. “The guide pointed out several landmarks including: the Citadel, the Al-Azhar Mosque, and the Nile.”

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) several landmarks, including the
  • C) several landmarks; including the
  • D) several landmarks: including the

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. “including the Citadel, the Al-Azhar Mosque, and the Nile” gives examples; a comma before “including” sets the examples off, and nothing comes after “including”.
  • A puts a colon straight after “including”.
  • C uses a semicolon before a phrase that is not a complete sentence.
  • D puts a colon before “including”: then the colon points to “including…”, which is not the list itself, and the result is awkward and incorrect on the ACT.
Classic traps
  • Colon after a verb: “The winners were: …”, “The kit includes: …”.
  • Colon after a preposition: “full of: …”, “work with: …”.
  • Colon after such as / including: “such as: …”.
  • Semicolon instead of a colon before a list: “three things; bread…”.
  • Comma splice instead of a colon: “The work is difficult, a mistake can crack the glass.”
  • Colon used to close extra information that opened with a dash or comma.
  • Colon + and/but: “…problem: and its engine…”.
Fast strategy for test day
  • See a colon in a choice? Cover it and everything after it: is the first part a complete sentence?
  • Last word before the colon is a verb, a preposition, such as or including? Eliminate it.
  • List after a complete sentence → colon; list after an incomplete start → no punctuation.
  • What follows is a list or phrase? A semicolon is impossible there.
  • Extra information in the middle? Use a matching pair (commas or dashes), never colons.

Practice

Two short ACT-style essays, one informational and one narrative, with 5 questions each. The numbers in brackets match the question numbers. Every question tests a colon decision from this page.

Remember
  • A colon comes after a complete sentence and introduces a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information.
  • Never a colon after a verb (are, include), a preposition (of, to, with) or such as / including.
  • A list after an incomplete start takes no punctuation: “The winners were Omar and Laila.”
  • Semicolon = complete sentence on both sides; colon = complete sentence before it only.
  • Colons never come in pairs; extra information in the middle uses matching commas or dashes.
