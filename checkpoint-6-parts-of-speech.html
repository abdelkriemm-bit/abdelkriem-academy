Checkpoint 6 · Reading: grammar

Parts of Speech (Word Classes)

Every word class you need for Checkpoint 6: nouns (including abstract nouns), pronouns (including relative pronouns), verbs, modal verbs and infinitives, adjectives, adverbs, determiners, prepositions and conjunctions, how to name a word class in context, and practice with answers.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

Every word in a sentence has a job. The job a word does is called its word class (another name is part of speech). Nouns name things, verbs show actions, adjectives describe nouns, and so on.

Checkpoint reading papers ask about word classes directly. You may be asked: *What word class is the word 'quietly' in this sentence?*, *Find a relative pronoun in paragraph 2*, *Find an abstract noun*, or *Copy an infinitive from the text*. These are quick 1-mark questions, but only if you know the names exactly. Word classes also help your writing: strong verbs, precise adjectives and well-placed adverbs earn Vocabulary marks.

Grammar words you must know
  • Word class / part of speech: the job a word does in a sentence.
  • In context: in this sentence, in this text. The same word can belong to different classes in different sentences.
  • Identify: find and name it.
  • Copy: write the word exactly as it is in the text.

1. Nouns

A noun is a naming word. It names a person, a place, a thing, an animal, a feeling or an idea. A quick test: can you put the or a in front of it? (the castle, a friend, the idea). Names of people and places (proper nouns) are nouns too, even though we do not usually put *a* in front of them.

Type of nounWhat it isExamples
Common nouna general name, with no capital lettercity, teacher, river, dog
Proper nounthe name of a particular person, place, day, month or organisation, always a capital letterNour, Egypt, the Nile, Monday, March
Concrete nounsomething you can see, touch, hear, smell or tastesand, bell, sandwich, kite
Abstract nouna feeling, idea or quality that you cannot touch or seefear, kindness, freedom, honesty, excitement, friendship
Collective nouna name for a groupteam, flock (of birds), herd (of cattle), crowd, class
Countable nounyou can count it, and it has a pluralone pen, two pens / a child, three children
Uncountable nounyou cannot count it, and it usually has no pluralwater, rice, advice, information, furniture
Compound nountwo words joined to make one nounfootball, toothbrush, sunflower, classroom

Abstract nouns are asked about often. Many are made from adjectives or verbs with endings such as -ness, -ment, -ship, -dom, -ity, -tion, -ance:

WordAbstract noun
kind (adjective)kindness
excite (verb)excitement
friend (noun)friendship
free (adjective)freedom
curious (adjective)curiosity
imagine (verb)imagination
patient (adjective)patience
brave (adjective)bravery
Quick test for an abstract noun

Ask: Could I put it in a box? You can put a pencil in a box, so *pencil* is concrete. You cannot put *hope*, *anger* or *success* in a box, so they are abstract. But careful: an abstract noun is still a noun. In "She felt brave", *brave* is an adjective. In "Her bravery surprised us", *bravery* is an abstract noun.

Plurals. Most nouns add -s or -es (books, boxes). Some are irregular: child → children, man → men, tooth → teeth, mouse → mice, foot → feet, person → people.

2. Pronouns

A pronoun replaces a noun so that we do not repeat it. *Salma lost Salma's bag* sounds strange; *Salma lost her bag* is better.

Type of pronounWordsExample
Subject (personal) pronounsI, you, he, she, it, we, theyThey walked to school.
Object pronounsme, you, him, her, it, us, themThe teacher thanked us.
Possessive pronouns (stand alone)mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirsThe red bike is mine.
Reflexive pronounsmyself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselvesAdam taught himself to swim.
Demonstrative pronounsthis, that, these, those (standing alone)This is my favourite song.
Interrogative pronouns (questions)who, whom, whose, which, whatWho opened the window?
Relative pronounswho, which, that, whose, whomThe boy who won the race is my cousin.
Indefinite pronounssomeone, anybody, nothing, everyone, somethingSomeone knocked at the door.

Relative pronouns are very important for Checkpoint. A relative pronoun begins a relative clause, which gives more information about a noun that comes just before it:

  • who is for people: *My grandfather, who is eighty, still walks every day.*
  • which is for things and animals: *The parcel, which arrived this morning, was for Dad.*
  • that is for people or things: *This is the book that I told you about.*
  • whose shows belonging: *That is the girl whose kite got stuck in the tree.*
  • whom is a formal object form of who: *the man to whom I spoke*.

You will also see where and when used in the same way to start a relative clause about a place or a time: *the village where my mother was born*, *the day when we moved house*.

Trap: 'who', 'which' and 'that' are not always relative pronouns
  • "Who broke the vase?" Here *who* asks a question, so it is an interrogative pronoun.
  • "Which cake do you want?" Here *which* asks a question before a noun.
  • "She said that she was tired." Here *that* is a conjunction: it joins the reported words to *She said*, and it does not describe a noun.
  • "That bag is heavy." Here *that* points to a noun, so it is a determiner.
  • It is a relative pronoun only when it comes after a noun and starts extra information about that noun.

3. Verbs

A verb shows what someone or something does, is or has. Every sentence needs at least one verb. Test: can you change its tense? (*I jump → I jumped*.)

Type of verbWhat it doesExamples
Action verbsshow an action you can do or thinkrun, write, whisper, decide, climb
Linking verbslink the subject to a word that describes itbe (am, is, are, was, were), seem, become, look, feel
Auxiliary (helping) verbshelp the main verb to make a tense or a questionbe, have, do: *She is reading. They have finished. Do you agree?*
Modal verbsshow how possible, necessary or certain something is (they come before another verb)can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would

Modal verbs often appear in persuasive writing and in reading questions about the writer's purpose:

Modal verbMeaningExample
mustit is necessary (very strong)We must protect our parks.
shouldadvice: it is a good ideaYou should drink more water.
can / couldability or possibilityAnyone can join the club. It could rain later.
may / mightit is possible but not certainThe trip might be cancelled.
will / wouldfuture, certainty or a polite requestI will help. Would you open the door?

The infinitive is the basic form of a verb, usually with to in front: *to run, to be, to discover, to help*. Checkpoint can ask you to find or copy an infinitive. Example: *She wanted to win the race.* The infinitive often shows purpose (why someone does something): *He saved his money to buy a bicycle.*

Infinitive or preposition?

to + verb = infinitive (*to swim, to explore*). to + noun = preposition (*to the beach, to school*). So in "We went to the market to buy fruit", the first *to* is a preposition and *to buy* is an infinitive.

Other verb forms you will meet:

  • Regular verbs add -ed in the past: walk → walked, play → played.
  • Irregular verbs change in their own way: go → went → gone, see → saw → seen, write → wrote → written.
  • -ing form (present participle): *running, laughing*. It makes continuous tenses: *They were laughing*.
  • Past participle: *broken, taken, painted*. It is used in the perfect (*has eaten*) and the passive (*The window was broken*).

4. Adjectives

An adjective describes a noun. It tells us what kind, what colour, what size, what something is like. It usually comes before the noun (*a narrow street*) or after a linking verb (*The street was narrow.*).

KindExamples
Describing (quality)ancient, gentle, enormous, nervous, glittering
Colour, size, shapegolden, tiny, round
Feelingsanxious, delighted, furious, proud
Comparative (comparing two)taller, faster, more beautiful, better, worse
Superlative (the most of all)tallest, fastest, most beautiful, best, worst

Rule for comparatives: short adjectives add -er/-est (big → bigger → biggest). Longer adjectives use more/most (careful → more careful → most careful). Some are irregular: good → better → best, bad → worse → worst. Never write *more bigger*.

Why does a writer use a comparative? To compare two things and show the difference clearly: *The new library is larger and brighter than the old one* shows how much better the new one is.

5. Adverbs

An adverb tells us more about a verb (how, when, where, how often), or about an adjective or another adverb (how much). Many adverbs end in -ly, but many important ones do not: *soon, often, never, very, here, well, fast*.

Kind of adverbAnswers the questionExamplesSentence
MannerHow?quickly, silently, carefully, wellThe cat crept silently across the roof.
TimeWhen?now, later, yesterday, soonWe will leave soon.
PlaceWhere?here, outside, everywhere, nearbyThe children played outside.
FrequencyHow often?always, often, sometimes, neverHe never forgets his homework.
DegreeHow much?very, extremely, quite, too, almostThe water was extremely cold.
Trap: -ly words that are adjectives

Not every -ly word is an adverb. *friendly, lovely, lonely, ugly, silly* are adjectives: *a friendly dog* (it describes the noun *dog*). And *fast* or *hard* can be both: *a fast car* (adjective) but *She ran fast* (adverb). Always ask: is it describing a noun (adjective) or a verb/adjective (adverb)?

6. Determiners

A determiner comes before a noun (and before any adjectives) and tells us which one or how many.

KindWordsExample
Articlesa, an, thea lemon, an owl, the moon
Demonstrativesthis, that, these, thosethese shoes
Possessivesmy, your, his, her, its, our, theirtheir garden
Quantifierssome, many, much, few, a few, little, a little, every, each, all, no, several, anyevery pupil, a few apples
Numbersone, two, first, secondthree puppies

a or an? Use an before a vowel sound: *an egg, an hour* (the h is silent). Use a before a consonant sound: *a cup, a university* (it sounds like "you").

few / a few, little / a little: *a few* and *a little* mean "some, enough", but *few* and *little* mean "not enough". *A few friends came* (good), but *Few friends came* (disappointing). Use *many / few* with countable nouns and *much / little* with uncountable nouns.

Determiner or pronoun?

If the word comes before a noun, it is a determiner: *"This bag is heavy."* If it stands alone instead of a noun, it is a pronoun: *"This is heavy."* The same goes for *my* (determiner: *my book*) and *mine* (pronoun: *The book is mine*).

7. Prepositions

A preposition shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and the rest of the sentence: where something is, when something happens, or how. A preposition is always followed by a noun or pronoun: *under the bridge*, *after lunch*, *with her*.

Prepositions of…WordsExamples
Placein, on, at, under, behind, between, beside, above, insideThe keys are under the mat.
Directionto, into, across, through, towards, along, pastShe swam across the pool.
Timebefore, after, during, since, until, at, on, inDuring the storm, the lights went out.
Otherwith, without, by, about, for, like, ofHe wrote with a pencil.
Little words, big rulesTimePlace
inin April, in 2026, in the morningin the room, in Cairo
onon Monday, on 5th May, on my birthdayon the table, on the bus
atat 7 o'clock, at night, at the weekendat the door, at school, at home
Same word, different class

*before, after, since, until* can be prepositions or conjunctions. If a noun follows, it is a preposition: *after lunch*. If a whole clause (with its own verb) follows, it is a conjunction: *after we ate lunch*.

8. Conjunctions

A conjunction joins words, phrases or clauses.

TypeWordsExample
Coordinating conjunctions (join two equal parts) — remember FANBOYSfor, and, nor, but, or, yet, soI was hungry, so I made a sandwich.
Subordinating conjunctions (begin a subordinate clause that cannot stand alone)because, although, when, while, if, unless, until, since, after, before, as, even thoughAlthough it was raining, we played outside.

Conjunctions are explained fully on the Connectives page and in Phrases and clauses, because a subordinating conjunction is how a complex sentence is built.

9. Interjections

An interjection is a short word or phrase that shows a sudden feeling. It is often followed by an exclamation mark: *Ouch! Hooray! Oh no! Wow! Oops!* Interjections appear mostly in speech in stories.

Word class in context: the same word, different jobs

Checkpoint usually asks: *What is the word class of the word X as it is used in this sentence?* You cannot answer from the word alone. You must look at what it is doing in that sentence.

WordSentenceWord classWhy

Four steps to name a word class in context:

  1. Find the word in the sentence and read the whole sentence.
  2. Ask: is it naming something? (noun) Is it an action or state? (verb) Is it describing a noun? (adjective) Is it telling how, when, where, how often about a verb? (adverb)
  3. Look at the neighbours. *The/a/my* before a word suggests a noun. A noun after it suggests a preposition or a determiner. *To* before a verb makes an infinitive.
  4. Write the exact name: "adverb", not "describing word", and "abstract noun", not "noun" if the question asks for an abstract noun.

Practice A: name the word class

Read this short text, then answer the questions.

*Early on Saturday morning, Karim and his sister hurried to the harbour. The fishing boats, which had been out all night, were slowly returning. Karim felt a wave of excitement. "Look! That boat is the biggest," he shouted. His sister wanted to see the fishermen unload their nets.*

  1. What word class is 'hurried'?

    Show answer
    Verb. It is the action that Karim and his sister did.

  2. What word class is 'slowly'?

    Show answer
    Adverb (of manner). It tells us how the boats were returning.

  3. Find an abstract noun in the text.

    Show answer
    excitement. It is a feeling that you cannot touch or see.

  4. Find a relative pronoun.

    Show answer
    which ("The fishing boats, which had been out all night…"). It begins a relative clause that gives more information about the boats.

  5. What word class is 'Saturday'? What type?

    Show answer
    Noun: a proper noun (a day of the week, so it has a capital letter).

  6. What word class is 'biggest'?

    Show answer
    Adjective (superlative). It compares the boat with all the others.

  7. Copy an infinitive from the text.

    Show answer
    to see

  8. What word class is 'Look!' here?

    Show answer
    It is a verb used as a command (an imperative).

  9. What word class is 'to' in 'hurried to the harbour'?

    Show answer
    Preposition, because it is followed by a noun phrase (the harbour), not a verb.

  10. What word class is 'their' in 'their nets'?

    Show answer
    Determiner (possessive). It comes before the noun nets and shows whose nets.

  11. What word class is 'That' in 'That boat is the biggest'?

    Show answer
    Determiner (demonstrative), because it comes before the noun boat.

  12. Find a proper noun that names a person.

    Show answer
    Karim. It is the name of a particular person, so it starts with a capital letter.

Practice B: choose the correct answer

  1. Which word is an abstract noun? (a) window (b) honesty (c) honest (d) teacher

    Show answer
    (b) honesty. (c) honest is an adjective.

  2. Which word is a modal verb? (a) should (b) quickly (c) because (d) under

    Show answer
    (a) should.

  3. In 'The man whose car broke down called for help', what is 'whose'?

    Show answer
    A relative pronoun. It begins a relative clause about the man and shows the car belongs to him.

  4. In 'She sings well', what word class is 'well'?

    Show answer
    Adverb. It tells us how she sings.

  5. In 'a lovely garden', what word class is 'lovely'?

    Show answer
    Adjective. It ends in -ly but it describes the noun garden.

  6. In 'We stayed inside until the rain stopped', what word class is 'until'?

    Show answer
    Conjunction (subordinating), because it is followed by a clause with a verb: the rain stopped.

  7. In 'We stayed inside until lunchtime', what word class is 'until'?

    Show answer
    Preposition, because it is followed by a noun: lunchtime.

  8. Make abstract nouns from: happy, enjoy, child, wise.

    Show answer
    happiness, enjoyment, childhood, wisdom.

  9. Which sentence uses 'that' as a relative pronoun? (a) That was close! (b) I know that you are right. (c) The cake that Gran made was delicious.

    Show answer
    (c). That comes after the noun cake and tells us which cake.

  10. Write the comparative and superlative of: good, careful, hot.

    Show answer
    good → better → best, careful → more careful → most careful, hot → hotter → hottest.

  11. Why might a writer use 'must' rather than 'could' in a leaflet about road safety?

    Show answer
    'Must' shows the action is necessary, not just possible, so the advice sounds serious and urgent and the reader is more likely to obey it.

  12. Underline the determiners: 'Every child brought some bread and a bottle of water.'

    Show answer
    Every, some, a.

Practice C: use word classes in your writing

  1. Improve this sentence with a precise verb and an adverb: 'The dog went across the garden.'

    Show answer
    Example: The dog bounded eagerly across the garden. (bounded = precise verb, eagerly = adverb of manner)

  2. Add two adjectives to: 'The house stood at the end of the road.'

    Show answer
    Example: The crumbling, silent house stood at the end of the road.

  3. Rewrite with a relative clause: 'My uncle lives in Aswan. He is a doctor.'

    Show answer
    My uncle, who lives in Aswan, is a doctor. (or: My uncle, who is a doctor, lives in Aswan.)

  4. Write a sentence with an infinitive that shows purpose.

    Show answer
    Example: Laila got up early to finish her painting before school.
