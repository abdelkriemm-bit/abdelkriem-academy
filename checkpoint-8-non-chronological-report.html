Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Non-Chronological Reports and Informational Texts — Writing Pack

How to write an organised, factual information report for Paper 1: features, subheadings, paragraph-by-paragraph structure, useful phrases, vocabulary and a strong 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

A non-chronological report (also called an informational text or information report) gives the reader organised facts about a topic: an animal, a place, a plant, an invention, a sport, a festival, an object. It is called 'non-chronological' because the information is not written in time order. Instead, it is grouped into sections by theme, each with its own subheading. Paper 1 tasks that ask you to 'inform', 'describe and explain' or write an 'information page/booklet/fact file' are asking for exactly this skill.

1. What is a non-chronological report?

QuestionAnswer
PurposeTo inform — to give clear, accurate, well-organised facts so that the reader understands a topic.
AudienceUsually readers who know little about the topic: visitors, younger students, readers of an information booklet, a school website or an encyclopaedia-style page.
ToneFormal and objective. No personal opinions (unless the task asks for an evaluation section at the end).
Voice and tenseThird person. Mainly the present simple for general facts (Camels store fat in their humps). Past tense only for history. Future forms only for predictions.
Where it appearsPaper 1 Non-fiction, Section B Writing. Information texts are a framework text type. Descriptive/informative tasks (for example, describing and explaining an object, a place or an activity) have been set.
Exam factWhat it means for you
PaperPaper 1 Non-fiction (Stage 8 Progression Test and Checkpoint 0861 have the same format).
Paper length1 hour 10 minutes for the whole paper: Section A Reading (25 marks) + Section B Writing (25 marks).
Writing timeAbout 35 minutes is suggested for Section B (some 2023 papers said 30 minutes).
Number of tasksOne extended writing task. There is a planning space. Planning is not marked, but it saves marks.
This genreAn information text / non-chronological report can appear as the Section B task, or its skills are needed inside other informative tasks (a fact file, an entry for an information booklet, a 'describe and explain' competition entry).

Chronological or non-chronological?

Chronological textsNon-chronological report
OrderTime order: first, next, then, finallyGrouped by topic: appearance, habitat, diet, uses
ExamplesRecount, diary, biography, newspaper report, instructionsInformation report, fact file, encyclopaedia entry

How your report is marked (25 marks)

CategoryMarksBandsWhat the top band needs in THIS genre
Creation of texts (content & purpose)5[1] [2] [3] [4–5]All parts of the task covered with well-chosen, developed facts and examples. Consistently informative and objective. The reader learns something and stays interested.
Vocabulary and language3[1] [2] [3]Precise subject-specific (technical) vocabulary used accurately and explained where needed (habitat, adaptation, species, pollinate).
Structure of texts (text structure)7[1] [2–3] [4–5] [6–7]Clear title, introduction, logical sections with subheadings and topic sentences, links between sections, and a conclusion that sums up — never a list of random facts.
Grammar and punctuation (sentence structure, punctuation & grammar)7[1] [2–3] [4–5] [6–7]Varied sentences (complex sentences with subordinate and relative clauses, passive voice), consistent present tense, accurate commas, colons for lists, brackets/dashes for extra information.
Word structure (spelling)3[1] [2] [3]Technical and ambitious words spelt correctly (environment, temperature, nutritious, approximately).
Marking rules you must know
  • The examiner uses 'best fit': each category is judged on its own, starting from the lowest band.
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. Between 21 and 60 words can score no more than 7 marks in total — so always finish a full piece.
  • Writing the wrong text type is expensive: in recent mark schemes Creation is capped at 1 and Structure is capped (at 3, or 2 if the content is also wrong).
  • Your plan is not marked, but students who plan for 4–5 minutes almost always score higher in Structure.

2. Features of a non-chronological report

FeatureWhy it is usedExample
Clear, specific titleTells the reader exactly what the report coversWeak: Camels. Strong: The Camel: Survivor of the Desert
Introduction / opening general statementHooks the reader and says what the report will coverFound in every Egyptian city, the humble bicycle is… This report will examine…
SubheadingsOrganise the facts into sections by themeHabitat · Appearance · Diet · Threats
Topic sentence at the start of each sectionTells the reader what the paragraph is aboutCamels are perfectly adapted to life in the desert.
Facts, figures and examplesMake the report accurate and informativeA thirsty camel can drink more than 100 litres of water in about ten minutes.
Present simple tenseFacts that are generally trueThe Nile flows north into the Mediterranean.
Third person, formalObjective and impersonal — no I, no youVisitors often notice… / It is estimated that…
Technical vocabularyShows expert knowledgespecies, habitat, nocturnal, irrigation, export
GeneralisationsTalk about the whole group, not one exampleMost, many, some, typically, usually
ComparisonsHelp the reader picture size or scaleabout the height of a six-storey building
Passive voiceFocuses on the topic, sounds formalDates are harvested in autumn.
Interesting fact box (optional)Engages the reader with something surprisingDid you know? A camel's hump stores fat, not water.
ConclusionSums up the key points and the topic's importanceIn summary, the camel remains…
Diagrams/images (optional in real texts)Support the facts visually. Not needed in the exam—
Do not invent impossible facts

In the exam you may not know exact figures. You can use careful language instead of inventing wild numbers: approximately, around, more than, up to, it is estimated that, typically. A believable report scores higher in Creation than one full of obviously false statistics.

3. How to structure a non-chronological report

PartWhat to writeTips
TitleShort, clear and specific, can include a colon and a subtitleThe Red Sea Coral Reef: A World Under the Water
Introduction (no subheading)1) a general statement that hooks the reader. 2) a short definition or classification. 3) what the report will coverDo not give all the details yet — preview them
Section 1 + subheadingWhere it is found / what it is (location, size, classification)Topic sentence, then 3–4 supporting facts
Section 2 + subheadingAppearance / main features / how it worksUse comparisons and precise adjectives
Section 3 + subheadingUses / behaviour / importance to peopleExplain why it matters. Examples
Section 4 + subheadingThreats / challenges / the future (if relevant)Cause and effect connectives
Interesting facts (optional)One or two surprising factsKeep it short — 1–2 sentences
ConclusionSummarise without repeating everything. Restate importance. End with a thought or a look to the futureIn summary… / Looking ahead…

The sections always depend on the task. If the task asks you to explain 'what it is, how it is used and why it is special', those three points become your three subheadings. Choose categories that fit the topic: an animal needs habitat and diet, a festival needs history and traditions, an invention needs how it works and its impact.

Subheading banks (choose 3–4)
  • Animal: Habitat · Appearance · Diet and Hunting · Life Cycle · Threats and Protection
  • Place: Location and Climate · History · Main Attractions · Daily Life · The Future
  • Object or invention: What It Is · How It Works · How It Is Used · Why It Matters
  • Festival or tradition: Origins · Preparations · How It Is Celebrated · Its Meaning Today
  • Sport or hobby: History · Rules and Equipment · Skills Needed · Benefits

How to start: five introductions

  1. General statement + preview: Known for its brilliant colours and extraordinary variety of life, the Red Sea coral reef is one of the most remarkable ecosystems on Earth. This report examines where it is found, the creatures that live there and the dangers it faces.
  2. Classification: The fennec fox is the smallest member of the dog family. It is a nocturnal mammal that lives in the sandy deserts of North Africa, including Egypt's Western Desert.
  3. Surprising fact: Every day, millions of people carry a computer more powerful than the one that guided the first astronauts to the Moon: the smartphone.
  4. Scale: Stretching for more than 6,600 kilometres, the Nile is one of the longest rivers in the world and the lifeline of Egypt.
  5. Definition: Ramadan lanterns, known in Egypt as fanous, are decorative lamps that have become one of the best-known symbols of the holy month.

How to end: five conclusions

  1. Summary: In summary, the camel's hump, feet and eyelashes make it perfectly suited to one of the harshest environments on the planet.
  2. Importance: Overall, it is evident that the Nile is not simply a river but the foundation of Egyptian life, past and present.
  3. Future: Looking ahead, the survival of the reef will depend on careful tourism and stronger protection.
  4. Lasting significance: The enduring popularity of the fanous ensures that this tradition will continue to light up Egyptian streets for generations.
  5. Final interesting fact: Remarkably, this tiny fox can survive for long periods without drinking at all, taking almost all the water it needs from its food.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Introduction

  • Renowned for its [feature], [topic] has fascinated [people] for centuries.
  • As one of the most significant [things] in [place], [topic] plays a vital role in…
  • Covering aspects from [A] to [B], this report will explore…
  • This report examines [topic], including its [section 1], [section 2] and [section 3].

Body sections

  • Situated in [place], [topic] is known for…
  • One of the most notable features of [topic] is…
  • Research indicates that… / It is estimated that…
  • An important aspect to consider is…
  • Another significant feature is… / In addition, …
  • Unlike [other thing], [topic]… / Similarly, …
  • This allows… / As a result, …

Giving facts carefully

  • approximately / around / more than / up to [number]
  • Most [species] typically…
  • [Topic] is commonly found in…
  • It is widely believed that…

Conclusion

  • In summary, …
  • Overall, it is evident that…
  • Looking ahead, …
  • The enduring significance of [topic] ensures that…
  • Given its [quality], [topic] remains…

5. Strong sample answer

Information report: a plant of Egypt459 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school is producing an information booklet for international visitors called 'Discover Egypt'. Write an informative report about one plant, animal or place found in Egypt. You should include: • what it is and where it can be found • its main features • why it is important to people.

The Date Palm: Egypt's Tree of Life

Tall, graceful and remarkably tough, the date palm has been part of Egyptian life for thousands of years. Images of it appear on the walls of ancient tombs, and today it still lines the banks of the Nile, the streets of cities and the edges of desert oases. This report examines where date palms grow, their main features, their many uses and the challenges they face.

Where Date Palms Grow

Date palms thrive in hot, dry climates with plenty of sunshine. Although they need very little rain, they cannot survive without water underground, which is why they are typically found near rivers, wells and oases. In Egypt, they are grown across the Nile Valley and the Delta, as well as in desert oases such as Siwa and Bahariya. Remarkably, Egypt is one of the largest producers of dates in the world.

Appearance and Growth

A mature date palm can grow to about twenty metres in height — roughly the height of a six-storey building — and it has a single, rough trunk covered in the bases of old leaves. Long, feather-shaped fronds spread out from the top like a giant umbrella. One of the most distinctive characteristics of the species is that male and female flowers grow on separate trees. Only female trees produce fruit, so farmers often climb the trunks by hand to pollinate the flowers. A healthy tree can produce more than seventy kilograms of dates each year and may keep fruiting for decades.

Uses and Importance

Almost every part of the palm is useful. The fruit is nutritious: it is rich in natural sugar, fibre and minerals such as potassium. Dates are eaten fresh, dried or pressed into paste for sweets, and many families break their fast with them during Ramadan. In addition, the fronds are woven into baskets and mats, the strong central ribs are made into crates and furniture, and the fibres are twisted into rope. In villages, trunks have traditionally been used as roof beams.

Threats and Protection

Despite its toughness, the date palm is vulnerable. The red palm weevil, a beetle whose larvae feed inside the trunk, can kill a tree before any damage is visible from the outside. Shortages of water and the spread of cities onto farmland are further challenges. As a result, farmers are now encouraged to inspect their trees regularly, and new varieties are being tested in research centres.

In summary, the date palm is far more than a source of sweet fruit; it is a provider of food, materials and income for millions of Egyptians. Given its long history and its ability to survive where few other trees can, it is likely to remain a symbol of Egypt for generations to come.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation 5/5 — all three task bullets are covered under their own subheadings (Where Date Palms Grow. Appearance and Growth. Uses and Importance) and developed with specific facts and examples. An extra section (Threats and Protection) adds depth. The voice is consistently informative and objective, and suits international visitors.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — accurate subject vocabulary (thrive, oases, mature, species, pollinate, varieties, larvae, nutritious) and careful factual language (typically, more than, may keep fruiting) that sounds expert without inventing wild figures.
  • Structure 7/7 — specific title with a colon and subtitle. Introduction with a general statement, background and a preview of the sections. Each section starts with a topic sentence. Facts are grouped by theme, not by time. The conclusion sums up and looks to the future.
  • Structure — cohesion: linking words inside and between sections (Although, In addition, Despite, As a result, In summary) and the introduction's preview matches the order of the subheadings.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — consistent present simple for facts, past only for history. Passive voice (are woven, are being tested, have been used). Relative clauses (which is why…, whose larvae feed…). A pair of dashes for extra information (the building comparison). A colon introducing an explanation (The fruit is nutritious: …). A semicolon joining two related independent clauses in the conclusion. List commas used accurately.
  • Spelling 3/3 — technical and ambitious words spelt correctly: graceful, characteristics, pollinate, nutritious, potassium, vulnerable, varieties.
What makes this sample 'non-chronological'?

Look at the order: location → appearance → uses → threats. None of these follows time. The writer could swap two sections and the report would still make sense — that is the test of a non-chronological text. Notice too that there is no 'I' and no 'you' anywhere in the report.

MistakeWhy it costs marksFix

Using 'I love camels' or 'You should visit'Breaks the objective toneThird person: 'Camels are popular with visitors because…'
Subheadings with only one sentence underneathIdeas not developed (Creation, Structure)Topic sentence + 3–4 supporting facts each
A list of random facts with no linksWeak cohesionUse connectives: in addition, similarly, as a result, however

Checklist before you finish
  • A clear, specific title (try a colon + subtitle).
  • An introduction with a hook, a short definition and a preview of the sections.
  • 3–4 subheadings that match the task bullets, each with a topic sentence.
  • Facts grouped by theme — not in time order.
  • Present simple, third person, formal — no I, no you.
  • At least five technical/topic words, used accurately.
  • Complex sentences, passive voice, and a colon, a pair of dashes or brackets used correctly.
  • A conclusion that sums up the importance of the topic.

Practice tasks

  1. Task 1: Your school website is creating a 'Wildlife of Egypt' page. Write an information report about an animal that lives in Egypt. Include: where it lives, what it looks like and how it is adapted to its environment, any dangers it faces. Write a title, an introduction and a plan with subheadings.

    Show answer
    Title: The Fennec Fox: Small Survivor of the Sahara. Introduction: The fennec fox is the smallest member of the dog family, a nocturnal mammal of North Africa's deserts, including Egypt's Western Desert. This report examines its habitat, its remarkable adaptations and the threats it faces. Subheadings: Habitat (sandy desert, burrows, hot days and cold nights) · Appearance and Adaptations (huge ears release heat and hear prey under the sand, thick fur on the feet, gets water from food) · Diet (insects, small rodents, plants) · Threats (habitat loss, capture for the pet trade). Conclusion: summary + why protecting it matters.

  2. Task 2: Which of these sentences belong in a non-chronological report? Correct the others. (a) I think the pyramids are amazing. (b) The Great Pyramid was built for the pharaoh Khufu. (c) You will love the view from the top. (d) Camels are able to close their nostrils during sandstorms.

    Show answer
    (b) and (d) are suitable (factual, third person). (a) Rewrite: The pyramids attract millions of visitors every year. (c) Rewrite: The view from the plateau is one of the most photographed in Egypt. Both rewrites remove I/you and opinion.

  3. Task 3: Turn this time-ordered note into non-chronological subheadings: 'In the morning bees leave the hive, then they collect nectar, later they make honey, and in winter they stay inside.'

    Show answer
    Possible subheadings: The Hive and the Colony · Collecting Nectar · How Honey Is Made · Surviving the Winter · Why Bees Matter to People. The facts are now grouped by topic rather than by time of day.

  4. Task 4: Improve this weak introduction: 'This report is about the Nile. The Nile is a river. It is long.'

    Show answer
    Stretching for more than 6,600 kilometres, the Nile is one of the longest rivers in the world and has supported life in Egypt for thousands of years. This report examines its course, its importance to farming and transport, and the challenges it faces today. (Hook + classification + preview.)
