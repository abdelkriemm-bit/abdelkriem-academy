Oxford ESL 9280 · Writing

The Writing Paper: Complete Guide

Everything about the OxfordAQA 9280 Writing paper: the four questions, how level-of-response marking works, a 75-minute timing plan, the Lead Examiner's advice and the language toolkit for top marks.

British & IGCSE › Oxford International GCSE English as a Second Language

This page is your map of the OxfordAQA International GCSE English as a Second Language (9280) Writing paper. Read it first. It tells you what the four questions are, how examiners give marks, how to use your 75 minutes, and which language skills earn the language marks. After that, study the pack for each question in exam order: picture description, letter/email, blog/diary, and the longer article or extended piece.

The Writing paper at a glance

ItemWhat you need to know
Component code9280/W (Writing). Some official documents call it Paper 1 and others Paper 2, so the safest name is simply "the Writing paper".
Time1 hour 15 minutes (75 minutes)
Total marks60
Weighting30% of the whole qualification (Reading 60 + Writing 60 + Listening 40 + Speaking 40 = 200 marks)
Questions4 questions. All are compulsory, and each one is longer and worth more than the one before.
DictionaryNot allowed
TopicsQuestions 1-3 are each based on one of the three themes. Question 4 can use one or more themes.
Word rangesFixed every series: 30-50, 50-70, 70-90 and 100-150 words
Grades9-1 (9 is the highest). Grades are set on your total out of 200 for all four papers.
Exam words you must know
  • Content and Communication = WHAT you say: did you answer the task, cover the bullet points, give detail and make your meaning clear?
  • Language = HOW you say it: spelling, punctuation, vocabulary, grammar and sentence variety.
  • Compulsory bullet points = points you MUST include (Questions 2 and 3).
  • Prompts = ideas to help you. They are not compulsory (Question 4).
  • Band / Level = a group of marks with a description, e.g. Level 3 = 5-6 marks.
  • Delay in communication = the reader has to stop and reread because a sentence is unclear.

Good news about the form

Examiners give no marks for layout or text-type features. A letter may have a greeting and a sign-off, a blog may start with a friendly hello, an article may have a heading, but none of these earns marks by itself. What earns marks is: covering the task, writing for the right reader, giving detail, and accurate, varied English. So do not waste time on fancy layout. Spend it on content and checking.

How the marking works: level of response

Your writing is not marked by counting points. It is marked by levels (also called bands). Each question has two grids: one for Content and Communication and one for Language. Each grid has levels, and each level has a short description and a range of marks. The examiner reads your whole answer and then does three things.

  1. Climb the ladder. The examiner starts at the lowest level and asks: "Does the answer do this?" If yes, they move up one level and ask again, until the description matches your answer.
  2. Best fit. Your answer may show some features of two levels. The examiner does not hunt for small faults. They choose the level that describes your answer best overall.
  3. Place the mark inside the level. The examiner then uses the strengths and weaknesses of your answer to choose the exact mark. For example, an answer that is mainly Level 3 with a little Level 4 quality stays in Level 3, but it gets a mark near the top of that level.
QuestionContent levelsLanguage levels
Q1 (9)3 levels: 1-2, 3-4, 5-63 levels: 1, 2, 3 (spelling and punctuation only)
Q2 (12)3 levels: 1-2, 3-4, 5-63 levels: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6
Q3 (16)4 levels: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-84 levels: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-8
Q4 (23)4 levels: 1-2, 3-4, 5-6, 7-85 levels: 1-3, 4-6, 7-9, 10-12, 13-15

In simple words, the top levels always ask for the same things. For content: everything is relevant, every compulsory point is covered, there is real detail, and the message is clear with no confusion. For language: accurate spelling and punctuation, plus (from Q2 onwards) a wide and appropriate variety of vocabulary and sentence structures. In Q4 the very top level also asks for confident complex sentences and well-chosen vocabulary used for effect.

Two rules that cost students the most marks
  • A missing bullet point keeps you out of the top content level in Q2 and Q3, however good the rest is. The examiner ticks the bullets one by one as they read.
  • "Delay" caps your content mark. If the examiner has to reread a sentence to understand it, the answer usually cannot reach the top content level. Clear, correct sentences are worth more than clever but confusing ones.

A worked example of best fit

Imagine a Q3 blog that covers all four bullets. Three bullets have good detail, but the last one gets only five words: "I liked it, it was nice." The meaning is always clear. The examiner climbs the ladder: Level 1 (limited)? It does more than that. Level 2 (satisfactory)? Yes, and more. Level 3 (good, almost always relevant, a range of detail)? Yes. Level 4 (excellent, wide range of detail)? Not quite, because one bullet is thin. Best fit: Level 3, top mark, so 6/8 for content. Two more sentences explaining WHY it was enjoyable would have lifted it to 7 or 8.

Timing plan for 75 minutes

Share your time according to the marks. Q4 is worth 23 marks, more than Q1 and Q2 together, so never leave it until the last ten minutes.

  • Planning means: underline the reader, the form and every bullet point. Number the bullets. Jot 2-3 words of detail next to each one.
  • Do not count every word. Count the words in one line of your handwriting, then multiply by the number of lines. That estimate is good enough.
  • Checking means reading your answer slowly "aloud in your head". You will hear missing words, wrong tenses and missing full stops.
  • If you are fast, use the extra minutes on Q4, not on making Q1 longer.

The three themes

Every question is built on the three specification themes. Q1, Q2 and Q3 each use one theme. Q4 can use any of them. You do not need special knowledge, but you do need the vocabulary to write about each topic quickly.

What the Lead Examiner advises (in short)

In 2026 the Lead Examiner published advice for students taking this paper, and recent examiner reports give the same messages. Here is that advice in our own words.

  1. Layout earns no marks. Differences between a letter, a blog and an article are only on the surface. Greetings, headings and sign-offs are fine, but they do not add marks.
  2. The bullet points in Q2 and Q3 are compulsory. Cover every single one. A missed bullet means "partial coverage" and you cannot reach the top content level.
  3. Get to the point quickly. Skip long social openings in letters ("How are you? How is your family? I hope you are in good health..."). They use your words and earn nothing. Start answering the first bullet in your first or second sentence.
  4. Word counts are a guide, not a rule. There is no penalty for going over or under, and examiners credit every word you write. But stay close to the range: very short answers are thin and lose detail marks, and very long answers waste time and usually contain more mistakes.
  5. Avoid memorised phrases. Ready-made sentences learnt by heart ("In this modern era, technology is a double-edged sword") often do not fit the task. Examiners reward language that fits the reader and the purpose.
  6. Detail usually lives in the last bullet. The first bullets are often simple facts (when, where, who). The last one (why, your opinion) is where you show real detail with reasons and examples.
  7. In Q1, accuracy beats ambition. Only spelling and punctuation are marked for language, so write clear, correct sentences. You do not need to describe everything in the photo.
  8. "Sophisticated" means the right word, not the longest word. Choose vocabulary that fits. Never fill your writing with impressive-sounding words that do not suit the context.

Full-marks checklist for every question

  • I read the task twice and underlined WHO I am writing to, WHAT form it is and WHY I am writing.
  • I numbered every bullet point and ticked each one in my answer (Q2 and Q3).
  • I answered the first bullet in my first or second sentence.
  • I gave a reason or example for the most important bullet (usually the last one).
  • My tone fits the reader: polite and formal for parents or a head teacher, friendly for a friend.
  • Every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark.
  • I used commas correctly and did not join two sentences with only a comma.
  • From Q2 onwards I used a mix of short and longer sentences, including some complex sentences (because, when, although, which, if).
  • I used the right tenses: past for what happened, present for facts, future for plans.
  • I used a range of linking words, not "and" and "then" again and again.
  • My answer is close to the word range.
  • I left time to check spelling, capital letters and verb endings (-s, -ed).

Language toolkit: how to earn the language marks

The language grids reward two things: accuracy (spelling, punctuation, grammar) and variety (different words and sentence structures). In Q1 only accuracy of spelling and punctuation counts. In Q2, Q3 and Q4 you need both. The tools below work for every question.

1. Sentence variety

Sentence typeHow it worksExample
SimpleOne clause (one main verb). Good for clear facts and for impact.The trip was a disaster.
CompoundTwo main clauses joined by and, but, or, soThe bus was late, so we missed the first film.
Complex (subordinate clause)A main clause + a clause starting with because, when, although, if, while, since, untilAlthough it was raining, the match continued.
Complex (relative clause)A clause starting with who, which, where, whose or that adds informationMy uncle, who is a pilot, gave a talk at our school.
Starting with an -ing or -ed phraseA participle phrase at the start, then a commaFeeling nervous, I walked onto the stage.
Question to the readerUseful in blogs, emails and articlesHave you ever tried cooking for your whole family?
The 3-2-1 rule for variety

In every Q2 or Q3 answer, aim for at least 3 complex sentences, 2 different linking words that are not "and" or "then", and 1 short sentence for effect. In Q4, aim for more, and vary how your sentences begin.

2. Tenses that examiners like to see

TenseUse it forExample
Present simpleFacts, habits, opinionsI usually study in the library.
Present continuousActions happening now (very useful in Q1)Two boys are playing chess.
Past simpleFinished events (blogs, diaries, emails)We visited the museum last Friday.
Past continuousBackground to a past eventWhile we were walking home, it started to rain.
Present perfectExperience, or a past action that matters nowI have just finished my exams.
Past perfectAn action before another past actionBy lunchtime, we had collected fifty bags of rubbish.
Future (will / going to)Plans, predictions, promisesI am going to apply for the course next year.
First conditionalReal possibilitiesIf you come, you will love it.
Second conditionalImagined situationsIf I had more time, I would learn to play the oud.
Modal verbsAdvice, possibility, obligationYou should bring a hat. Students must register by Thursday.
PassiveFormal writing, when the action matters more than the personThe event is organised by the student council.

Quick check: test yourself

  1. How many marks are for Language on the whole Writing paper, and how many for Content?

    Show answer
    Language 32, Content and Communication 28. Total 60.

  2. In Q1, what does the Language mark assess?

    Show answer
    Only spelling and punctuation (3 marks). Vocabulary range is not rewarded in Q1, so write simple, accurate English.

  3. A student covers only two of the three bullet points in Q2 but writes beautifully. Can they get 6/6 for content?

    Show answer
    No. Missing a compulsory point is partial coverage, so the answer cannot reach the top content level.

  4. Is there a penalty for writing 95 words in a 70-90 word task?

    Show answer
    No. Word counts are approximate and every word is credited. But keep close to the range: extra words take time and often add mistakes.

  5. Which question should get the most time, and roughly how long?

    Show answer
    Q4 (23 marks): about 26 minutes including planning and checking.

  6. Are the two bullet points in Q4 compulsory?

    Show answer
    No. In Q4 they are prompts to help you. In Q2 and Q3 the bullets are compulsory.

  7. Does a heading on your article or a sign-off on your letter earn a mark?

    Show answer
    No. Layout and text-type features earn no marks by themselves. Marks come from content, clarity, detail, audience awareness and language.

  8. What does "best fit" mean?

    Show answer
    The examiner chooses the level whose description matches the answer best overall, without picking holes in small parts, and then uses the strengths and weaknesses to choose a mark inside that level.
