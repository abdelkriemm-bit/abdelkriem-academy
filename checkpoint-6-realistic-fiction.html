Checkpoint 6 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Realistic Fiction: STEAL and Imagery

How to write a believable realistic story for Paper 2 with the two key tools — STEAL characterisation and imagery — plus a paragraph plan, openings and endings, phrases, vocabulary and a strong 250-350 word sample annotated letter by letter.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is realistic fiction?

Realistic fiction is an invented story that could really happen. There is no magic, no talking animals, no spaceships and no impossible luck. There are only ordinary people in ordinary places (a home, a school, a club, a market, a family trip) who face a problem that you or your friends could really have.

A realistic story does not need a big, dramatic event to be exciting. It needs a believable character, a believable problem and a believable ending. When the examiner finishes your story, you want them to think: “That could happen to anyone.”

Purpose: to entertain the reader and make them feel something: worry, sympathy, relief or pride. Audience: the examiner, who reads lots of stories and rewards the one that feels true. Point of view: first person (I) or third person (he/she), usually in the past tense.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Realistic fiction is written in Paper 2 (Fiction), Section B Writing — 25 marks. The whole paper lasts 1 hour, and about 30 minutes is suggested for the writing.
  • The task does not usually say “write a realistic story”. It gives you a theme or a situation, often linked to the reading extract, and sometimes bullet points telling you what to include. Recent Paper 2 writing tasks have asked for a story opening, a continuation of a story, and a story about a special place.
  • Most of these themes are perfect for realistic fiction, because they are about real feelings in real places. You choose the genre, and realistic fiction is often the safest and strongest choice.

Themes that suit realistic fiction: a mistake you have to put right, losing something important, a first day somewhere new, a competition or a performance, a falling-out with a friend, helping a stranger, a family surprise, keeping a promise, facing a fear, being trusted with an important job.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Your writing is marked in five categories. The examiner reads the whole story and chooses the band that fits it best in each category (we explain the bands in our own words below).

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in this genre
Creation of texts5Everything in the task and every bullet point is covered · the characters feel like real people because they are SHOWN with STEAL · the problem and the ending are believable · the reader cares what happens.
Vocabulary and language3Precise words for feelings, actions and places (crinkling, scraped, steadily, trembling) and imagery that creates a mood — chosen for effect, not random “big words”.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7A clear shape: hook → character and setting → problem → rising tension → climax → resolution. New paragraphs for a new time, place, speaker or mood · the ending links back to the start.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation & grammar)7A mix of simple, compound and complex sentences · speech punctuated correctly on a new line for each speaker · a range of punctuation used accurately (commas, apostrophes, colon, semicolon, dash, ellipsis).
Word structure (spelling)3Ambitious words spelt correctly (believe, embarrassed, disappeared, immediately, necessary) · no slips with their/there/they’re or its/it’s.
Total25Marked by best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and chooses the band that describes it best in each row.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0.
  • An answer of 21-60 words can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example, a diary or a letter when the task asks for a story), Creation of texts can be capped at 1 and Structure of texts at 3 (sometimes 2).
  • A lovely story that ignores the task or one of its bullet points loses Creation marks. Tick each bullet on your plan.

2. Features of realistic fiction

FeatureWhat it meansExample
Realistic settingA familiar, everyday place you can describe with your sensesThe classroom smelt of pencil shavings and orange peel.
Everyday problemSomething that really happens to children your agea lost key, a broken promise, stage fright, a new school
Believable eventsNo magic and no lucky coincidence that solves everythingKarim does not find the watch by magic — he tells the truth and asks for help.
Relatable themeFriendship, family, honesty, courage, growing upTelling the truth is harder than hiding it.
Character growthThe main character learns something, changes or makes a choiceAt the start she hides. At the end she speaks up.
Natural dialogueShort lines that sound like real people“Are you okay?” “Not really.”
STEAL characterisationShow what people are like through Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions and LooksHe chewed his lip until it hurt.
ImagerySensory and figurative language that puts the reader inside the sceneDry leaves scraped across the concrete like tiny claws.
Clear sequenceBeginning, middle and end, and each event leads to the nextloss → search → decision → help → ending
Believable resolutionThe problem is solved, or partly solved, in a way that could really happenThe caretaker finds the watch by the goalpost.

Layout features every story needs: a short title that makes the reader curious, paragraphs (with a one-line paragraph for a big moment), a new line for each new speaker, the past tense all the way through and one point of view.

The two tools of realistic fiction

In realistic fiction there is no dragon or magic spell to impress the reader. Everything depends on two tools: STEAL characterisation (to make your people real) and imagery (to make your places and feelings real). These are the two tools your teacher wants you to use in every realistic story.

3. Tool 1: STEAL characterisation

There are two ways to show a character. Telling (direct characterisation) gives the reader the answer: “Grandad was kind.” Showing (indirect characterisation) gives the reader clues, and the reader works it out: “Grandad’s eyes crinkled when he smiled, and he always saved the biggest piece of cake for me.” Showing is almost always stronger. We show characters with STEAL.

LetterAsk yourselfTelling (weak)Showing with STEAL (strong)
S — SpeechWhat does the character say, and HOW (quietly, sharply, with a joke)?Mum was worried.“Where have you been? I’ve called you ten times!” Mum said, her voice shaking.
T — ThoughtsWhat do they think, worry about, hope or remember?Lina was nervous.What if I forget my lines? What if everyone laughs?
E — Effect on othersHow do other people react to them?Mr Adel was strict.When Mr Adel walked in, the whole class stopped talking at once.
A — ActionsWhat do they do? Think about habits and body language.Omar was angry.Omar threw his bag on the floor and slammed the door.
L — LooksFace, clothes, hair, expression, the way they standGrandad was old.Grandad’s hair was as white as cotton, and his glasses sat on the end of his nose.
  • You do not need all five letters for every character. A small character may need only one or two (Looks + Effect on others). Give your main character at least three, especially Thoughts and Actions, because they show feelings.
  • Use STEAL to show feelings. Do not write “I was scared.” Show it: Thoughts (What if…?), Actions (I gripped the desk), Looks (my face went pale).
  • Spread STEAL through the story. Do not stop for a long paragraph of description. Add a Looks detail when someone first appears, a line of Speech when they act, and a Thought at the most worrying moment.
  • Effect on others is the letter most students forget — and it impresses the examiner. One reaction (a silence, a laugh, someone leaning forward) tells the reader a lot about a character.

S — Speech

  • “[Short line],” [Name] said, without looking up.
  • “[Line],” [he/she] whispered, so quietly that I almost missed it.
  • “[Question]?” [Name] asked, already knowing the answer.

T — Thoughts

  • What if [worry]? What if [bigger worry]?
  • A little voice inside me whispered that [doubt].
  • I kept telling myself that [it was fine], but I did not believe it.

E — Effect on others

  • When [Name] spoke, even [the noisiest boy] stopped [talking].
  • The whole room went quiet when [Name] [action].
  • [Name] put down [his/her] pen the moment [he/she] saw my face.

A — Actions

  • [Name] [gripped / twisted / tapped] [object] until [his/her] knuckles turned white.
  • Instead of [expected action], [Name] [surprising action].
  • [He/She] [habit], the way [he/she] always did when [he/she] was [feeling].

L — Looks

  • The first thing anyone noticed about [Name] was [detail].
  • [His/Her] [shoes / bag / hands], [worn / spotless / cracked], told me everything about [him/her].
  • [Name]’s face went [pale / red] and [his/her] eyes [filled with tears / narrowed].

4. Tool 2: Imagery

Imagery is descriptive language that helps the reader see, hear, smell, taste or feel what is happening, as if they were inside the story. It has two parts: sensory details (the five senses) and figurative language (simile, metaphor, personification).

Type of imagerySenseRealistic example
VisualsightThe goalposts threw long, thin shadows across the playground.
AuditoryhearingThe bus doors hissed open.
OlfactorysmellThe kitchen smelt of cumin and warm bread.
GustatorytasteThe medicine tasted bitter, like burnt lemons.
TactiletouchThe metal railing was cold and wet under my fingers.
Similea comparison using like or asMy legs felt as heavy as sandbags.
Metaphorsays one thing IS anotherThe empty hall was a cave of silence.
Personificationgives a human quality to a thingThe old fan groaned above our heads.
  • Use two or three senses in each scene, not all five at once. Sound and smell are the most forgotten, and they make a place feel real immediately.
  • Make imagery do a job. Use it to create a mood or to show a feeling: an empty, silent playground shows how lonely and worried the character feels.
  • One strong simile is better than three weak ones. Avoid tired clichés (as white as snow, as fast as lightning). Choose a comparison that fits your setting.
STEAL and imagery together

The best sentences use both tools at once: “Karim walked to the office, his legs as heavy as sandbags.” This is Actions (walked to the office) + a simile (as heavy as sandbags) that shows his fear. Try to write two or three sentences like this in your story.

5. How to structure a realistic story

First, plan with G.C.S.P.P. for 3-4 minutes:

  1. G — Genre: realistic fiction. Write the problem in one line (A boy loses his grandfather’s watch at school).
  2. C — Characters: 2-3 only. For each one, note two STEAL details (one Looks and one Speech or Action).
  3. S — Setting: one or two everyday places. Note three sensory details (sight, sound, smell).
  4. P — Point of view: first person (I) is great for Thoughts, and third person (he/she) is fine too. Keep the past tense. If you continue an extract, keep its point of view.
  5. P — Plot: six short paragraph notes following the plan below. Tick each bullet point from the task.
StageWhat to writeSTEAL / imagery focus
1. HookStart in the middle of a moment: a line of speech, an action or a surprising short sentence.Actions or Speech
2. Character and settingIntroduce the main character and the place. Give a little background.Looks + one or two senses
3. The problemSomething goes wrong. Show how the character feels.Thoughts (What if…?) + Actions
4. Rising actionThe character tries to fix it and meets a difficulty or a choice.Imagery for mood + Speech
5. ClimaxThe most important moment: a brave choice, a confession, a risk. Use a short paragraph.Actions + Effect on others
6. ResolutionThe problem is solved (or partly solved) in a believable way. Show what the character has learned. Link back to the opening.Speech or Thoughts to close

How to start your story

Never start with “One day I woke up and brushed my teeth.” Start close to the problem. Here are five kinds of opening:

  • A short, shocking sentence: The watch was gone.
  • Speech: “You’re not going to tell Dad, are you?” my sister whispered.
  • Action: Laila sprinted through the school gate just as the bell stopped ringing.
  • A thought or question: How do you tell your best friend that you broke her favourite thing?
  • Setting with a mood: The hospital corridor smelt of soap and worry, and the clock above the door ticked far too loudly.

How to end your story

Do not end with “Then I woke up and it was all a dream.” That cancels the whole story. Try one of these:

  • Link back to the opening (circular ending): The story began with “The watch was gone.” It can end with the watch ticking on the character’s wrist.
  • A lesson learned, shown not preached: From then on, I always told the truth first — even when it was hard.
  • A final line of speech: “Same time next week?” Coach asked. This time, I nodded.
  • A final image: As we drove away, the old house grew smaller and smaller until it was just a white dot between the palm trees.
  • A feeling shown with STEAL: I smiled all the way home, and for once I did not care who saw me.

Timing: 30 minutes for the writing

Paper 2 (Fiction) lasts 1 hour: Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks). About 30 minutes is suggested for each section. Use your 30 writing minutes like this:

MinutesWhat to do
0-4Read the task twice and underline the key words and bullet points. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) in the planning space.
4-26Write the story paragraph by paragraph, following your plan. Many strong answers are about 250-350 words: long enough to develop every stage, short enough to finish and check.
26-30Check: spelling of ambitious words, full stops and capital letters, speech punctuation, tense (past tense all the way), and that every bullet point is answered.

6. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Openings

  • I never imagined that [an ordinary Tuesday / one small mistake] could change everything.
  • “[Look out! / Wait!]” [someone] shouted, but it was already too late.
  • That was the day [something] changed, though at the time [character] had no idea.

Describing the setting

  • The air smelt of [detail], mixed with [detail].
  • In the distance, [character] could hear [sound].
  • [Place] was [empty / crowded], and [detail of sight or sound].

Building tension

  • [Character]’s heart pounded harder with every [step / second].
  • The more [character] tried to [fix it], the worse things became.
  • “Did you [hear / see] that?” [character] asked, [his/her] voice breaking the silence.

Climax

  • [Character] took a deep breath and [brave action], even though [his/her] hands were shaking.
  • It was now or never.
  • In that moment, everything seemed to slow down.

Resolution

  • In the end, [character] realised that [lesson].
  • A quiet smile spread across [character]’s face.
  • Things were not perfect, but they were better.

7. Strong sample answer

Read the task, then the story. The notes underneath show exactly where each STEAL letter, each type of imagery and each mark is earned. The numbers in brackets are paragraph numbers.

Realistic fiction — Grandad’s Watch344 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: In the extract you have read, a character is given an important responsibility. Write a story about a character who is trusted with something precious and then loses it. In your story you should: introduce the character and the precious thing, describe how the character feels when it goes missing, and show what the character decides to do and how the story ends.

The watch was gone. Karim patted his blazer pocket once, twice, three times, but his fingers found only a pencil sharpener and a crumpled tissue.

That morning, Grandad had pressed it into his palm at breakfast. It was old and heavy, with a scratched silver face and a leather strap that smelt of soap and strong coffee. “Bring it back safely,” Grandad had said, his eyes crinkling behind his thick glasses. “It has been ticking since before your father was born.”

Karim had shown it to the whole class in his history talk. Even Ali, who never listened to anyone, had leaned forward to hear it tick. Then, at break, Karim had played football in the dusty corner of the playground… and now it had disappeared.

After school, the playground was empty. Dry leaves scraped across the concrete like tiny claws, and the goalposts threw long, thin shadows. Karim searched under every bench and behind every bin. What would Grandad say? What if he never trusted him again?

He could say nothing. He could even pretend that someone had stolen it. Karim chewed his lip until it hurt. Then he picked up his bag and walked to Mr Fathy’s office, his legs as heavy as sandbags.

Mr Fathy put down his pen the moment he saw Karim’s pale face.

“Sir, I’ve lost my grandad’s watch,” Karim said, his voice cracking.

Mr Fathy listened without interrupting; then he picked up the phone. “Telling the truth takes more courage than scoring a goal,” he said quietly.

Ten minutes later, the caretaker knocked on the door. In his rough, oil-stained hand lay the watch: its strap was muddy, but it was still ticking steadily. He had found it beside the goalpost.

That evening, Karim told Grandad everything: the football, the search and the office. Grandad was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed, a warm, rumbling laugh that filled the whole kitchen, and fastened the watch round Karim’s wrist.

“Keep it until tomorrow,” he said. “You brought back something more precious than a watch – the truth.”

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — all three bullet points are answered: the character and the precious thing (paragraphs 1-3) · his feelings when it is missing (paragraphs 4-5) · his decision and the ending (paragraphs 5-11). Every event could really happen: the watch is found because Karim tells the truth and an adult helps, not by magic or luck.
  • STEAL — S (Speech): Grandad’s gentle warning “Bring it back safely” (2) shows how much the watch means to him · Karim’s confession “Sir, I’ve lost my grandad’s watch” with his voice cracking (7) shows his fear and honesty · Mr Fathy’s calm line (8) shows his wisdom without any adjective.
  • STEAL — T (Thoughts): the questions “What would Grandad say? What if he never trusted him again?” (4) and the two choices he considers, saying nothing or pretending it was stolen (5), put the reader inside Karim’s head.
  • STEAL — E (Effect on others): Ali, “who never listened to anyone”, leans forward to hear the watch tick (3) · Mr Fathy puts down his pen the moment he sees Karim’s face (6) · Grandad’s silence and then his warm laugh (10) show how he reacts to Karim’s honesty.
  • STEAL — A (Actions): patting his pocket “once, twice, three times” (1), searching under every bench (4), chewing his lip until it hurt (5), walking to the office (5) — every feeling is shown through actions, never told.
  • STEAL — L (Looks): Grandad’s eyes “crinkling behind his thick glasses” (2) · Karim’s “pale face” (6) · the caretaker’s “rough, oil-stained hand” (9), which tells us he is a hard worker in only three words.
  • Imagery — sight (scratched silver face, long thin shadows), sound (the tick, leaves scraping, the rumbling laugh), smell (soap and strong coffee), touch (heavy watch pressed into his palm, rough hand). Similes: leaves “like tiny claws” (the lonely, scary mood) and legs “as heavy as sandbags” (his fear).
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — precise words chosen for effect: crumpled, crinkling, scraped, cracking, oil-stained, steadily, rumbling, precious. The empty playground creates a worried mood, and the warm kitchen creates a happy one.
  • Structure 7/7 — hook with a short shocking sentence (1) → flashback to the morning (2-3) → the problem and the search (4) → the choice (5) → climax: the confession, with a one-line paragraph for impact (6-7) → resolution (8-11). The ending links back to the opening: the watch that was “gone” is now ticking on Karim’s wrist.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — sentences are varied: very short for impact (“The watch was gone.”), compound (“its strap was muddy, but it was still ticking steadily”), complex (“Even Ali, who never listened to anyone, had leaned forward…”). The past perfect (“had pressed”, “had played”) is used correctly for the flashback. Punctuation as taught: a pair of commas for extra information (“Even Ali, who never listened to anyone, had leaned…”) · an ellipsis for a suspense pause (“…and now it had disappeared”) · a semicolon joining two related sentences (“Mr Fathy listened without interrupting; then he picked up the phone”) · a colon for an explanation (“lay the watch: its strap was muddy…”) and for a list (“everything: the football, the search and the office”) · a single dash for emphasis (“– the truth”) · apostrophes for possession (Karim’s, grandad’s, Fathy’s) and contraction (I’ve) · speech on new lines with correct punctuation.
  • Spelling 3/3 — ambitious words are spelt correctly: crumpled, crinkling, disappeared, interrupting, courage, caretaker, steadily, precious.

8. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
Telling feelings: “I was very sad and scared.”Creation and Vocabulary stay in a middle bandShow it with STEAL: a Thought (What if…?), an Action (I chewed my lip), Looks (my face went pale).
A list of looks: “He was tall. He had black hair. He wore a blue shirt.”It reads like an ID card and shows no personalityChoose ONE detail that shows character: his rough, oil-stained hands.
Impossible events: winning a million pounds, a superhero arrivingIt is not realistic any more, and the reader stops believingKeep the problem and the solution small and real: one apology, one brave sentence.
Long speechesReal people do not talk in speeches; the story slows downShort lines, with an action in between.
No setting at allThe story floats in empty spaceAdd two or three sensory details in the first half.
“…and then I woke up. It was a dream.”It cancels the whole storyEnd with a real, believable change.
Comma splices: “I was tired, I kept walking.”Grammar & punctuation marksUse a full stop or a connective: “I was tired, but I kept walking.” A semicolon can join two closely related sentences: “The bell rang; the hall emptied.”
Changing tense: “I ran to the door and I see my friend.”Grammar & punctuation marksStay in the past tense: I ran… and I saw…
Checklist before you hand in
  • My story answers the task and EVERY bullet point.
  • Everything that happens could happen in real life.
  • My main character is shown with at least three STEAL letters, including Thoughts and Actions.
  • There is at least one moment of Effect on others (how people react).
  • I used imagery from at least three senses, plus one strong simile, metaphor or personification.
  • My paragraphs follow the shape: hook → problem → rising action → climax → resolution, with one short paragraph for impact.
  • Speech is on new lines with correct punctuation, and I used a colon, a dash or an ellipsis correctly.
  • My ending shows what the character has learned and links back to the opening.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: Write a story about a character who has to do something new for the first time. In your story you should: introduce the character and what they have to do, describe how they feel before and during the new experience and show how the experience changes them. Write a short G.C.S.P.P. plan with STEAL notes.

    Show answer
    G: realistic fiction — Malak, 11, must read her poem aloud in the school assembly. C: Malak — L: hair tied back too tightly, paper shaking in her hand · T: “What if my voice disappears?” · A: practises in the bathroom mirror. Her little brother Hamza — S: “You read it to me every night. Just pretend I’m there.” Ms Rania, the teacher — E: the hall goes quiet when she raises her hand.   2. Rewrite this sentence using THREE STEAL letters instead of telling: “My uncle was a kind man and everyone liked him.”

    Show answer
    Example: Uncle Sherif always kept a bag of sweets in his jacket pocket for the neighbours’ children (Actions + Looks). “Only one each, or your mothers will blame me,” he would say with a wink (Speech) — and the children would run to meet him the moment his old car turned into our street (Effect on others).

  3. Add imagery from three senses to this weak setting: “The beach was nice.”

    Show answer
    Example: Waves hissed over the pebbles and pulled back with a soft rattle (sound). The sand was so hot that it burned the soles of my feet (touch), and the air smelt of salt and grilled corn (smell). Far out, the sea glittered like a carpet of broken mirrors (sight + simile).

  4. Which STEAL letter is used in each sentence? (a) “Not again,” she sighed. (b) The whole class went silent when he stood up. (c) Was it my fault? I kept asking myself. (d) He tugged at his collar and stared at the floor. (e) Her school shoes were scuffed grey at the toes.

    Show answer
    (a) Speech. (b) Effect on others. (c) Thoughts. (d) Actions. (e) Looks.

  5. This ending is weak: “Then I woke up and it was all a dream. The end.” Write a better ending for the story “Grandad’s Watch” (or your own story) that links back to the opening.

    Show answer
    Example: The next morning, Karim gave the watch back to Grandad. As it ticked against his ear, Grandad smiled. “Next time,” he said, “I will give you something even more precious.” Karim did not ask what it was. He already knew: his trust.
