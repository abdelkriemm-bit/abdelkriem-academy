Oxford ESL 9280 · Listening

The Listening Paper (9280/L)

Everything about the 45-minute Listening paper: the five tasks, how answers are marked, how to use the reading time and the two listenings, the traps examiners set, and a weekly practice plan.

British & IGCSE › Oxford International GCSE English as a Second Language

The Listening paper (9280/L) is the shortest paper in the Oxford International GCSE ESL exam, but it is worth 20% of your final grade. Many students lose easy marks here, not because their English is weak, but because they do not know how the paper works: how long they have to read, what to do on the first and second listening, how answers are marked and where the traps are. This page explains all of that, step by step. When you finish it, go to the next page (Listening: task by task) and practise each of the five tasks with full answers.

The paper in one line

45 minutes (including 5 minutes' reading time) · 40 marks · 20% of the qualification · 5 tasks × 8 marks · every task is played twice · no dictionary.

1. The Listening paper at a glance

ItemWhat you need to know
Component9280/L Listening (all four components — Reading, Writing, Listening and Speaking — are taken in the same exam series)
Time45 minutes in total. This includes 5 minutes of reading time before the recording starts.
Marks40 marks: 5 tasks, each worth 8 marks
Weighting20% of the whole qualification (the other papers: Reading 30%, Writing 30%, Speaking 20%)
RecordingsMonologues (one speaker) and dialogues (two speakers), linked to the three themes of the course
How many times?You hear each task twice.
DifficultyThe tasks get harder as the paper goes on: the recordings get longer and the questions ask more about opinions and meaning.
Language of answersYou answer in English. No dictionary is allowed.
MarkingPoint-based: one correct point = one mark. You get the mark when your answer shows understanding, even if it has small language errors.

The recordings are on the same three themes as the rest of the course: Identity and culture, Local, national, international and global areas of interest, and Current and future study and employment. So the vocabulary you learn for speaking and writing (family, technology, free time, your town, healthy living, the environment, travel, school, jobs) is also your listening vocabulary.

2. The five tasks

Learn this table. If you know what each task looks like before you walk into the exam, you will not waste any of the reading time trying to understand the instructions.

TaskQuestion typeWhat you hearLength of each part (segment)MarksWhat it mainly tests
1Multiple choice (write the letter in the box)Short monologues on everyday topics, by several different speakersAbout 30-50 words8L1: picking out key details
2Open questions (short written answers)A dialogue, e.g. a radio interview or two people presentingAbout 30-50 words8L2, L3: main points and putting information together
3Gap-fill: completing notesA monologue, e.g. a talk or someone telling the story of their lifeAbout 40-80 words8L3: understanding and collating information
4Open questions and collation (lists of points)A dialogue, e.g. a radio interview or a conversation between two peopleAbout 50-100 words8L1, L3: details and gathering several points
5Multiple choice and open questionsA monologue, e.g. a travel podcastAbout 50-100 words8L1, L2, L4: purpose, attitudes, feelings and implied meaning

A segment is one short piece of the recording that matches one question (or one group of questions). The recording is divided into segments, and each segment is usually followed by its questions in the same order. This is very helpful: the answers come in order.

3. What the examiners are testing (L1-L4)

StrandIn simple wordsWhere you meet it mostTypical question
L1Catch key details (a place, a time, a number, a reason)Tasks 1, 4, 5At what time does the school trip leave?
L2Understand the main message and key points, with some harder languageTasks 2, 5What is the overall message of the talk?
L3Understand and collect (collate) information from different places in a longer recordingTasks 2, 3, 4List three things she does to prepare for her exams.
L4Recognise purpose, implied meaning, attitudes and feelingsTask 5How does the speaker feel about…? Explain in your own words what the phrase … means.
What this means for you

Tasks 1-3 reward careful listening for facts. Tasks 4-5 reward thinking: why the speaker says something, what they really feel, and what a phrase means. Do not copy a long sentence you heard in Task 5 and hope it is right. Show that you understood it.

4. How your answers are marked

The Listening paper is point-based. Each correct piece of information gets a mark. This is very different from Writing, where your language is marked. In Listening, you are marked on understanding.

  • Spelling: small spelling mistakes do not lose marks if the meaning is still clear and the word would sound right if read aloud. For example, *restorant* for *restaurant* or *vegtables* for *vegetables* is accepted. But if the spelling makes a different word (*fourteen* when the answer is *forty*, *dessert* when the answer is *desert*), the meaning has changed and you lose the mark.
  • Grammar: a wrong tense or a missing article does not lose the mark if your meaning is clear. *He go to gym every day* still shows understanding.
  • But if your sentence is so broken that it does not make sense, or it says something different from the recording, it gets no mark.
  • Number of answers: if a question asks for two things and gives you two numbered lines, write one answer on each line. Extra answers do not earn extra marks, and a wrong extra answer can cost you a mark.
  • Space: write your final answer on the lines given. Anything squeezed in under the lines or in the margins may not be marked. If you need more room, use the extra pages at the end of the booklet and write the question number.
  • Changing your mind: cross out the old answer with one clean line and write the new one clearly. Never leave two answers for the examiner to choose from.
Do not write a paragraph

Most open answers need only a few words: *a science club*, *because it was too expensive*, *he lost his phone*. Long answers waste time during the second listening and can include wrong information that cancels your mark.

5. Using the 5 minutes' reading time

Before the recording starts, you get 5 minutes to read the whole question paper. You may write notes on the paper. There is also a short pause before each task to read its questions again. Use the 5 minutes like this:

  1. Minute 1: Look at all five tasks quickly. Read each task's introduction line (for example: *Two friends are talking about a school trip*). This tells you the topic, the speakers and the type of recording.
  2. Minutes 2-3: Underline the key word in every question. The key word is the question word (*when, why, how many, which*) plus the main noun or verb (*cost, problem, advice, feel*). If you underline *least popular*, you will not choose the most popular one by mistake.
  3. Minute 4: Predict the answer type. Next to each question, write a tiny note: N (a number), D (a day or date), P (a place), V (a verb), Adj (a feeling word), R (a reason). For gap-fills, read the words before and after the gap and decide what kind of word fits.
  4. Minute 5: Read the multiple-choice options and spot the differences. If the three options are *by bus with friends / by bus alone / on foot alone*, the differences are how and with whom. That is exactly what you must listen for.
  5. Last seconds: Go back to Task 1. Have your pen ready and your eyes on question 1.

6. First listening vs second listening

You hear every task twice. Good students use the two listenings for two different jobs.

First listeningSecond listening
Main jobFind the answers and write them quickly in short formCheck every answer and fill any gaps
What to writeA short note or the letter: even a first letter or a number is enough to remind youComplete, clear answers on the lines. Cross out what is wrong
Where to focusFollow the questions in order; listen for the key words you underlined and their synonymsListen especially to the parts you were not sure about and to words like *but, actually, no, in fact*
If you miss somethingDo not stop. Move to the next question immediately: you will hear it againNow give it your full attention. If you still do not know, make a sensible guess
DangerPanicking because you did not understand one wordChanging a correct answer without a clear reason
Never leave a box empty

There is no penalty for a wrong answer. If you are not sure after the second listening, choose the most likely letter or write your best guess. An empty space is always zero.

7. Writing short answers that get the mark

  • Answer the exact question. If the question asks *why*, give a reason (*because… / to…*). If it asks *what does she do*, give a verb (*walks the dog*). Some answers need a verb form: a noun alone may not be enough.
  • Keep the key detail. *A bike* and *a new bike* may be different answers if the speaker said the old bike was stolen. Keep words that change the meaning: *not, never, only, more, less, before, after*.
  • Use the speaker's words or your own: both are fine for detail questions. But in Task 5, when you are asked to explain a phrase *in your own words*, you must not repeat the phrase.
  • Numbers: write them as digits (*15*, *£40*, *7:45*). It is quicker and clearer than words.
  • One idea per line. In list questions, do not put two ideas on one line and leave the next line empty.
QuestionSpeaker saysAnswer that scoresAnswer that does not score, and why
Why did Karim stop playing football?"I loved football, but when I started my new school the training was on Thursdays, the same day as my maths lessons."The training was on the same day as his maths lessons.*He started a new school.* True, but it is not the reason he stopped.
What does Sara do every morning before school?"I used to go running, but now I just walk the dog before school."She walks the dog.*She goes running.* This is a past habit: the trap is *used to*.
How much is a student ticket?"Adults pay sixteen pounds, but students pay only six."£6*£16*. This is the adult price.

8. Multiple choice: writing letters in boxes

Since November 2021, multiple-choice questions on this paper ask you to write the letter (A, B or C) in a box, instead of ticking. The number of letters you write must match the marks for the question. Write capital letters clearly: a lazy A can look like an H, and an unclear B can look like a D.

Worked example. Read this short transcript (it is original and only for practice) and answer the question.

Transcript (practice)

"Our school library is open every day except Friday. Most students come in the morning, but honestly, the best time to find a quiet seat is after lunch — the morning crowd has gone by then, and the after-school clubs haven't started yet."

QuestionOptionsAnswerWhy
When is the best time to find a quiet seat in the library?A In the morning · B After lunch · C After schoolB*Morning* is mentioned, but only because it is crowded. *After school* is mentioned, but clubs are starting then. The speaker says *the best time … is after lunch*. A and C are distractors.

9. Distractors: how the recording tries to trick you

A distractor is a wrong option that you will actually hear in the recording. The examiners do this on purpose to check that you really understand. In almost every multiple-choice question, you will hear more than one of the options. Here are the most common tricks:

Type of trapExample of what you hearWhat it is testing
Mentioned but rejected"I thought about going to Alexandria, but in the end we went to Hurghada."Listen for the final decision, not the first idea.
Past vs present vs future"I used to cycle to school. Now I take the bus, and next year I'm hoping to walk with my friends."Check the tense in the question: *does / did / will*.
Correction"The match is on Saturday — oh no, sorry, it's been moved to Sunday."The second piece of information replaces the first.
Another person"My brother is crazy about basketball. Me? I prefer swimming."The question asks about the speaker, not the brother.
Most / least / not"Many students enjoy comedies. Fewer like horror films, and almost nobody chooses documentaries."Underline *least* or *most* in the question before you listen.
Not this, but that"It isn't the price that worries me. It's the distance."The negative cancels the first idea.
Same word, different meaningThe option says *a long walk*, but the speaker says *the walk wasn't long at all*.Hearing the same words does not make the option correct.
Signal words that change the answer

but · however · actually · in fact · in the end · instead · although · used to · not … but · sorry, I mean · on second thoughts · the only problem is. When you hear one of these, the answer often comes right after it.

10. Numbers, dates, times and names

Numbers are one of the hardest parts of any listening exam. Practise these pairs until you can hear the difference instantly.

TrapHow to hear the differenceExample
13 / 30, 14 / 40, 15 / 50, 16 / 60, 17 / 70, 18 / 80, 19 / 90The *-teen* numbers are stressed at the end (fif-TEEN) and the *n* sound is clear. The *-ty* numbers are stressed at the start (FIF-ty)."It costs fif-TEEN pounds" = £15
PercentagesWrite the number with %. Listen for *per cent* after the number."Forty per cent of students cycle to school" = 40%
Prices*Two fifty* = 2.50, not 250. *A pound fifty* = £1.50."Tickets are three fifty each" = 3.50
Times*Quarter past seven* = 7:15 · *quarter to seven* = 6:45 · *half seven* = 7:30 (British English)."We meet at half eight" = 8:30
DatesBritish English says *the third of May* or *May the third*. Listen for *-th*, *-st*, *-nd*, *-rd*."On the twenty-first" = 21st
Small sumsSometimes you must do easy maths: "I bought twelve eggs, but three broke on the way home." How many eggs were not broken? 9.Write the final number the question asks for.
Names and placesIf a name is unusual, it is often spelt out, or it is a common word. Write what you hear; spelling is not marked if the meaning is clear."Our teacher, Mr Harb — H-A-R-B"
Phone numbers*Double five* = 55, and *oh* = 0."Oh-one-double-two" = 0122

  1. Practice 1. You hear: "Last year the club had thirty members. This year we have fourteen more." How many members does the club have this year?

    Show answer
    44. 30 + 14 = 44. The trap is to write 30 or 14, or to confuse *fourteen* with *forty*.

  2. Practice 2. You hear: "The coach was supposed to leave at a quarter to nine, but it was twenty minutes late." What time did the coach leave?

    Show answer
    9:05. A quarter to nine is 8:45; twenty minutes later is 9:05. 8:45 is the planned time: a distractor.

  3. Practice 3. You hear: "I wanted the blue trainers, but they only had them in white, so I ended up buying black ones from another shop." What colour trainers did the speaker buy? A Blue · B White · C Black

    Show answer
    C (Black). Blue was wanted, white was available in the first shop, but *ended up buying* tells you the final action: black.

  4. Practice 4. You hear: "People think I'm a morning person, but actually I study best late at night, when the house is quiet." When does the speaker study best?

    Show answer
    Late at night (when the house is quiet). *Actually* corrects the idea of the morning.

  5. Practice 5. You hear: "Entry to the museum is free for children under twelve. Teenagers pay five pounds, and adults pay eight fifty." How much does a fifteen-year-old pay?

    Show answer
    £5. A fifteen-year-old is a teenager. £8.50 is the adult price, and *free* is for under-twelves.

  6. Practice 6. You hear: "The festival used to be in July, but because of the heat it has moved to the first weekend of October." When is the festival now?

    Show answer
    The first weekend of October. *Used to be in July* is the old date.

11. Common mistakes and how to avoid them

MistakeWhat to do instead
Trying to understand every wordListen for the key words and their meaning. You can get full marks without understanding everything.
Stopping when you miss an answerMove on immediately. The second listening is your chance to go back.
Writing the first thing you hearWait for the end of the sentence. Many answers are corrected or changed with *but*.
Writing more answers than the linesGive exactly the number of answers asked for, one per line.
Copying a phrase in a 'your own words' questionExplain each part of the phrase with simple words of your own.
Leaving answers emptyAlways guess. There is no penalty for a wrong answer.
Untidy letters in boxesWrite capital letters clearly. If you change one, cross it out and write the new letter beside the box clearly.

12. A weekly listening practice plan

Listening improves with regular practice: 20 to 30 minutes a day is better than three hours once a week. The secret is active listening: listen with a question in your mind, pause, note what you heard, then check. Use audio-only sources such as BBC Learning English (its audio programmes and podcasts) and podcasts on BBC Sounds. Choose recordings on the course themes: young people, technology, sport, the environment, travel, school and careers.

Make your own vocabulary list

After each session, write down five useful words with an example sentence. Group them by theme (Identity and culture, Global issues, Study and work). These are the words you will hear in the exam, and you can use them in your speaking test too.

13. Exam-day checklist

  • Black pen (and a spare). No dictionary.
  • Use all 5 minutes of reading time: underline key words, predict answer types, compare the options.
  • First listening: quick short notes. Second listening: check, complete, correct.
  • Watch for signal words: *but, actually, in the end, used to, not … but*.
  • Numbers as digits. Check *-teen* and *-ty*.
  • One answer per line, and exactly the number asked for.
  • Letters in boxes: clear capitals.
  • Task 5: explain phrases in your own words; think about the speaker's feelings and purpose.
  • No empty answers at the end.
