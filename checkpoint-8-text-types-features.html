Checkpoint 8 · Reading: understanding texts

Text Types and Their Features

Fiction and non-fiction, every genre you can meet in the reading papers with its purpose and features, and how to answer “name the text type” and “give two features” questions.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Every reading text in Checkpoint 8 belongs to a text type (also called a genre). Paper 1 always gives you non-fiction (real information, real people, real events). Paper 2 always gives you fiction (an extract from a novel or short story). If you can recognise the text type quickly, you already know what the writer is trying to do, how the text will be organised and which features the examiner expects you to spot. This page gives you every genre you need, the features of each one, and a method for the questions that ask you to name a text type or give its features.

How this topic is tested
  • Name the text type: “What type of text is this?” or “Tick the text type that best describes paragraph 2.”
  • Give features: “Give two features that show this is a review.” / “Tick two features of a newspaper report used in the text.”
  • Formal or informal: “Give two features that make Text A formal.”
  • Compare two texts: when the insert has a Text A and a Text B, you may be asked for one feature they share and one way they are different.
  • Purpose links: a text-type question often sits next to a purpose question (inform, persuade, entertain…). See the page on purpose and viewpoint.
  • These questions are usually short (1-2 marks each), so they are quick marks if you know your features.

1. Fiction or non-fiction?

Before you name a genre, decide which family the text belongs to. The one-line test: if the writer’s job is to report what really happened or what is really true, it is non-fiction. If the writer’s job is to invent what could happen, it is fiction — even when it is set in a real place.

Non-fiction (Paper 1)Fiction (Paper 2)
Based onFacts, real life, real people and eventsThe writer’s imagination
Main purposesTo inform, explain, persuade, argue, advise, instruct, describe, recountTo entertain, to make the reader feel, to explore an idea or theme
People in itReal people (named experts, witnesses, the writer)Invented characters
Typical layoutHeadings, sub-headings, bullet points, dates, captions, sign-offsParagraphs of narrative and dialogue · chapters
Typical languageFacts, statistics, quotations from experts, opinions, technical wordsDescription, dialogue, figurative language, suspense
ExamplesBiography, article, report, leaflet, review, letter, blog, speechAdventure, mystery, fantasy, science fiction, realistic fiction, fable
Tricky cases
  • A historical novel about a real war is still fiction: the characters and their conversations are invented.
  • A travel article uses beautiful description like a story, but it is non-fiction: the writer really went there.
  • An autobiography can read like a story (dialogue, suspense), but it is non-fiction: the events really happened to the writer.
  • A newspaper report about a story-writing competition is non-fiction, even though the winning story it mentions is fiction.

2. What counts as a “feature”?

A feature is something you can point to in the text. There are two kinds. When a question says “give two features”, the safest answers name the feature and give a short example from the text.

Kind of featureExamples you can name
Language features (how it is written)first person “I” · third person “he/she” · past tense · present tense · imperative verbs (“Mix…”, “Book now”) · time connectives (“First”, “After that”) · facts and statistics · opinions · quotations from experts or witnesses · rhetorical questions · direct address (“you”) · emotive language · formal vocabulary · informal language/slang/contractions · technical vocabulary · descriptive language and imagery
Layout / organisational features (how it looks)headline · title · sub-headings · byline (writer’s name) · date · bullet points · numbered steps · paragraphs organised by topic or by time · captions · fact box · contact details · greeting and sign-off · star rating · subject line · slogan
A feature is not a topic

“It talks about dogs” is not a feature. “It uses technical vocabulary such as ‘canine’ and ‘breed’” is a feature. Always describe how the text is written or laid out, not what it is about.

3. All non-fiction genres and their features

Learn this table well. The left column is the genre, the middle is why people write it, and the right column lists the features that prove it. In the exam you only need two features, but knowing four or five gives you choice.

GenrePurposeKey features
AutobiographyTo tell the story of the writer’s own lifeFirst person (I, my) · past tense · covers a long period of the writer’s life, usually in time order · the writer’s feelings and reflections (“Looking back, I realise…”) · real names, dates and places
MemoirTo tell about one important period or experience of the writer’s lifeFirst person · past tense · focuses on one time or theme · strong feelings and reflection · sensory description
BiographyTo tell the story of another person’s lifeThird person (he, she) · past tense · usually chronological (birth → achievements → later life) · facts, dates, places · quotations from the person or people who knew them · fairly formal, balanced tone
RecountTo retell events in the order they happenedOpening that tells who, what, where, when · past tense · time connectives (first, later, finally) · usually first person · specific details · ending with a personal comment
Diary / journalTo record the writer’s own day and feelings privatelyDate at the top · “Dear Diary” (optional) · first person · past tense for events, present for feelings · informal, chatty · honest emotions · exclamations and questions to self
Letter (formal)To complain, request, apply, inform or persuade someone officialAddresses and date · “Dear Sir or Madam” / “Dear Mrs Hassan” · clear reason for writing in paragraph 1 · formal vocabulary · no contractions · polite requests · “Yours faithfully” (name unknown) / “Yours sincerely” (name known)
Letter (informal)To share news or feelings with a friend or relative“Dear + first name,” · friendly, chatty tone · contractions (I’m, can’t) · questions to the reader · personal news · informal sign-off (“Love,” / “Best wishes,”)
EmailTo communicate quickly, formally or informallyTo / From / Subject line · greeting · short paragraphs · purpose stated early · sign-off · tone depends on reader (formal to a manager, informal to a friend)
BlogTo share experiences, opinions or advice onlineTitle and date · first person · chatty, personal voice · direct address (“Have you ever…?”) · opinions · humour · sometimes links, comments or hashtags · can be updated in posts
Magazine / feature articleTo inform and entertain (sometimes persuade) readers about a topicCatchy headline · strapline or sub-headings · engaging opening hook · mixture of facts and opinions · quotations · direct address · lively vocabulary · conclusion that returns to the opening idea
Newspaper reportTo inform readers about a recent real eventHeadline (often present tense, short) · byline · opening paragraph answers who, what, where, when (why/how) · third person · past tense · quotations from witnesses or officials · most important facts first · formal, factual tone · photograph and caption
Non-chronological reportTo give facts about a subject organised by topic, not timeTitle · introduction that classifies the subject · sub-headings (appearance, habitat, diet…) · present tense · third person · formal, impersonal tone · technical vocabulary · facts and statistics · diagrams and fact boxes
Information / explanation textTo explain how or why something happensPresent tense · cause-and-effect connectives (because, as a result, therefore) · technical vocabulary, sometimes explained · diagrams · logical sequence
Persuasive text (speech, advert, letter, article)To make the reader think or act in a certain wayStrong one-sided opinion · emotive language · rhetorical questions · direct address (you) · inclusive “we/us” · rule of three · repetition · exaggeration · statistics chosen to support the writer · call to action with imperatives (“Join us today!”)
Argument (argumentative text)To prove one point of view using reasons and evidenceClear position stated early · each paragraph gives one reason with evidence · logical connectives (therefore, this shows, furthermore) · the opposite view is mentioned and then answered (counter-argument) · confident, formal tone · conclusion repeats the position
Discursive / balanced argumentTo discuss both sides of an issue fairlyIntroduction presents the issue · arguments for and against given equal space · connectives of contrast (however, on the other hand) · cautious words (may, might, perhaps) · writer’s own view only in the conclusion
SpeechTo speak to and move a live audienceGreeting to the audience · direct address · rhetorical questions · repetition and rule of three · short sentences for impact · signposting (firstly, finally) · memorable ending or call to action
ReviewTo judge a film, book, product, place or event and recommend it (or not)Title or name of what is reviewed · short summary without spoilers · first-person opinion · evaluative adjectives (gripping, disappointing) · comparatives and superlatives · good and bad points with examples · final verdict, recommendation or star rating
Leaflet / brochureTo inform or persuade the reader quickly about one subjectEye-catching title and slogan · sub-headings · bullet points · short sentences · direct address (you) · imperatives · persuasive adjectives · boxed key facts · contact details, prices, opening times
Travel writingTo describe a place and the writer’s journey so the reader can imagine itFirst person · vivid description using the five senses · similes and metaphors · personal anecdotes · mixture of past (the journey) and present (what the place is like) · practical tips (how to get there, cost, best time)
Instructions / procedural textTo tell the reader how to do or make somethingTitle stating the goal (“How to…”) · list of equipment/ingredients · numbered steps in order · imperative verbs at the start (Cut, Stir, Press) · time connectives · short, precise sentences · no unnecessary description · diagrams
Advice textTo help the reader solve a problem or make a good choiceDirect address (you) · modal verbs (you should, you could, it might help to) · imperatives softened politely (Try to…) · sympathetic, reassuring tone · tips organised as points or paragraphs · often a question-and-answer format (problem page)

4. Genres that students confuse

Many wrong answers come from mixing up two genres that look similar. Learn the one difference that separates each pair.

These two…The key difference
Biography vs autobiographyBiography = someone else’s life, third person (she). Autobiography = the writer’s own life, first person (I).
Autobiography vs memoirAutobiography covers most of a life. Memoir focuses on one period or experience.
Recount vs diaryBoth retell events in order. A diary is dated, private and full of feelings. A recount is written for a reader and is more organised.
Diary vs blogA diary is private. A blog is public and talks to readers (“What do you think?”).
Newspaper report vs magazine articleA report gives the facts of a recent event objectively. An article explores a topic, mixes facts with opinion and tries to entertain.
Recount vs non-chronological reportA recount is organised by time (what happened next). A non-chronological report is organised by topic (habitat, diet, appearance).
Persuasive vs argumentPersuasive writing mainly uses emotion (emotive words, direct appeals). An argument mainly uses logic and evidence and answers the other side.
Argument vs discursiveAn argument takes one side from the start. A discursive text gives both sides fairly and decides only at the end.
Instructions vs adviceInstructions give exact steps in order with commands (Press the button). Advice gives suggestions with modal verbs (You could try…).
Review vs articleA review judges one thing and ends with a verdict or rating. An article discusses a wider topic.
Leaflet vs articleA leaflet is short, broken into boxes and bullet points, with contact details. An article is continuous paragraphs.
Travel writing vs non-chronological reportTravel writing is personal (I, feelings, the journey). A report about a country is impersonal (third person, facts by topic).
Letter vs emailAn email has a subject line and is usually shorter. A formal letter has addresses and “Yours sincerely/faithfully”.

5. Formal and informal features

A common question asks for features that make a text formal (or informal). Use this table. Remember to quote an example for each feature you give.

Formal featuresInformal features
Full forms: “do not”, “it is”Contractions: “don’t”, “it’s”
Standard English and precise vocabulary: “purchase”, “assist”, “numerous”Slang and chatty words: “loads of”, “awesome”, “stuff”
Third person and impersonal phrases: “It is recommended that…”First and second person: “I reckon you’ll love it”
Passive voice: “The bridge was opened in 2019.”Active, personal voice: “They opened the bridge ages ago.”
Polite, distant greetings and sign-offs: “Dear Sir or Madam”, “Yours faithfully”Friendly greetings: “Hi Omar,” and “Cheers,”
Longer, complex sentencesShort sentences, questions, exclamation marks, humour
Facts, statistics, named expertsPersonal anecdotes and feelings

6. Fiction genres and their features

Paper 2 extracts come from stories and novels. You may be asked what kind of story the extract seems to come from, or which features show this. Look at the setting, the characters and the kind of events.

FantasyInvented worlds or a hidden magical side of our worldWizards, magical creatures, a chosen heroMagic with its own rules · good against evil · quests · invented names and places
Science fictionThe future, space, other planets, laboratoriesScientists, astronauts, robots, aliensAdvanced technology · “What if…?” ideas · technical-sounding vocabulary · questions about the future
DystopianA future society that has gone wrongA hero who questions the rulesControl, surveillance, strict rules · fear · rebellion · a warning about the present

Whatever the genre, every story extract also has the features of narrative: a narrator (first person “I” or third person “he/she”), a setting, characters, dialogue in inverted commas, description, and a plot that builds towards a problem or a turning point. If a question asks for “two features of a story”, these are safe answers.

7. Method: name the text type and prove it

  1. Look at the layout first. Headline? Bullet points? Date? Numbered steps? “Dear…”? A subject line? Layout often gives the answer in five seconds.
  2. Check the person and tense. I/past = autobiography, recount, diary. She/past = biography, report. Present/third person = non-chronological report or information text. Imperatives = instructions or leaflet.
  3. Ask what the writer wants. To inform, persuade, advise, entertain? The purpose narrows the genre.
  4. Name the genre exactly. Write “a newspaper report”, not “an informative text”, unless the question asks for the purpose.
  5. Prove it with features. For each mark: name the feature + quote a short example. One feature per mark, clearly separated.

8. Worked examples

Example 1. Read this extract:

“FLOODS CLOSE COASTAL ROAD. Residents of Ras El Bar were moved to safety yesterday after heavy rain flooded the main coastal road. ‘The water rose faster than anyone expected,’ said local official Karim Fawzy. The road will remain closed until Friday.”

Question: What type of text is this? Give two features that show this. (2 marks)

Answer: It is a newspaper report. It has a short headline in capital letters, “FLOODS CLOSE COASTAL ROAD”, and it includes a quotation from an official: “The water rose faster than anyone expected”.

Why it works: the genre is named exactly, and each feature is named and then proved with a quotation. Other correct features: the opening sentence tells who, what, where and when · it is written in the past tense and the third person.

Example 2. Read this extract:

“Feeling anxious before exams? You are not alone. Try breaking your revision into short sessions with breaks in between. It might also help to talk to someone you trust. Remember: a good night’s sleep is worth more than an extra hour of panic.”

Question: Give two features of advice writing used in this text. (2 marks)

Answer: It speaks directly to the reader with a question, “Feeling anxious before exams?”. It uses gentle suggestions with modal verbs, “It might also help to talk to someone you trust”.

Why it works: “direct address” and “modal verbs” are features, not topics. A weak answer would say “it is about exams”, which earns nothing.

Example 3 (comparing two texts). Text A is a leaflet for a science museum. Text B is a student’s diary entry about visiting the same museum.

Question: Give one way the two texts are similar and one way they are different. (2 marks)

Answer: Similar: both describe the museum’s exhibits, for example the planetarium. Different: Text A is formal and uses bullet points and opening times, while Text B is informal and uses the first person to share feelings, “I couldn’t stop smiling”.

Common traps
  • Giving the purpose when the question asks for the text type (“to persuade” is not a genre).
  • Writing “it has paragraphs”. Almost every text has paragraphs, so it proves nothing. Say how the paragraphs are organised (by time / by topic).
  • Writing two features in one long sentence so the examiner cannot separate them. Use two sentences.
  • Naming a feature that is not in this text. Only give features you can quote.
  • Calling a story “non-fiction” because it feels realistic, or calling travel writing “fiction” because it is descriptive.

9. Practice questions

Each extract below is short and original. Answer in full sentences, then check.

  1. “Born in a small village near Aswan in 1931, Hamid Ali taught himself to read by candlelight. By the age of twenty, he had opened the town’s first library.” What type of text is this? Give one feature that proves it.

    Show answer
    A biography. It is written in the third person about someone else’s life (“he had opened the town’s first library”). It also gives dates (“in 1931”) and follows time order.

  2. “I was seven when my family moved to Alexandria. I remember hating the noise at first. Later, the sea became the best friend I had.” Biography or autobiography? Explain.

    Show answer
    Autobiography: the writer tells the story of their own life in the first person (“I was seven”, “my family”).

  3. “1. Fill the pot with soil. 2. Push the seed two centimetres down. 3. Water gently every morning.” Name the text type and give two features.

    Show answer
    Instructions. Features: numbered steps in order (“1.”, “2.”, “3.”) and imperative verbs at the start of each step (“Fill”, “Push”, “Water”).

  4. “Sea turtles are reptiles that live in warm oceans. Diet: most adults eat seagrass. Lifespan: many live for over fifty years.” Name the text type and give two features.

    Show answer
    A non-chronological report. It uses the present tense for general facts (“are reptiles”, “live”) and is organised by topic with sub-headings (“Diet”, “Lifespan”).

  5. “Monday 4th March. What a disaster! I forgot my PE kit AGAIN and Mr Samir gave me that look. Tomorrow I’m packing my bag the night before. I mean it this time.” Name the text type and give two features.

    Show answer
    A diary entry. It has a date at the top (“Monday 4th March”) and an informal, emotional first-person voice with exclamations (“What a disaster!”).

  6. “Are you tired of boring weekends? Then come to the Green Valley Farm Festival! Pick fresh strawberries, meet friendly animals and taste homemade bread. Don’t miss out — book today!” Give two persuasive features.

    Show answer
    A rhetorical question to the reader (“Are you tired of boring weekends?”) and an imperative call to action (“book today!”). Also accept: rule of three (“Pick…, meet…, taste…”), positive adjectives (“friendly”, “fresh”).

  7. “Some people believe school should start later because teenagers need more sleep. On the other hand, a later start would mean finishing later in the afternoon.” Is this persuasive, argument or discursive writing? Explain.

    Show answer
    Discursive (balanced): it presents both sides fairly and links them with the contrast connective “On the other hand”, without taking a side yet.

  8. “The acting is superb, but the story drags in the middle. Overall, I would give it three stars and recommend it to anyone who enjoys slow, thoughtful films.” Name two features of a review.

    Show answer
    A first-person opinion with evaluative words (“superb”, “drags”) and a final verdict with a rating and recommendation (“three stars”, “recommend it to anyone who…”).

  9. “From: Laila Adel. Subject: Missing library book. Dear Mr Nabil, I am writing to explain why my book is late…” Name the text type and say whether it is formal or informal, with one reason.

    Show answer
    An email (it has From and Subject lines). It is formal: it uses a polite greeting with a title, “Dear Mr Nabil”, and the full form “I am writing” instead of a contraction.

  10. Give two features that make this sentence formal: “Visitors are requested not to feed the animals as this may cause them serious harm.”

    Show answer
    The passive voice (“Visitors are requested”) and formal vocabulary with no contractions (“requested”, “may cause them serious harm”). It is also impersonal (no “I” or “you”).

  11. “The ferry chugged into the harbour as the sun melted into the sea. I could smell grilled fish and hear fishermen shouting prices. Top tip: arrive before nine to beat the crowds.” Name the text type and give two features.

    Show answer
    Travel writing. It uses sensory description (“smell grilled fish”, “hear fishermen shouting”) and gives practical advice to the reader (“Top tip: arrive before nine”). It is also written in the first person.

  12. “The fox praised the crow’s beautiful voice. The crow opened its beak to sing, and the cheese fell straight into the fox’s mouth. Never trust a flatterer.” Name the fiction genre and give two features.

    Show answer
    A fable. It has animals that behave like people (a fox and a crow who talk and think) and it ends with a moral (“Never trust a flatterer”).

  13. “The airlock hissed open. Beyond it, the red dust of the third colony stretched to the horizon, and the dome’s computer announced that oxygen reserves were at nine per cent.” Which fiction genre is this? Give two clues.

    Show answer
    Science fiction. Clues: advanced technology (“airlock”, “the dome’s computer”) and a setting on another planet or colony (“the red dust of the third colony”).

  14. “Thunder shook the shutters of the empty house. Somewhere upstairs, a door creaked open by itself.” Which genre and which features?

    Show answer
    Horror (or Gothic). It has a dark, isolated setting (“the empty house”), stormy weather that creates fear (pathetic fallacy, “Thunder shook the shutters”) and an unexplained, frightening event (“a door creaked open by itself”).

  15. Text A is a newspaper report about a new metro line. Text B is a blog post by a commuter about her first ride. Give one difference in the way they are written.

    Show answer
    Text A is written in the third person with a formal, factual tone and quotations from officials, while Text B is written in the first person with an informal, personal tone that shares the writer’s feelings.

  16. Why is a novel about a real ancient battle, with invented soldiers as main characters, called fiction?

    Show answer
    Because the main characters, their thoughts and their conversations are invented by the author. The real battle is only the background.

  17. A student writes: “Two features of this leaflet are that it is about healthy eating and it is interesting.” Why does this score 0? Rewrite it.

    Show answer
    Both are about the topic or the student’s opinion, not features. Better: “It uses sub-headings such as ‘Five a day’, and bullet points to list the tips.”
