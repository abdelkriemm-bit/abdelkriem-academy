Fractions: Recurring Decimals, Mixed Numbers and Order of Operations

Recognise recurring decimals, subtract mixed numbers, multiply a whole number by a mixed number, divide a whole number by a fraction, and use the laws of arithmetic with fractions and decimals.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Recognise fractions that are equal to recurring decimals, for example 1/3 = 0.333… and 2/11 = 0.1818… (8Nf.01)
  • Estimate and subtract mixed numbers, and write the answer as a mixed number in its simplest form (8Nf.02)
  • Estimate and multiply a whole number by a mixed number, and divide a whole number by a proper fraction (8Nf.03)
  • Use the laws of arithmetic and the order of operations (including brackets) to simplify calculations with decimals or fractions (8Nf.04)

This page has four parts: (1) fractions that turn into recurring decimals, (2) subtracting mixed numbers, (3) multiplying a whole number by a mixed number and dividing a whole number by a fraction, and (4) using the laws of arithmetic to make fraction and decimal calculations easier. All of this can appear on the non-calculator paper, so the methods are written out by hand.

1. Fractions and recurring decimals

A fraction is a division: 3/8 means 3 ÷ 8. Doing the division gives the decimal. Sometimes the division stops (a terminating decimal); sometimes it never stops because the same remainders come round again and again (a recurring decimal).

Short divisionWhat happensDecimal
3 ÷ 8 = 0.375remainders 6, 4, then 0 — it stops0.375 (terminating)
1 ÷ 3remainder 1 every time, so the digit 3 repeats0.333… (recurring)
5 ÷ 68, then remainder 2 every time, so 3 repeats0.8333… (recurring)
2 ÷ 11remainders 9, 2, 9, 2 … so the block 18 repeats0.181818… (recurring)

Dot notation. Instead of writing "…", mathematicians put a dot over the digit that repeats. If a block of digits repeats, put a dot over the first and last digit of the block.

Learn these. You should recognise them on sight:

FractionDecimalPattern to spot

1/6, 5/60.1666…, 0.8333…one digit, then the repeat starts
1/9, 2/9, 4/9, 7/90.111…, 0.222…, 0.444…, 0.777…ninths: the numerator repeats
1/11, 2/11, 3/11, 5/110.0909…, 0.1818…, 0.2727…, 0.4545…elevenths: 9 × numerator repeats (9 × 3 = 27)
1/70.142857142857…a block of six digits repeats
1/2, 1/4, 3/8, 1/5, 7/200.5, 0.25, 0.375, 0.2, 0.35these all terminate

Calculators round the last digit. A calculator may show 2/3 as 0.6666666667. That 7 is only the calculator rounding — 2/3 = 0.666… recurring. Do not say "it terminates" just because the screen is full.

2. Subtracting mixed numbers

Always estimate first. Round each mixed number to the nearest whole number (or half). For 5 1/4 − 2 2/3: about 5 − 3 = 2, and a bit more because 2 2/3 was rounded up. So expect a little over 2.

Method A — change to improper fractions (safe and always works):

  1. Write each mixed number as an improper fraction: 5 1/4 = 21/4 and 2 2/3 = 8/3.
  2. Use a common denominator (the LCM of 4 and 3 is 12): 21/4 = 63/12 and 8/3 = 32/12.
  3. Subtract the numerators: 63/12 − 32/12 = 31/12.
  4. Change back to a mixed number and simplify: 31 ÷ 12 = 2 remainder 7, so 2 7/12. (7 and 12 have no common factor.) This matches the estimate.

Method B — wholes and fractions separately (quicker with small numbers, but watch the fractions):

  1. Common denominator: 5 1/4 − 2 2/3 = 5 3/12 − 2 8/12.
  2. 3/12 is smaller than 8/12, so you cannot take 8/12 away. Exchange one whole for 12/12: 5 3/12 = 4 15/12.
  3. Wholes: 4 − 2 = 2. Fractions: 15/12 − 8/12 = 7/12.
  4. Answer: 2 7/12.

Method C — count up on a number line (good for checking):

Simplest form. The answer must be a mixed number with the fraction part in its simplest form: 3 6/8 should be written 3 3/4, and 2 15/12 is not finished — it is 3 3/12 = 3 1/4.

4. Dividing a whole number by a proper fraction

6 ÷ 3/4 asks: how many 3/4 fit into 6? Draw it: 6 wholes are 24 quarters, and 24 quarters make 8 groups of 3 quarters.

The quick method: multiply by the reciprocal. Dividing by 3/4 is the same as multiplying by 4/3 ("keep, change, flip": keep the 6, change ÷ to ×, flip 3/4 to 4/3).

  1. 6 ÷ 3/4 = 6 × 4/3
  2. = 24/3
  3. = 8 ✓ (the same as the diagram)

Sense check: dividing by a fraction less than 1 gives an answer bigger than the number you started with. 3/4 fits into 1 more than once, so it fits into 6 more than 6 times.

5. Laws of arithmetic and order of operations with decimals and fractions

The order of operations is the same as for whole numbers: Brackets, Indices, Division and Multiplication (left to right), Addition and Subtraction (left to right). The laws of arithmetic let you reorder or regroup to make a calculation easy without a calculator.

LawExampleWhy it helps
Commutative (swap order)0.25 × 7.3 × 4 = 0.25 × 4 × 7.30.25 × 4 = 1, so the answer is 7.3
Associative (regroup)1/3 × (3 × 17) = (1/3 × 3) × 171/3 × 3 = 1, so the answer is 17
Distributive (take out a common factor)3.7 × 2.5 + 6.3 × 2.5 = (3.7 + 6.3) × 2.510 × 2.5 = 25
Distributive (split a number)9.9 × 6 = 10 × 6 − 0.1 × 660 − 0.6 = 59.4
Distributive with fractions3/4 × 17 + 3/4 × 3 = 3/4 × 2020 ÷ 4 × 3 = 15

Brackets change the answer:

  • 1/2 + 1/4 × 2/3 = 1/2 + 2/12 = 1/2 + 1/6 = 3/6 + 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3 (multiply first)
  • (1/2 + 1/4) × 2/3 = 3/4 × 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2 (bracket first)
  • 4.8 − 1.2 × 3 = 4.8 − 3.6 = 1.2 (not 3.6 × 3)
  • (2.4 + 3.6) ÷ 0.1 = 6 ÷ 0.1 = 60

Subtraction and division are not commutative: 5 − 2 1/2 ≠ 2 1/2 − 5, and 6 ÷ 3/4 ≠ 3/4 ÷ 6. Only swap the order for + and ×.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — (a) Write 7/9 as a decimal. (b) Draw a ring around the fraction that is equal to a recurring decimal: 2/5 5/6 7/20 3/8 [2 marks]

  • (a) Ninths: the numerator repeats, so 7/9 = 0.777… (written 0.7 with a dot over the 7). Check: 7 ÷ 9 = 0 r 7, 70 ÷ 9 = 7 r 7, 70 ÷ 9 = 7 r 7 … the remainder 7 keeps coming back.
  • (b) 2/5 = 0.4, 7/20 = 0.35, 3/8 = 0.375 all stop. 5/6 = 0.8333… so ring 5/6.

Worked example 2 — Work out 4 3/8 − 1 5/6. Give your answer as a mixed number in its simplest form. [2 marks]

  1. Estimate: about 4 1/2 − 2 = 2 1/2.
  2. Improper fractions: 4 3/8 = 35/8 and 1 5/6 = 11/6.
  3. Common denominator 24: 35/8 = 105/24 and 11/6 = 44/24. (1 mark for a correct common denominator.)
  4. 105/24 − 44/24 = 61/24 = 2 remainder 13 → 2 13/24. (13 is prime and does not divide 24, so this is the simplest form.) Close to the estimate ✓

Common mistake: subtracting the fractions the wrong way round: 4 − 1 = 3 and 5/6 − 3/8 = 11/24, giving 3 11/24. You cannot take 20/24 from 9/24 without exchanging a whole.

Worked example 3 — Work out 3 × 4 5/6. Give your answer as a mixed number in its simplest form. [2 marks]

Estimate: 3 × 5 = 15, a bit less. Split: 3 × 4 = 12 and 3 × 5/6 = 15/6 = 2 3/6 = 2 1/2. Add: 12 + 2 1/2 = 14 1/2 ✓. Common mistake: multiplying only the whole number (3 × 4 5/6 = 12 5/6) — the fraction part must be multiplied too.

Worked example 4 — A ribbon is 9 m long. It is cut into pieces that are each 3/5 m long. How many pieces are there? [2 marks]

  1. "How many 3/5 fit into 9?" → 9 ÷ 3/5.
  2. 9 × 5/3 = 45/3 = 15 pieces.
  3. Check: 15 × 3/5 = 45/5 = 9 ✓. The answer is more than 9 because each piece is shorter than 1 m.

Worked example 5 — Without a calculator, work out 4.6 × 0.7 + 5.4 × 0.7. Show how you used a law of arithmetic. [2 marks]

Both terms have the factor 0.7, so use the distributive law: (4.6 + 5.4) × 0.7 = 10 × 0.7 = 7. Much quicker than working out 3.22 + 3.78.

Worked example 6 — Work out 2/3 + 1/3 × (5/2 − 1). [2 marks]

  1. Brackets: 5/2 − 1 = 5/2 − 2/2 = 3/2.
  2. Multiply: 1/3 × 3/2 = 3/6 = 1/2.
  3. Add: 2/3 + 1/2 = 4/6 + 3/6 = 7/6 = 1 1/6.

Common mistake: adding first (2/3 + 1/3 = 1, then 1 × 3/2 = 3/2). Multiplication comes before addition.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking a decimal terminates because the calculator screen is full: 2/3 shows as 0.6666666667, but it recurs.
  • Putting the dots on the wrong digits: 0.1818… has dots over the 1 and the 8 (the first and last digits of the repeating block).
  • Subtracting mixed numbers without a common denominator, or taking the smaller fraction from the bigger one when it is the wrong way round.
  • Leaving an answer like 2 14/12 or 3 6/8 — change to a proper mixed number in its simplest form.
  • Multiplying only the whole part: 4 × 2 3/5 is not 8 3/5.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing: 6 ÷ 3/4 = 6 × 4/3, not 1/6 × 3/4.
  • Working left to right when × or ÷ comes after + or −. Brackets first, then × and ÷, then + and −.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • "Draw a ring around the fraction that is equal to a recurring decimal" or "Tick the fractions that are recurring decimals".
  • "Work out 5 1/4 − 2 2/3. Give your answer as a mixed number in its simplest form." — usually 2 marks: 1 for a correct method (e.g. a correct common denominator), 1 for the final answer. An answer that is not in simplest form can lose the final mark.
  • "Estimate …" — show the rounded numbers. An exact answer with no estimate may get no mark.
  • Word problems with division by a fraction: "How many 3/4-litre glasses can be filled from 6 litres?" — write the division first.
  • "Use the distributive law to work out …" or "Show how you can work out … without a calculator" — show the regrouped calculation, not just the answer.
  • Spot the mistake in a student's working, then correct it (Thinking and Working Mathematically: critiquing).

Practice

Remember
  • A fraction is a division. If the division never stops, the decimal recurs: 1/3 = 0.333…, 2/11 = 0.1818…, 1/6 = 0.1666…
  • Subtracting mixed numbers: estimate, use a common denominator, exchange a whole if needed (or use improper fractions), and give the answer as a mixed number in simplest form.
  • Whole number × mixed number: multiply the whole part and the fraction part, then add. 4 × 2 3/5 = 8 + 2 2/5 = 10 2/5.
  • Whole number ÷ fraction: multiply by the reciprocal. 6 ÷ 3/4 = 6 × 4/3 = 8 — bigger than 6.
  • Brackets, then × and ÷, then + and −. Use the commutative, associative and distributive laws to make calculations easier.
