American Diploma · Digital SAT · Grammar: Boundaries

Commas: Introductions, Lists, Adjectives — and When NOT to Use One

Commas after introductions, between list items and coordinate adjectives, before FANBOYS — plus every place a comma is forbidden, with 10 practice questions.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Comma questions belong to the Boundaries skill in the Standard English Conventions domain (about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, 11–15 questions in total). The College Board describes this skill as punctuation at the end of sentences and inside them: linking clauses, separating list items, setting off supplements, and knowing when no punctuation is needed — for example, never between a subject and its verb.

The question is always the same: a short passage (25–150 words) with a blank and the stem “Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?” This lesson covers the four comma jobs that are not about extra information — introductions, lists, adjectives and FANBOYS — and then the most important list of all: the places where a comma is never allowed. (Commas in pairs around extra information have their own lesson: Extra Information.)

الفاصلة مش نَفَس

ما تحطش فاصلة لمجرد إنك بتاخد نفس وانت بتقرا. الفاصلة ليها شغلانات محددة: بعد المقدمة، بين عناصر القائمة، بين صفتين متساويتين، وقبل and/but لما توصل جملتين كاملتين. أي مكان تاني غالبًا غلط.

Job 1: After an introductory word, phrase or clause

When something comes before the main clause (the subject and its verb), put a comma after it. This tells the reader where the main sentence begins.

Type of introduction✓ Example
Dependent clause (because, when, although, if, after, since, while …)✓ Because the road was flooded, the bus took a different route.
Long prepositional phrase✓ After three years of careful restoration work, the old station reopened.
Participle phrase (-ing / -ed)✓ Opened in 1869, the Suez Canal shortened the trip from Europe to Asia. / ✓ Hoping to save water, the farmers changed their schedule.
Infinitive phrase (purpose)✓ To reduce costs, the factory switched to solar power.
Transition word or phrase✓ However, the results were not final. / ✓ For example, Siwa grows dates and olives.
  • ✗ Because the road was flooded; the bus took a different route. (a semicolon needs a complete sentence on both sides)
  • ✗ Because the road was flooded. The bus took a different route. (the first part is a fragment)
  • ✗ Because the road, was flooded the bus took a different route. (the comma is inside the introduction, between its subject and verb)
  • ✗ Although the road was flooded, but the bus arrived on time. (although + but: two linking words for one join)

The reverse order: when the dependent clause comes after the main clause, you normally need no comma: “The bus took a different route because the road was flooded.” A very short introduction (“In 2015 the museum reopened”) may take a comma or not; the test will not make you choose on that alone.

Find where the main clause starts

Look for the subject of the main sentence (often “the …” or a name) followed by its verb. The comma goes immediately before that subject — never after it.

Job 2: Before FANBOYS joining two complete sentences

FANBOYS = for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. When one of them joins two independent clauses, put a comma before it, never after it. (The full rules for joining clauses are on the Sentence Boundaries page.)

  • ✓ The road was closed, but the bus found another route.
  • ✗ The road was closed but, the bus found another route. (comma after the FANBOYS word)
  • ✗ The road was closed, the bus found another route. (comma splice — the FANBOYS word is missing)

Job 3: Between items in a list

Separate three or more items with commas. The comma before the final “and” or “or” is called the serial comma (Oxford comma). Using it is always safe.

  • ✓ The kit contains a compass, a flashlight, and a first-aid manual.
  • ✓ Over the weekend, the volunteers planted trees, cleaned the canal banks, and painted the school. (the items can be verb phrases)
  • ✓ Two items only: “a compass and a flashlight” — no comma.
  • ✗ The kit contains a compass a flashlight, and a first-aid manual. (comma missing between the first two items)
  • ✗ The kit contains, a compass, a flashlight, and a first-aid manual. (comma between the verb and its object)
  • ✗ A compass, a flashlight, and a manual, are in the kit. (comma after the last item splits the subject from the verb)
  • ✗ Spices such as, cumin, coriander, and cardamom are used. (no comma right after “such as”)

When the items themselves already contain commas (“Luxor, Egypt”), the items are separated by semicolons — a special SAT/ACT rule taught in the Semicolons lesson.

Job 4: Between coordinate adjectives

Two adjectives before a noun are coordinate when each one describes the noun separately and equally. Coordinate adjectives take a comma between them. Cumulative adjectives build on each other (the first one describes the second adjective + noun together) and take no comma.

Two quick tests — if both work naturally, use a comma:

  1. Swap test: change the order. “a steep, long climb” still sounds natural → coordinate.
  2. “And” test: put “and” between them. “a long and steep climb” sounds natural → coordinate.
PhraseSwap / “and” testComma?
a long, difficult journey“a difficult and long journey” ✓Yes ✓
a cold, windy morning“a windy and cold morning” ✓Yes ✓
a small wooden chest“a wooden and small chest” ✗No ✓
three large stone statues“large and three stone statues” ✗No ✓
a bright red car“a red and bright car” ✗ (bright describes red)No ✓
an old Egyptian coin“an Egyptian and old coin” ✗No ✓
  • ✗ It was a long, difficult, journey. (never a comma between the last adjective and the noun)
  • ✗ They found a small, wooden chest. (cumulative adjectives: size + material)
Numbers, colors and materials

Adjectives of number, age, color, origin and material (three, old, red, Egyptian, wooden) usually join the noun closely and take no comma before them. Opinion and quality adjectives (beautiful, difficult, long, steep) are often coordinate with each other.

When NOT to use a comma — the big list

On the SAT an unnecessary comma is an error, not a matter of style. Many questions offer three choices that put a comma in a forbidden place and one choice with no punctuation. Learn these no-comma zones:

No comma between …✗ Wrong✓ Right
A subject and its verb (even a long subject)The students who planted the trees in the school garden, watered them every day.The students who planted the trees in the school garden watered them every day.
A verb and its objectThe engineer designed, a small robot.The engineer designed a small robot.
A linking verb and its complementThe winners were, Hana and Omar.The winners were Hana and Omar.
A verb and a that-clauseThe survey found, that most teens sleep too little.The survey found that most teens sleep too little.
“That” (or “because,” “if,” “when”) and its clauseThe survey found that, most teens sleep too little.The survey found that most teens sleep too little.
A preposition and its objectStorks rest along, the shores of the lake.Storks rest along the shores of the lake.
A verb and a prepositional phrase that completes itStorks rest, along the shores of the lake.Storks rest along the shores of the lake.
Two verbs that share one subject (compound predicate)She wrote the report, and sent it to the director.She wrote the report and sent it to the director.
Two items joined by “and” / “or”The museum displays statues, and jewelry.The museum displays statues and jewelry.
A FANBOYS word and what follows itThe road was closed but, the bus found another route.The road was closed, but the bus found another route.
The last adjective and its nounIt was a long, difficult, journey.It was a long, difficult journey.
Cumulative adjectivesa small, wooden chesta small wooden chest
“Such as” / “like” / “including” and the examplesSpices such as, cumin and coriander are common.Spices such as cumin and coriander are common.
A noun and essential information after itThe plants, that received extra light, grew faster.The plants that received extra light grew faster.
The main clause and a dependent clause that follows it (usually)The bus was late, because the road was flooded.The bus was late because the road was flooded.
Strip the sentence to its skeleton

Cross out any introduction and any extra information, then read the skeleton: subject → verb → object or complement. If a choice puts any mark between those parts, it is wrong. Example: “The researchers who studied the grain samples from the Fayoum concluded that …” — no comma can stand between “Fayoum” and “concluded.”

الأقل غالبًا هو الصح

لو مش متأكد، جرّب تشيل الفاصلة واقرا: هل الجملة اتقطعت بين الفاعل والفعل أو الفعل والمفعول؟ لو الفاصلة بتقطع حاجة من دول يبقى غلط. الامتحان بيحب يحط فواصل زيادة عشان يوقعك، فالاختيار اللي من غير ترقيم كتير بيبقى هو الصح — بس اتأكد إنه ما بيعملش جملتين لازقين في بعض.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — introductory clause

Text: Farmers in the valley had waited months for rain. When the rains finally arrived in late October ______ began planting wheat and barley on the same day.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) October; farmers
  • B) October, farmers
  • C) October farmers,
  • D) October. Farmers

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon needs a complete sentence before it; “When the rains finally arrived in late October” is a dependent clause.
  • B) ✓ Correct: An introductory dependent clause is followed by a comma, then the main clause “farmers began planting …”.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Two errors: no comma after the introduction, and a comma between the subject “farmers” and its verb “began.”
  • D) ✗ Wrong: The period leaves “When the rains finally arrived in late October.” as a fragment.

Worked example 2 — coordinate adjectives

Text: Visitors to the monastery are warned to wear good shoes. The path from the village to the gate is a narrow ______ that can be dangerous after rain.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) winding path
  • B) winding, path
  • C) , winding path
  • D) , winding path,

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Trap: “a narrow and winding path” sounds natural, and so does “a winding, narrow path,” so the adjectives are coordinate and need a comma between them.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: The comma comes between the two adjectives, never between the last adjective and the noun.
  • C) ✓ Correct: Both tests work, so “narrow, winding” are coordinate adjectives: “a narrow, winding path that can be dangerous …” ✓
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Trap: the comma after “path” separates the noun from its essential that-clause.

Worked example 3 — items in a list

Text: The village school had not been repaired for years, so the student council organized a work weekend. Over two days, the volunteers planted trees, cleaned the canal banks ______ painted the walls of every classroom.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) , and
  • B) and,
  • C) , and,
  • D) ; and

Answer: A

  • A) ✓ Correct: The third item in a list of three verb phrases is introduced by “, and.” The serial comma matches the comma already used after “trees.”
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Trap: a comma after “and” separates the conjunction from the item it introduces.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Trap: the second comma separates “and” from “painted.”
  • D) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon is used between list items only when the items already contain commas (SAT/ACT extra rule). These items do not.

Worked example 4 — compound predicate

Text: The novelist Salma Nabil publishes her books in both Arabic and English. For her newest book, the author wrote the first draft in Arabic ______ translated it into English herself.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) , and,
  • B) ; and
  • C) and
  • D) , and

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Trap: two extra commas, one on each side of “and.”
  • B) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon needs a complete sentence on both sides; “translated it into English herself” has no subject.
  • C) ✓ Correct: One subject (“the author”) has two verbs (“wrote … and translated …”). This is a compound predicate, so no comma comes before “and.”
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Trap: this is the most common wrong answer. A comma + “and” joins two complete sentences; here the second part has no subject of its own.
Classic traps
  • The lonely comma between a long subject and its verb, or between a verb and its object.
  • The semicolon after an introduction: “Because …; the …”.
  • Comma after the joining word: “but, the …” / “such as, cumin …” / “that, most …”.
  • Comma before a second verb with the same subject: “wrote the draft, and translated it.”
  • Comma between cumulative adjectives (“a small, wooden chest”) or after the last adjective (“a long, steep, climb”).
  • Comma after the last list item: “salt, pepper, and cumin, are …”.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Find the main subject and verb first. No single mark may separate them.
  • Is there an introduction before the main subject? Put the comma right before that subject.
  • Is there a list of three or more? Commas between all items; nothing after the last one.
  • Two adjectives? Try the swap and “and” tests.
  • Before “and”: is there a new subject after it? Yes → comma. No → no comma.

Practice

Ten original Digital SAT-style questions on introductions, lists, adjectives and the no-comma zones.

Remember
  • Comma after an introductory clause or phrase — right before the main subject.
  • Comma before FANBOYS only when it joins two complete sentences; never after it.
  • Lists of three or more: comma after each item except the last. Two items: no comma.
  • Coordinate adjectives (swap and “and” tests work) take a comma; cumulative adjectives do not.
  • Never a single comma between subject–verb, verb–object, a preposition and its object, or before “that.”
