Transitions: Choosing the Right Linking Word

How to choose the transition word that shows the true relationship between two ideas — logic families, exact meanings, the same-meaning trick and when to delete the transition.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Transition questions belong to Production of Writing, the reporting category that is 38–43% of the ACT English score. They test cohesion: does the linking word tell the reader the true relationship between two ideas? A transition is a signpost. If the signpost says "turn left" (however) when the road goes straight on (in addition), the reader gets lost.

On the test, a transition word or phrase is underlined and the choices are usually NO CHANGE plus three other transitions. Very often all four choices are grammatically correct. Only the logic decides. Sometimes one choice removes the transition completely.

Where the transition appearsWhat it looks like
At the start of a sentence"__However,__ the plan failed."
After a semicolon (joining two independent clauses)"The road was closed; __as a result,__ we were late."
In the middle of a clause"The plan, __however,__ failed."
As a conjunction joining two clauses"She trained every day, __but__ she lost the race."
Combined with the first words of the sentence"__For example, the__ oven has cracked" — so that one choice can drop the transition and keep "The"

Some transition questions ask you only to read the sentence before. Others need the whole paragraph, because the relationship is with an idea two sentences back. Never judge a transition from its own sentence alone.

قبل ما تبص على الاختيارات

غطّي الاختيارات بإيدك. اقرا الجملة اللي قبل والجملة اللي فيها الكلمة، وقول العلاقة بينهم بكلامك: عكس؟ نتيجة؟ مثال؟ إضافة؟ خطوة بعدها؟ حاجة حصلت رغم اللي قبلها؟ بعد كده بس دوّر على الكلمة اللي من نفس العيلة.

The method: name the relationship before you look

  1. Cover the transition and the choices. If you read "However" first, your brain will try to make a contrast fit.
  2. Read the sentence before and the sentence with the transition. If the link is unclear, read the whole paragraph.
  3. Fill in this frame: "The second idea is ______ the first." (the opposite of / the result of / an example of / another point like / the next step after / surprising after / a stronger version of)
  4. Choose the family from the table below.
  5. Check the exact meaning of the word in that family — "instead," "otherwise" and "meanwhile" have very narrow meanings.
  6. Use the same-meaning trick to cross out twins (see below).
  7. Consider no transition at all. If every word forces a false link, the choice that deletes it is right.

The logic families

FamilyThe second idea …Common wordsMini example
Additionadds another point of the same kind, in the same directionalso, in addition, moreover, furthermore, besides, additionallyThe flat is close to the metro; moreover, the rent is low.
Contrastgoes against or differs from the first ideahowever, but, yet, in contrast, on the other hand, whereas, by comparisonCairo is crowded; in contrast, Siwa is quiet.
Cause → effectis the result of the first ideatherefore, as a result, consequently, thus, so, for this reason, accordingly, in this wayThe bridge was closed; therefore, we took the ferry.
Exampleis one case of the general idea before itfor example, for instance, to illustrate, specificallyMany birds migrate; for example, storks fly over Sinai each autumn.
Sequence / timeis the next step or a later momentfirst, then, next, later, afterwards, eventually, finally, meanwhile, subsequentlyMix the flour and water. Then knead the dough.
Concessionhappens even though the first idea suggests the oppositenevertheless, nonetheless, even so, still, despite this, regardlessThe exam was hard; nevertheless, she scored full marks.
Emphasismakes the first idea stronger, or goes even furtherin fact, indeed, above all, especially, in particularThe old bridge was narrow; in fact, two cars could not pass.

Three smaller families also appear: restatement (in other words, that is, in short) says the same idea again more simply; similarity (similarly, likewise, in the same way) shows two things work alike; conclusion (ultimately, in the end, overall) sums up.

Exact meanings: the words students confuse

Knowing the family is not always enough. Some transitions have a narrow meaning, and the ACT likes to test it.

WordExact meaning✓ / ✗
insteadin place of the thing just said not to happen (it needs a negative or a rejected option before it)✓ He did not take the bus; instead, he walked. ✗ He took the bus; instead, he arrived early.
otherwiseif not / if that does not happen✓ Book early; otherwise, the seats will be gone. ✗ The seats were cheap; otherwise, we bought four.
meanwhileat the same time (often in another place)✓ Ali cooked the rice; meanwhile, Omar set the table. ✗ The film ended; meanwhile, we went home.
eventually / finallyafter a long time or a long process / last in a series✓ The engine failed twice but eventually started. ✗ Eventually, the first step is to read the question.
similarly / likewisethe second thing works in the same way as the first✓ Camels store fat for the desert; similarly, some cacti store water. ✗ Camels store fat; similarly, horses cannot.
in fact / indeedgoes further than the first idea in the same direction✓ The test was long; in fact, it took four hours. ✗ The test was long; in fact, it was short.
in other wordssays the same idea again in simpler words — no new information✓ The drug is lethal; in other words, it can kill. ✗ The drug is lethal; in other words, it is expensive.
for examplethe second idea is one case of the general claim✓ Some metals are soft; for example, gold bends easily. ✗ Gold bends easily; for example, some metals are soft. (reversed — the general claim must come first)

Contrast or concession? The subtle difference

Both families "go against" something, so students mix them up. Contrast puts two different things side by side (Cairo is hot; Alexandria is cooler). Concession says a result happened even though the first idea made us expect the opposite.

  • ✓ The team lost every match; nevertheless, the fans kept coming. (we expect fans to stop — they did not: concession)
  • ✓ My brother loves football; in contrast, I prefer chess. (two different people compared: contrast)
  • ✗ My brother loves football; nevertheless, I prefer chess. (my preference is not surprising because of his — no expectation is broken)
  • ✓ The tray was heavy; even so, the boy did not drop it. (concession)
  • ✗ The tray was heavy; therefore, the boy did not drop it. (heaviness does not cause safe carrying — reversed logic)

"However" can do both jobs, which is why it is so often correct — and so often a trap. Never choose "However" just because it sounds academic; first prove there is a real contrast or a broken expectation.

The "two choices mean the same" trick

A transition question has exactly one right answer. So if two choices mean the same thing in that sentence, they must be equally right or equally wrong — and since they cannot both be right, both are wrong. Cross them out.

  • Choices: therefore / thus / however / in fact → "therefore" and "thus" are twins; the answer is "however" or "in fact."
  • Choices: NO CHANGE (Nevertheless) / Even so / In this way / On the other hand → "Nevertheless" and "Even so" are twins; both go.
  • Choices: as a result / consequently / for this reason / similarly → three cause words are triplets; only "similarly" is left, so check it carefully.

Be careful: this trick works only when the words truly mean the same in this sentence. "However" and "nevertheless" are close but not always identical (however can show simple contrast; nevertheless always shows concession). Use the trick to narrow the choices, then confirm with the logic.

خدعة الكلمتين اللي بنفس المعنى

لو لقيت اختيارين معناهم واحد زي therefore و thus، أو nevertheless و even so، شيلهم الاتنين. السؤال ليه إجابة واحدة بس، فمستحيل الاتنين يكونوا صح.

When the best transition is no transition

Some choices drop the transition completely, for example "The" instead of "For example, the," or the instruction "DELETE the underlined portion." Choose it when:

  • Every transition word forces a false relationship. If the sentence is not a contrast, not a result, not an example, the plain sentence is better than a wrong signpost.
  • The logic is already shown by another word. "Because the road was closed, therefore we were late" doubles the cause; drop "therefore."
  • The sentence simply continues a description or story and the reader needs no signpost.
  • ✗ We reached the village at noon. For example, the streets were empty.
  • ✓ We reached the village at noon. The streets were empty.
  • ✗ Although it rained, however, the match continued. (although already shows concession)
  • ✓ Although it rained, the match continued.

The ACT does not prefer the short answer automatically. A deleted transition is right only when the logic is clear without it. If the reader really needs to know "this is a result," keep a result word.

A note on punctuation. Words like however, therefore and nevertheless are not conjunctions, so they cannot join two complete sentences with only a comma. Use a full stop or a semicolon (which joins two independent clauses): "The bridge was closed; therefore, we took the ferry." If the four choices differ only in punctuation, it is a punctuation question, not a transition question.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — cause and effect

Passage: The new metro line cut the journey from Heliopolis to downtown from an hour to twenty minutes. __However,__ many families who used to drive to work started taking the train.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) As a result,
  • C) For example,
  • D) In other words,

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: The automatic "However" trap: faster trips and more passengers go together; there is no contrast.
  • B) ✓ Correct: A faster journey caused families to switch — cause → effect.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Families switching is not one case of a shorter journey.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: The second sentence adds new information; it does not restate the first.

Worked example 2 — concession and the same-meaning trick

Passage: The village well was more than two kilometres from my aunt's house, and the path was steep and stony. __Consequently,__ she walked there twice a day for years and never once complained.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) Thus,
  • C) Even so,
  • D) For instance,

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "Consequently" says the long, steep path caused her not to complain — reversed logic. A hard path makes us expect complaints. Also, A and B are twins.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Twin of A (cause → effect) — both must be wrong.
  • C) ✓ Correct: She never complained despite the hard path — a result that breaks the expectation: concession.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Not complaining is not an example of the path being steep.

Worked example 3 — delete the transition

Passage: Our class visited the Egyptian Museum on Thursday. __Therefore, the__ guide began with the statue of King Khafre and then led us upstairs.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) In contrast, the
  • C) The
  • D) Similarly, the

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: The visit did not cause the guide's choice of where to begin — a false cause.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Nothing contrasts with the visit.
  • C) ✓ Correct: The second sentence simply continues the story; no signpost is needed.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: The guide's route is not similar to the visit.

Worked example 4 — exact meaning: instead vs in fact

Passage: The engineers did not repair the old wooden bridge. __In fact,__ they built a new steel one a few metres downstream.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) Instead,
  • C) Similarly,
  • D) For example,

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "In fact" would mean the second idea goes further in the same direction, but building a new bridge is not a stronger form of "not repairing"; it is what they did in its place.
  • B) ✓ Correct: After a negative (did not repair), "instead" introduces what was done in place of it — exact meaning.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Building a new bridge is not like not repairing the old one.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: A new bridge is not an example of not repairing.
Classic traps
  • The automatic "However": choosing contrast because it sounds formal, without checking for a real contrast.
  • Reversed cause: "therefore" placed before the cause instead of the effect (the oven cracked → she buys bread, not the other way round).
  • Contrast vs concession: "in contrast" compares two things; "nevertheless" means a result happened despite an expectation.
  • Twins: two choices that mean the same are both wrong.
  • Wrong direction of example: "for example" must come after the general claim, not before it.
  • Narrow words: "instead" needs something rejected before it; "meanwhile" means at the same time; "in other words" adds nothing new.
  • Double signpost: although … however, because … therefore — one is enough.
  • Ignoring the deletion choice: if every word gives a false link, no transition is best.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Cover the choices and say the relationship in a few words: opposite, result, example, more, next, surprising, stronger.
  • Cross out twins first — it often leaves only two choices.
  • Test the last two in the frame "The second idea is ___ the first."
  • Read one sentence further if the link is still unclear; the relationship may be with the whole paragraph.
  • If nothing fits, choose the choice with no transition.
أشهر غلطة

ما تختارش However علشان شكلها حلوة. لازم يكون فيه عكس حقيقي. ولو الجملة التانية نتيجة للأولى استخدم therefore أو as a result، ولو حصلت رغم الأولى استخدم nevertheless أو even so.

Practice

Read the two essays and answer the 10 questions. Each underlined portion is numbered. Every question tests the logic of a transition.

Remember
  • Name the relationship BEFORE you look at the choices.
  • Learn the families: addition, contrast, cause–effect, example, sequence, concession, emphasis.
  • Check exact meanings: instead, otherwise, meanwhile, in other words, for example.
  • Two choices that mean the same are both wrong.
  • If every transition gives a false link, delete it.
