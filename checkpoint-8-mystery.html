Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Mystery Stories

How to write a gripping mystery story for Paper 2 — a puzzle, clues, a red herring and a satisfying reveal — with features, a six-stage plan, phrases, vocabulary and a full-mark 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is a mystery story?

A mystery story is built around an unanswered question: something is missing, something has changed, something cannot be explained — and an ordinary, curious person decides to find out why. The reader follows the clues with the character and tries to solve the puzzle before the end.

Not every mystery is a crime. A general mystery does not need a detective, a victim or a criminal. The puzzle can be a strange key, a disappearing object, a letter from someone nobody knows, a locked room, a family secret or a neighbour who behaves oddly. What matters is that something is unknown, someone wants to know it, and the story earns the answer through clues, doubt and discovery. (If your idea involves a crime, suspects and someone guilty, use the Crime Mystery pack instead — it adds alibis, motives and interrogation.)

General mysteryCrime mysteryHorror
The central questionWhat is this? What happened? Why?Who did it?Is it real? Will it get them?
The investigatoran ordinary curious person (often you, in first person)a detective, police officer or amateur sleutha frightened victim, not an investigator
Is there a culprit?not always — the answer may be kind, sad or surprisingyes: a person who is guiltya threat, often unexplained
How it endsthe puzzle is solved and the investigator has changed a littlethe culprit is revealed. Justice is doneunresolved. A lingering chill

Purpose: to make the reader curious and keep them guessing, then satisfy them with an answer that makes sense. Audience: the examiner — a sharp reader who enjoys spotting clues and wants a reveal that is surprising but fair.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Paper 2 is the Fiction paper. It lasts 1 hour 10 minutes and is worth 50 marks: Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks).
  • Section B is one story task. It is usually linked by theme to the fiction extract you read in Section A, and it gives you bullet prompts (for example: the character, the place, what happens, how it ends).
  • The suggested time for Section B is about 35 minutes, and planning space is given. Planning is not marked, but it is where good stories are won.
  • The task will not say "write a mystery story". It gives you a theme. YOU decide that this genre is the best way to answer it — and then you stay inside the genre from the first line to the last, while still answering every bullet.
  • The same format is used in the Stage 8 Progression Test you sit at school, so everything on this page works for both.

Themes that suit a mystery story: a discovery, a secret, something lost or found, an unexpected visitor or letter, an old object, a family member’s past, a new neighbour, a place that has changed, a promise, "things are not what they seem", an unexpected event on a trip or holiday.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Your story is marked in five categories (official names first, with older names in brackets). Here is what the top band looks like for a mystery story, in our own words.

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in this genre
Creation of texts5The story answers the theme and every bullet. The puzzle is clear early. The clues are fair. The reader is kept guessing. The solution is satisfying and makes sense. The investigator’s viewpoint is consistent.
Vocabulary and language3Precise words for noticing and thinking (peered, examined, puzzled, faint, dented, concealed) and for mood. Imagery that makes the key place and object vivid.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7Six clear stages: strange opening → the puzzle stated → characters → clues and a red herring → discovery → reveal and reflection. Clues planted early are paid off at the end.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7A mix of sentence types for effect (short sentences for discoveries, complex sentences for reasoning), questions that show the investigator thinking, and accurate, varied punctuation including speech.
Word structure (spelling)3Ambitious words spelt correctly (mysterious, suspicious, mysteriously, disappointment, neighbour, curious, conscious). No homophone slips (where/were, knew/new, past/passed).
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. A very short answer (about 21-60 words) can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example, a diary entry or a newspaper report instead of a story), Creation of texts is capped at 1 and Structure of texts is capped too.
  • If the story drifts away from the task's theme, you lose Creation marks even if the writing is beautiful — keep the bullet prompts in front of you.
  • Marking is best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best.

2. Features of a mystery story

A fair mystery is like a game with the reader. Use these features — and remember that the reader must be able to solve it if they notice the right details.

FeatureWhat it meansExample sentence
A central puzzle (enigma)The one question that drives the whole story — state it clearly and earlyThere were twelve flats in the building. So what did the label 13 open?
An ordinary investigatorNot a detective — a curious person who cannot let the question goI tried it in every lock I could find.
A companionSomeone to think aloud with, who sometimes notices what the investigator missesIt was Mariam who noticed the letterboxes.
CluesSmall, meaningful details for the reader to noticepainted the same grey as the wall so that your eyes slid straight past it
A red herringA misleading clue or suspicious character that points the wrong way — but is fairly explained laterMr Halim watched us with narrowed eyes… he kept pigeons up there.
SuspenseThe answer stays just out of reach. Each discovery raises a new questionIt had no number and no name. It had a keyhole.
An evocative settingA place that holds the mystery and shapes the moodthe dented metal letterboxes in the dim lobby
A twist or revealThe answer changes how we see earlier detailsThe Helper was the quiet old man with the grey cardigans.
Change in the investigatorSolving it leaves them different from how they startedI hung the little key on a string around my neck.

Language and layout features every story needs (these earn the Structure and Grammar marks):

FeatureHow to use it in a mysteryExample
A titleHint at the puzzle without giving away the answerFlat 13 / The Locked Drawer / The Thirteenth Letterbox
ParagraphsNew paragraph for each discovery. One-line paragraphs for key momentsMy hands were not quite steady when I turned the key.
DialogueQuestions, short answers, a character who avoids answering“Looking for something?” he asked.
Show, don’t tell (STEAL)Show curiosity and suspicion through actions, looks and what characters avoid sayingHe did not look convinced, and neither, I am sure, did I.
Rhetorical questionsShow the investigator thinking — but only two or threeThirteen what? And why had Grandad hidden it?
Sentence varietyLong sentences to reason things out, short sentences for each discoveryEverything except the key.
Varied punctuationColon before a list or a reveal. Pair of dashes for extra information. Inverted commas for written messagesInside were envelopes: hundreds of them.
Past tense, one point of viewFirst person (I) lets the reader discover clues at the same time as the narratorKeep the same choice from start to finish.
Play fair with the reader

Plant the real clue EARLY and quietly — hide it in a list or a description so the reader passes over it ("forty years of newspapers, a radio, a cupboard full of grey cardigans"). Then, at the reveal, bring that detail back. The reader should think: "Of course! It was there all the time." An answer that comes from nowhere (a stranger explains everything, or it was magic) feels like cheating and loses Creation and Structure marks.

3. How to structure your story

First, plan with G.C.S.P.P. (about 5 minutes):

  1. G — Genre: general mystery. Write the puzzle in one line (What does the key labelled 13 open?) AND the answer in one line (a hidden letterbox full of thank-you letters). Know the answer before you start writing.
  2. C — Characters: 2-3 only. An investigator with a reason to care, a companion, and one character who seems suspicious (your red herring).
  3. S — Setting: one main place that holds the secret — a flat, a library, a school storeroom, a village shop. If the task gives a setting, use it.
  4. P — Point of view: first person (I) works best — the reader finds each clue at the same moment as the narrator. Past tense.
  5. P — Plot: list your real clue, your red herring and your reveal, then write notes for the six stages below.

Then follow this paragraph-by-paragraph plan, based on the six-stage mystery structure from my writing book.

ParagraphStageWhat to writeLength
1Opening — something unusualStart with a small, unexplained detail or object. A small strange detail is more effective than a big obvious one.2-4 sentences
2Present the central mysteryState plainly what is missing, wrong or unexplained. Do not bury the question.2-4 sentences
3Introduce the investigator and othersWho is looking, why they care, and who else is around. A line of behaviour per character is enough. Plant your real clue here, quietly.3-6 sentences
4Clues and a red herringThe investigator follows a wrong lead (a suspicious person or place). Explain it fairly when it leads nowhere.5-8 sentences
5Build tension through discoveryA new clue — often noticed by the companion — opens the real path. Short sentences. The answer is close.3-6 sentences
6Reveal the truthThe answer, shown through a discovery, not a long explanation. Link it back to the early clue.4-7 sentences
7Resolution and reflectionWhat the investigator does next and how they have changed. End with a short, memorable line that echoes the title or opening.1-4 sentences
MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph. Write the puzzle and its answer in two lines.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). By about minute 15 you should be following your red herring (paragraph 4).
30–35Proofread: spelling of your ambitious words, full stops (no comma splices), speech punctuation, capital letters, one tense and one point of view.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Adapt these — change the names, places and objects to fit your story. Use one or two per stage. Do not stack them.

Openings (something unusual)

  • It was supposed to be an ordinary [morning / Friday / visit] — until [the letter / the knock / the discovery] changed everything.
  • The first sign of trouble was small, almost invisible, but impossible to ignore.
  • The [diary / key / photograph] had vanished from [the locked drawer / its usual shelf], leaving no trace behind.
  • Something felt wrong, though [Name] couldn’t yet explain why.
  • Out of nowhere, [a knock / a shadow / a phone call] broke the silence.

Presenting the puzzle

  • No explanation seemed possible — yet there had to be one.
  • The question hung over all of us: [what had happened to it / where had they gone / who had sent it]?
  • Nothing had been taken, nothing had been broken, and nothing made sense.
  • The puzzle only grew deeper with every passing hour.
  • Dust lay thick on every surface except one.

The investigator and the characters

  • I wasn’t a detective, but I couldn’t ignore what had happened.
  • [Companion]’s voice was tight with worry, while [other character] avoided my eyes entirely.
  • Each person reacted differently: one stayed too calm, another grew restless, a third wouldn’t speak at all.
  • When I asked about [the missing object], [Character] hesitated a moment too long.

Clues and red herrings

  • At first glance, it seemed unimportant. It meant everything.
  • A [torn note / muddy footprint / broken lock] lay there, but it led nowhere at all.
  • What looked like the answer turned out to be only a distraction.
  • The pieces almost fit. Almost, but not quite.
  • Every new detail twisted the mystery tighter, not looser.

The reveal

  • At last, the pieces fitted together.
  • The truth had been hidden in plain sight the entire time.
  • Everything suddenly became clear, and I wondered how I had missed it before.
  • One final detail changed everything I thought I knew.

Endings (reflection)

  • The mystery was solved, but some questions lingered long after.
  • Life returned to normal. Almost.
  • Some secrets, once found, never fully let go of the person who found them.
  • [The object / place] still had a story to tell — and now it was mine.
Word / phraseMeaning / use
enigma (n.)a puzzle or mystery — The locked box remained an enigma.
cryptic (adj.)with a hidden meaning that is hard to understand — a cryptic note in the margin
baffling (adj.)impossible to understand or explain — The timing was utterly baffling.
elusive (adj.)difficult to find or catch — The answer stayed elusive.
unravel (v.)to solve or untangle little by little — Slowly, we began to unravel the secret.
shrouded (adj.)covered or hidden — a house shrouded in mist
inscrutable (adj.)impossible to read or understand — His inscrutable expression gave nothing away.
salient (adj.)most noticeable or important — The salient detail was the one nobody noticed.
ambiguity (n.)the quality of having more than one possible meaning — The ambiguity of her answer worried me.
infer (v.)to work something out from evidence — From the dust, I inferred that nobody had been there for weeks.
painstaking (adj.)done with great care and thoroughness — a painstaking search of every drawer
conceal (v.)to hide on purpose — The letterbox was concealed by grey paint.
misleading (adj.)giving the wrong idea — The muddy footprints were misleading.
intrigue (n./v.)curiosity / to make someone curious — The label intrigued me.
portentous (adj.)suggesting that something important is about to happen — a portentous silence

5. Strong sample answer

Mystery story — Flat 13539 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: In the extract you have read, a character finds an old object that once belonged to a member of their family. Write a story about someone who finds something unexpected that leads them to discover a secret. In your story you should: introduce the character and the place where the discovery happens, describe what they find and how they try to understand it, and explain what the secret turns out to be and how it affects them.

The key was smaller than my little finger, and it was wrapped in a handkerchief at the very back of Grandad’s desk drawer, as if it had been hidden from someone. A cardboard label hung from it on a piece of string. In Grandad’s careful capitals, it said: 13.

“Thirteen what?” asked my sister, Mariam, peering over my shoulder.

That was the problem. Grandad’s building on Nour Street had twelve flats — I had counted the doorbells a thousand times as a child — and his was number 7. There was no Flat 13. There never had been.

For three days, Mum, Mariam and I had been packing his life into boxes: forty years of newspapers, a radio that only played one station and a cupboard full of identical grey cardigans. Everything in the flat was exactly what I expected. Everything except the key.

I tried it in every lock I could find. It did not fit the front door, the kitchen cupboards or the old wooden chest under the bed. Mariam suggested the roof room, which had always been locked, and which Mr Halim from Flat 2 had always been strangely interested in. When we climbed the stairs, he appeared in his doorway at once, watching us with narrowed eyes.

“Looking for something?” he asked.

“Just Grandad’s things,” I said. He did not look convinced, and neither, I am sure, did I.

The roof room was a disappointment; its padlock was far too big for our tiny key. Through the dusty window, I could see nothing but broken chairs, a satellite dish and a row of wooden cages. Mr Halim, it turned out, kept pigeons up there. That was his only secret.

It was Mariam who noticed the letterboxes. We were sitting on the bottom step of the lobby, tired and defeated, when she pointed at the dented metal boxes on the wall. There were twelve, neatly numbered. Below them was a thirteenth, painted the same grey as the wall so that your eyes slid straight past it. It had no number and no name. It had a keyhole.

My hands were not quite steady when I turned the key.

Inside were envelopes: hundreds of them. Some were addressed in shaky handwriting, some in pencil, a few in a child’s crayon, and every one said the same thing: ‘To the Helper, Flat 13’. I opened one at random. ‘Thank you for paying for my medicine when I could not,’ it said. ‘I never knew who you were.’

Suddenly, I remembered the stories the neighbours used to tell: bags of bread that appeared on doorsteps every Ramadan, school fees that were mysteriously paid, a broken stair rail that was fixed overnight. Everyone on Nour Street had wondered who the Helper was. Nobody had ever guessed that it was the quiet old man in Flat 7 with the grey cardigans.

We did not tell anyone that afternoon. We simply carried the letters home and read every single one.

The next morning, before school, I left a bag of warm bread on Mr Halim’s doorstep. Then I locked the thirteenth letterbox again and hung the little key on a string around my neck.

Flat 13, it seemed, still had someone living in it.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — every bullet is answered: the character and place (paragraphs 1-4), what he finds and how he tries to understand it (1, 5-10), the secret and how it affects him (11-15). The puzzle is stated clearly in paragraph 3 ("There was no Flat 13"), and the reader is kept guessing until paragraph 11.
  • Creation — fair play: the real clue is planted early and quietly (the grey cardigans in paragraph 4, "as if it had been hidden from someone" in paragraph 1) and paid off in the reveal ("the quiet old man in Flat 7 with the grey cardigans"). The red herring, Mr Halim, is fairly explained (pigeons).
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — precise noticing words: peering, narrowed eyes, dented, painted the same grey as the wall, eyes slid straight past it, shaky handwriting, mysteriously paid. Warm, domestic word choices (handkerchief, cardigans, warm bread) suit a kind secret.
  • Vocabulary — imagery and effect: "packing his life into boxes" (metaphor), the personal details of the letters, and the final line that personifies the flat as still "living".
  • Structure 7/7 — the six stages: unusual opening (1) → puzzle stated (2-3) → investigator, family and setting (4) → red herring (5-8) → discovery by the companion (9-10) → reveal (11-12) → resolution and change (13-15). Single-line paragraphs mark key moments ("My hands were not quite steady when I turned the key.").
  • Structure — cohesion: "13" runs from the label in paragraph 1 to the title-echo in the last line. "Everything … Everything except the key" closes paragraph 4 with a turn. The narrator ends by taking over the Helper’s role, showing change.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — sentence variety: rhetorical-style question in speech ("Thirteen what?"), short sentences for discoveries ("It had a keyhole."), complex sentences with relative clauses ("which had always been locked, and which Mr Halim … had always been strangely interested in"), repetition for effect ("There was no Flat 13. There never had been.").
  • Grammar & punctuation — range used accurately: colons for a list ("packing his life into boxes: forty years of newspapers…"), emphasis ("Inside were envelopes: hundreds of them.") and extra information ("it said: 13."), a pair of dashes for extra information ("— I had counted the doorbells a thousand times as a child —"), a semicolon joining two related sentences ("The roof room was a disappointment; its padlock was far too big…"), a pair of commas for extra information ("and neither, I am sure, did I"), inverted commas for written words quoted from the letters, and speech on new lines.
  • Spelling 3/3 — ambitious words spelt correctly: handkerchief, identical, cardigans, disappointment, satellite, neighbours (British spelling), mysteriously, Ramadan, defeated. Apostrophes correct (Grandad’s, child’s, Halim’s).

6. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
Starting to write before knowing the answerThe story wanders and the ending is rushed or confusingPlan the puzzle AND the solution in two lines before you write.
The answer comes from nowhere (a stranger explains everything, or "it was magic")Unfair to the reader. Weak Creation and StructurePlant the real clue early. Let the investigator (or companion) find the answer.
Too many clues and charactersConfusing in a 35-minute storyOne real clue, one red herring, 2-3 characters.
The puzzle is never stated clearlyThe reader does not know what to be curious aboutState the question plainly by paragraph 2 or 3.
A long explanation paragraph at the endTelling, not showing. The tension dropsReveal through a discovery or one line of dialogue, then reflect briefly.
Turning it into a crime with no planning (suddenly there is a murderer)Changes genre halfway. May become unsuitableKeep the secret human-sized: lost, hidden, forgotten, misunderstood.
Comma splices: "I opened the box, it was empty."Grammar & punctuationFull stop, semicolon or connective: I opened the box; it was empty.
Too many rhetorical questions ("Who was it? Why? Where? How?")Looks repetitive and childishTwo or three thoughtful questions in the whole story.
Checklist before you hand in
  • My story answers the theme and EVERY bullet prompt.
  • The puzzle is clear by paragraph 2 or 3, and I knew the answer before I started.
  • I planted at least one real clue early and used one fair red herring.
  • My investigator does the solving (with help from a companion), not a stranger or luck.
  • My reveal links back to an earlier detail, and my ending shows how the investigator has changed.
  • I used short sentences for discoveries and complex sentences for reasoning.
  • I used a colon, a semicolon and a pair of dashes correctly, and my speech is on new lines.
  • I checked spelling of ambitious words and stayed in past tense and one point of view.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: The extract you read describes a character who receives an unexpected message. Write a story about someone who receives something they cannot explain and decides to find out the truth. You should introduce the character, describe what they receive and how they investigate, and explain what they discover. Write a G.C.S.P.P. plan with your puzzle, clue, red herring and answer.

    Show answer
    G: general mystery. Puzzle: every Thursday, a library book is left on Salma’s desk with one word underlined. Answer: the school librarian, retiring and too shy to say goodbye, is sending her (the only student who visits) a message, one word a week. C: Salma (13, bookish), her friend Yara, Mr Tarek the grumpy PE teacher (red herring — he is seen near her classroom every Thursday, but only because he is collecting the football kit). S: the school library and Salma’s classroom. P: first person, past tense. Real clue (planted early): each book has the library stamp but no borrowing record. Plot — 1 Opening: the third book appears. 2 Puzzle: who, and what do the words mean? 3 Characters + early clue. 4 Red herring: Mr Tarek. 5 Discovery: Yara lines the words up — THANK YOU FOR ALWAYS COMING. 6 Reveal: the librarian’s handwriting on a farewell card in the staffroom matches the underlining. 7 Ending: Salma underlines a word in a book of her own and leaves it on the librarian’s desk.

  2. Practice task 2: Write a story called "The Unexpected Visitor". Plan it in 7 paragraph notes using the six-stage structure.

    Show answer
    1 Opening: a man in an old-fashioned suit knocks and asks for my grandmother by her maiden name. 2 Puzzle: Grandma has been dead for two years. Who is he, and why does he have a photo of her at 16? 3 Characters: me (narrator), Dad (who goes quiet), the visitor (polite, nervous, holding a tin box). Clue planted: he speaks with the same rare village accent as Grandma. 4 Red herring: Dad thinks he wants money. I find out he has visited three houses on our street asking questions. 5 Discovery: the tin contains letters in Grandma’s handwriting, signed "your sister". 6 Reveal: he is Grandma’s younger brother, separated from her as a child, who spent fifty years searching for her. 7 Ending: we invite him to dinner. Dad, for the first time, tells stories about Grandma — "Some doors open fifty years too late. This one still opened."

  3. Why does the writer use the colon in: "Inside were envelopes: hundreds of them."? Give all the possible effects.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: for emphasis — the colon makes the reader pause and stresses the surprising number. To give extra information about the envelopes. To create a dramatic pause that mirrors the narrator’s shock. To build suspense before the reveal of what the letters say. (Any two well-explained effects would usually earn the marks.)

  4. Find the red herring and the real clue in this short paragraph: "The cake had vanished from the fridge. My brother had chocolate on his sleeve, and he looked guilty. But I noticed that the fridge door was still open, and the dog’s bowl had been pushed right up against it."

    Show answer
    Red herring: the brother with chocolate on his sleeve who looks guilty (it points the reader the wrong way). Real clue: the open fridge door and the dog’s bowl pushed against it — suggesting the dog stood on the bowl and opened the fridge. A good mystery explains the red herring fairly later (for example, the chocolate came from a biscuit).

  5. Improve this ending: "Then a policeman came and explained that the old lady had taken the key and that was the mystery. The end."

    Show answer
    Example: I looked again at the photograph on her mantelpiece — the one I had passed a dozen times — and saw the key, tiny and brass, hanging from the chain around her neck. It had been there all along. I did not say anything. I simply put the box back on her shelf and closed the door quietly behind me. — Why it is better: the investigator solves it, the answer links to an earlier detail, there is no outside explainer, and the ending reflects instead of saying "The end".
