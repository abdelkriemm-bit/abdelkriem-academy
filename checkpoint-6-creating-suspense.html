Checkpoint 6 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

How to Create Suspense

How to keep the reader asking ‘what happens next?’ — strong verbs, short sentences, sound words, showing fear, keeping secrets, slow motion and cliffhangers — with a suspense build-up plan, ‘Why does the author use…?’ practice and a full sample story continuation.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is suspense?

Suspense is the ‘what happens next?’ feeling. The reader is not sure what is about to happen, and they suspect it might be exciting, frightening or surprising — so they cannot stop reading.

Good suspense does not need lots of action. It comes from waiting. The writer makes the reader wait a little longer before showing what is behind the door, who is following the character, or whether the plan will work. That wait is what keeps a story exciting.

In Paper 2 (Fiction), suspense is useful in almost every task: at the end of a story opening (a hook), in a continuation of an exciting or mysterious extract, and at the climax of a complete story. It is essential in adventure, mystery and horror stories. Suspense earns marks in Structure of texts (controlling pace and building to a climax), Grammar and punctuation (sentences chosen for effect) and Vocabulary and language (words that build tension). In the exam, about 30 minutes is suggested for the whole writing task (Paper 2, Section B, 25 marks). Many strong answers are about 250-350 words.

2. Techniques for building suspense

TechniqueExampleWhy it works
Strong action verbsShe sprinted down the corridor. He yanked the door open.The scene feels fast, urgent and alive.
Short sentencesA knock. Silence. Then another knock.They feel like a fast heartbeat and make each moment important.
One-line paragraphsTwo green eyes were shining in the corner.The white space around the line makes the reader stop and stare.
Sound words (onomatopoeia)Scrape. Drip. Creak.The reader hears the danger, so it feels close and real.
Show the character’s fear through the bodyHer hands were shaking. He held his breath and listened.The reader feels the fear too, instead of just being told.
Keep a secret (withhold information)Something moved behind the curtain, but she could not see what it was.Curiosity keeps the reader turning the page.
A dark or isolated settingA power cut, a storm, an empty corridor, a locked room.The character is alone and cannot see clearly, so anything could happen.
Slow motionShe reached for the handle. It was cold. She turned it slowly…Stretching a moment over several sentences makes the reader wait.
False alarmThe shape in the doorway was only a coat on a hook. She laughed — then the coat moved.The reader relaxes, then the tension returns even stronger.
ForeshadowingNobody noticed that the gate had been left unlocked.A small hint makes the reader expect trouble.
Questions in the narrationWho had opened the door? And why was it still warm?The reader asks the same questions as the character.
Time pressureThe bus would leave in two minutes, and the ticket was still missing.A race against the clock makes every second count.
CliffhangerShe switched on the light — and a hand touched hers in the dark.The scene stops at the most exciting point, so the reader must go on.
Do not use every technique at once

Choose four or five techniques and use them well. A paragraph full of ‘Suddenly!’, ‘BANG!’ and ‘AAARGH!’ is not suspenseful. It is just noisy. Quiet, slow details are often more frightening than loud ones.

3. The slow-motion technique

The most important moment of your story should take the longest to read. Stretch it out: describe each tiny action, what the character sees and hears, and what their body feels. Compare:

Fast (no suspense)Slow motion (suspense)
Omar opened the box and found a snake inside.Omar knelt beside the box. The lid was heavier than he expected. He lifted it one centimetre. Then two. Something inside rustled. He froze, his breath stuck in his chest. Slowly, very slowly, he lifted the lid all the way — and a pair of black eyes stared back at him.
  1. Break the action into tiny steps (knelt → lifted one centimetre → two → froze → lifted all the way).
  2. Add a sense after each step (heavy lid, a rustle, breath stuck).
  3. Add the character’s body reaction (froze, breath stuck in his chest).
  4. Use short sentences and a fragment (‘Then two.’).
  5. Reveal the thing at the very end of the paragraph, or not at all (a cliffhanger).

4. How to structure suspense: the tension ladder

Suspense works best when it climbs step by step. Use this ladder for the build-up and climax of a story, or for a whole continuation.

StepWhat happensExample (a power cut at night)
1. NormalShow the ordinary situation, calm and safe.Hana and Adam were playing cards by the window while the storm grew louder.
2. First hintSomething small is not quite right.The lights flickered and went out. Then came a scrape from upstairs.
3. InvestigateThe character decides to find out. Slow down here.They climbed the creaking stairs with one torch.
4. A worse hint (or false alarm)The danger seems closer or stranger.The storeroom that was always locked stood half open.
5. The reveal or cliffhangerShow what it was — or stop just before you do.Two green eyes shone in the corner…
6. Release (complete stories only)Tension drops: relief, a twist or a lesson.It was only Grandad’s cat with four kittens.
Opening or full story?

If the task asks for an opening, you can stop at step 5 with a cliffhanger. If it asks for a complete story or a continuation that should end, you need step 6 as well: let the reader breathe at the end, with a clear resolution.

5. Pace: long sentences and short sentences

Long sentences slow the reader down and build an atmosphere. Short sentences speed things up and hit hard. Suspense often uses a long sentence to build, then a short one to strike.

Example: The corridor stretched ahead of him, silent except for the hum of the old fridge and the drip of the kitchen tap, and every shadow seemed to lean a little closer as he passed. Then the hum stopped. Silence.

SentenceEffect
Long, complex sentence with many detailsSlows the pace, builds the mood, makes the reader wait.
Short simple sentence (‘Then the hum stopped.’)A sudden change · shock.
One-word sentence or fragment (‘Silence.’)Freezes the moment · the reader holds their breath.

6. Punctuation for suspense

  • Ellipsis ( … ) — creates a pause or suspense, leaves information incomplete, or shows that speech is interrupted: ‘The torch flickered once, twice… and steadied.’ / “I think there’s someone…”
  • Single dash ( – ) — gives emphasis to what comes after it, often a surprise at the end of a sentence, or shows an interruption in speech: ‘…a small sound came out of the dark – a tiny mew.’ / “Don’t open – ”
  • Question mark in the narration — shares the character’s worry: ‘What was making that noise?’
  • Exclamation mark — for a sudden shout or shock in speech. Use it rarely in narration, or it loses its power.

7. ‘Why does the author use…?’ — reading practice

Section A of Paper 2 can ask about the effect of a writer’s choices. Read this short original passage. The lines are numbered.

  1. The lift shuddered between the fourth and fifth floors.
  2. Then it stopped.
  3. Yassin pressed the button again and again, but nothing happened…
  4. Above his head, something scraped across the roof of the lift. Scratch. Scratch.
  5. Who else was in the shaft? He pressed his back against the cold metal wall.
  6. The light buzzed, dimmed – and went out.

  1. Why does the author use the short sentence ‘Then it stopped.’ in line 2?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to create a sudden shock after the movement in line 1 · to show that the lift has stopped without warning · to build suspense, because the reader wonders what will happen next · to slow the pace and make the moment feel frozen · to make the reader feel Yassin’s fear and helplessness.

  2. Why does the author use an ellipsis at the end of line 3?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to create a pause, as if we are waiting with Yassin · to build suspense · to show that nothing happens and the silence goes on · to suggest that the information is incomplete — we do not yet know why the lift has stopped · to make the reader expect something to happen next.

  3. Why does the author use onomatopoeia (‘Scratch. Scratch.’) in line 4?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to help the reader hear the sound, as if they are in the lift · to make the danger feel close and real · to build suspense, because the sound has no explanation · to show that the sound repeats slowly, which is more frightening · the short, separate words also slow the pace.

  4. Why does the author use a question in line 5?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show Yassin’s thoughts and fear · to make the reader ask the same question · to keep the identity of the thing secret (withholding information) · to build suspense and curiosity.

  5. Why does the author use a dash in line 6?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to emphasise the final words ‘and went out’ · to create a short pause before the worst moment · to make the ending sudden and dramatic · to leave Yassin — and the reader — in darkness at the most tense point (a cliffhanger).

  6. Why does the author write ‘Then it stopped.’ as a separate line/paragraph?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to make the moment stand out · to create a pause and a change of pace · to mark the start of the problem · to build suspense.

8. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Hints that something is wrong

  • Something was not right.
  • At first, I thought I had imagined it.
  • The silence was too complete.
  • Then I heard it again.
  • The door I had closed was open.

Showing fear

  • My heart hammered against my ribs.
  • Her breath caught in her throat.
  • A shiver crawled down his spine.
  • My legs refused to move.
  • He swallowed hard.

Slowing the moment

  • Slowly, very slowly,…
  • Inch by inch,…
  • For a moment, nobody moved.
  • Time seemed to stop.
  • She counted to three.

The reveal or cliffhanger

  • And that was when I saw it.
  • There, in the corner, was…
  • It was not a shadow.
  • Behind him, the door clicked shut.
  • Then the light went out.

9. Strong sample — a story continuation

In a continuation, the extract has already set up the characters, setting, point of view and mood. Your job is to pick up at the exact moment it stops and keep everything consistent. The extract here (summarised in the task) is in the third person and the past tense, so the continuation is too.

The Storeroom (mystery, story continuation)298 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: You have read the opening of a story. Hana and her cousin Adam are staying at their grandparents’ old farmhouse near Mansoura. During a storm the electricity goes off, and Grandad has gone out. The extract ends: ‘Then, from the room above them, came a slow, heavy scrape.’ Continue the story, using your own ideas.

For a moment, neither of them breathed.

“What was that?” Adam whispered. His voice cracked in the middle.

Hana gripped the torch. Its yellow beam trembled across the kitchen wall, over Teta’s copper pots, over the clock that had stopped at nine. Outside, the rain hammered on the windows like a thousand impatient fingers.

Scrape.

There it was again. Slower this time.

“Grandad’s upstairs,” Adam said hopefully.

“Grandad’s at the mosque,” Hana replied. “Remember?”

Without a word, they began to climb the stairs. Every step creaked under their feet. The torch flickered once, twice… and steadied. Hana’s heart was beating so hard that she was sure whatever was up there could hear it.

At the top, the corridor disappeared into darkness. The door of the old storeroom, the one that was always locked, stood half open.

Hana pushed it with one finger. It swung inwards with a long, low groan.

The beam swept across dusty sacks of rice, a broken chair and a wardrobe with a cracked mirror. Then it stopped on something that made Adam grab her arm.

Two green eyes were shining in the corner.

Nobody moved. The eyes blinked. Something shifted in the shadows, and a small sound came out of the dark – a tiny mew.

Hana lowered the torch. Inside an open sack, on a nest of torn newspaper, lay Grandad’s grey cat, Zeina. Pressed against her were four kittens, no bigger than teacups. The scraping had been Zeina dragging the sack into the corner to build her nest.

Adam let out a laugh that was half a sob. “We nearly ran away from a cat!”

Downstairs, the front door banged, and the lights blinked back on.

“Hana? Adam?” Grandad called. “Why is the storeroom open?”

The cousins looked at each other and grinned.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: a true continuation — it begins at the exact moment the extract stops (the scrape), and keeps the same characters (Hana, Adam, Grandad), setting (the farmhouse in a storm), third-person point of view, past tense and mysterious mood. The ending gives a believable explanation and a warm resolution.
  • Suspense techniques: the tension ladder is followed — first hint (the scrape) → a second, slower scrape → investigation (creaking stairs, flickering torch) → a worse hint (the always-locked storeroom is open) → the reveal (green eyes) → release (the cat and kittens). The identity of the noise is kept secret until paragraph 14.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise verbs (gripped, trembled, hammered, swept, shifted) · a simile for the storm (‘like a thousand impatient fingers’) · personification (the door swings open ‘with a long, low groan’) · fear shown through the body (‘neither of them breathed’, ‘His voice cracked’, her heart ‘beating so hard’) · onomatopoeia ‘Scrape.’
  • Structure of texts 7/7: very short paragraphs (‘Scrape.’, ‘Two green eyes were shining in the corner.’) control the pace and isolate the key moments · the slow-motion climax stretches over paragraphs 8–13 · Grandad’s return and the lights coming back on bring a clear resolution that links back to the extract (the power cut).
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: ellipsis for a pause/suspense (‘once, twice… and steadied’) · a single dash for emphasis before the surprise (‘– a tiny mew’) · a pair of commas for extra information (‘the one that was always locked’) · sentence fragments for effect (‘Slower this time.’) · a long sentence followed by a short one to change pace · dialogue punctuated correctly with a new line for each speaker.
  • Spelling 3/3: whispered, trembled, impatient, hopefully, creaked, flickered, wardrobe, newspaper — all correct.

10. Common mistakes and checklist

MistakeWhy it failsFix
‘Suddenly’ in every paragraphIt stops being sudden.Use it once at most. Try ‘Without warning,’ ‘Just then,’ or a one-line paragraph.
Lots of exclamation marks!!!Looks childish and loses its effect.Let the words create the shock. Use ! mainly in speech.
Revealing the monster in the first lineNothing left to wait for.Hints first · reveal late, or not at all.
All sentences shortIt becomes choppy and boring.Mix long, descriptive sentences with short ones.
Too much blood or violenceNot needed and not suitable · fear works better than gore.Use sounds, shadows and the character’s feelings.
‘It was all a dream’ endingCancels the suspense you built.Give a real explanation, a twist or a cliffhanger (for an opening).
In a continuation, changing the mood or the narratorLoses Creation of texts marks.Keep the same POV, tense, characters and mood as the extract.
Suspense checklist
  • My setting helps the tension (dark, quiet, isolated, stormy, trapped).
  • I showed my character’s fear through their body, not just ‘scared’.
  • I kept a secret from the reader for as long as possible.
  • I used slow motion at the most important moment.
  • I mixed long sentences with short ones and at least one one-line paragraph.
  • I used a sound word and a question in the narration.
  • I used an ellipsis or a dash correctly, for a pause or for emphasis.
  • My story ends with a release or a deliberate cliffhanger — not a dream.

11. Practice

  1. Rewrite this sentence to add suspense: ‘She opened the door.’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: She reached for the handle. It was ice-cold. Slowly, she turned it, and the door swung open with a long creak… The room was dark. And someone was breathing.

  2. Add a sound word to: ‘Someone was walking down the corridor.’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: Thud. Thud. Thud. Someone was walking down the corridor, one heavy step at a time.

  3. Finish this as a cliffhanger in one or two sentences: ‘He looked under the bed and saw…’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: He looked under the bed and saw a small wooden box. It had his name on it — written in his own handwriting.

  4. Change ‘Ali was very scared.’ into a sentence that shows his fear.

    Show answer
    Possible answer: Ali’s knees locked, and his mouth went so dry that he could not even call for help.

  5. Why does the author of the sample write ‘Scrape.’ as a paragraph on its own?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to let the reader ‘hear’ the sound on its own, with nothing to distract them · to create a pause and a sudden change of pace · to build suspense, because the noise has no explanation yet · to show that the noise is repeating, so the danger seems real.

  6. Practice task: ‘You have read the opening of a story in which Ziad is on a school trip to a museum and gets separated from his class. Continue the story.’ Write a short plan using the tension ladder.

    Show answer
    Same POV/tense as extract (third person, past). 1. Normal: Ziad realises the hall is empty and the class has gone. 2. First hint: the lights in the mummy room dim · footsteps echo behind him. 3. Investigate: he follows the footsteps past glass cases · slow motion, heart thumping. 4. Worse hint / false alarm: a shadow on the wall — just a statue · then a door clicks shut behind him. 5. Reveal: a hand on his shoulder… it is the museum guard with a torch. 6. Release: the guard leads him back · his teacher is waiting · Ziad promises never to wander off again, but he glances back once at the mummy case, and wonders.
