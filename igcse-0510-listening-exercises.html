IGCSE 0510 · Paper 2 Listening

Listening Exercises 1-5: Method and Practice

Exercise-by-exercise method for Paper 2 Listening, with original recording scripts, exam-style questions, answers and an explanation of every distractor.

British & IGCSE › Cambridge IGCSE English as a Second Language (0510)

On this page we go through Paper 2 one exercise at a time. For each exercise you get: what it is, the method step by step, an original mini recording script written out in full, exam-style questions, the answers, and an explanation of why every wrong option is wrong. If you have not read the overview page yet (Paper 2 Listening: Overview and Strategy), start there.

How to use the scripts on this page
  • Best way: ask a parent, brother or friend to read the script aloud to you twice, at normal speed, while you look only at the questions.
  • Second-best way: read the questions first, cover the answers, then read the script once only and answer quickly.
  • Then read the explanations. The explanations matter more than the score: they show you how the distractors work.

Signal words you must recognise

Many questions ask about a speaker's attitude (how he feels), opinion (what he thinks), agreement or disagreement (in conversations and interviews) and changes of mind. These ideas are rarely stated with one obvious word. Instead, they are carried by phrases. Learn these groups.

Attitude: positive

  • I was really impressed by…
  • it was far better than I expected
  • I couldn't wait to…
  • I'm a big fan of…
  • it was well worth it
  • I'd do it again tomorrow

Attitude: negative

  • it was a bit of a let-down
  • I wasn't too keen on…
  • I can't stand…
  • it got on my nerves
  • it wasn't worth the money
  • I was fed up with…

Attitude: uncertain or mixed

  • I'm not sure about…
  • I've got mixed feelings
  • in a way… but on the other hand…
  • it depends
  • I suppose so
  • I can see the point, but…

Giving an opinion

  • personally, I think…
  • as far as I'm concerned…
  • if you ask me…
  • the way I see it…
  • to be honest…
  • I'm convinced that…

Agreeing

  • exactly
  • I couldn't agree more
  • that's just what I was thinking
  • you've got a point there
  • absolutely
  • fair enough

Disagreeing (often politely)

  • I'm not so sure
  • I see what you mean, but…
  • that's not how I see it
  • actually, I think…
  • really? I'd say…
  • yes, but don't forget…

Changing your mind

  • at first I thought… but now…
  • I used to… but these days…
  • on second thoughts…
  • I've come round to the idea
  • I was wrong about…
  • in the end I decided…
Word / phraseMeaning / use
enthusiasticvery keen and excited
doubtful / scepticalnot sure something is true or will work
relievedhappy because a worry has gone
frustratedannoyed because you cannot do or change something
gratefulthankful
reluctantnot wanting to do something
confidentsure of yourself or of a result
regretfulsorry about something you did or did not do

Exercise 1 (Questions 1-8): short recordings with picture options

What you hear: eight short, separate recordings. Each one is a monologue or a dialogue from everyday life: a conversation, a phone message, an announcement, a short news or radio extract. What you do: answer one question for each recording by choosing one of four pictures (A-D). 8 marks.

  1. Read the question and underline the question word and any key word (*first*, *most*, *decide*, *not*).
  2. Look at the four pictures and name what you see in English in your head (backpack, shoulder bag, sports bag). Notice the difference between the pictures: often they are the same object with one small change (with or without pockets, morning or afternoon clock).
  3. Expect all four pictures, or three of them, to be mentioned. Listen for the reason each one is rejected.
  4. First listening: cross out pictures that are rejected. Second listening: confirm the one that is left.
  5. These are often seen as easy marks. They are, but only if you listen to the end of each recording.

Worked example 1. In the exam you would see four pictures. Here they are described in words.

Question 1: Which bag does the boy decide to buy?
  • A a black backpack with no side pockets
  • B a black backpack with two side pockets
  • C a blue sports bag
  • D a grey shoulder bag
Recording script (heard twice)
  • Boy: I need a new bag for school. This blue sports bag is nice and big.
  • Mother: It's big, but it's for the gym, not for books. You'll hurt your back carrying it on one shoulder. Same with that grey one.
  • Boy: OK, a backpack then. This plain black one is the cheapest.
  • Mother: Where would you put your water bottle, though? There's nowhere on the outside.
  • Boy: True. Then I'll get the other black one, the one with a pocket on each side. It's only a bit more.
OptionHeard?Why it is right or wrong
AYesMentioned as 'the cheapest', then rejected: 'There's nowhere on the outside' for the bottle.
BYesCorrect. 'A pocket on each side' is a paraphrase of 'two side pockets'. 'I'll get' is the decision.
CYesThe boy likes it first, but the mother rejects it: 'it's for the gym, not for books'.
DYesRejected together with C: 'Same with that grey one' (you carry it on one shoulder).

Worked example 2.

Question 2: Where will the chess club meet this week?
  • A in the library
  • B in the school hall
  • C in the computer room
  • D in a classroom
Recording script (heard twice)
  • Teacher (announcement): Good morning, everyone. A quick message for members of the chess club. As you know, we normally meet in the library on Wednesdays, but this week the library is closed for painting. We did think about using the school hall, but it's booked for the drama rehearsal. So this Wednesday only, please go to Room 12 on the first floor, next to the computer room. Next week we'll be back in the library as usual.
OptionHeard?Why it is right or wrong
AYesThe usual place, and the place for next week, but closed this week (changed plans).
BYesConsidered and rejected: 'it's booked for the drama rehearsal'.
CYesOnly used to give directions: Room 12 is next to the computer room. A classic location trap.
DYesCorrect. 'Room 12 on the first floor' is a classroom.
Exercise 1 traps
  • The usual or normal arrangement ('we normally…') is almost never the answer.
  • Places used to give directions ('next to', 'opposite') are distractors.
  • Listen to the last sentence: it often contains the decision.

Exercise 2 (Questions 9-18): five short extracts, two questions each

What you hear: five short extracts, often a conversation between two people or a short interview. What you do: answer two questions on each extract, choosing from three written options (A-C). 10 marks. One question is often about a fact or a reason, and the other is often about an opinion, a feeling or something implied.

  1. Read both questions for the extract before it starts. They come in the order of the recording: the answer to the first question comes first.
  2. Underline who the question is about. In a dialogue, a question about the woman's opinion is not answered by the man's opinion.
  3. Paraphrase each option in your head before you listen.
  4. For opinion and feeling questions, listen to how the speaker reacts, not only to the words: 'Oh, great… another meeting' with a flat voice means the opposite of 'great'.
  5. If the two speakers disagree, decide carefully who says what.
Questions 9 and 10: You will hear two friends, Karim and Lina, talking about volunteering.
  • 9 Why did Karim start volunteering at the animal shelter?
  • A His teacher suggested it. · B He wanted to become a vet. · C His cousin needed help there.
  • 10 What does Lina think about volunteering at the shelter?
  • A She would find it too upsetting. · B She does not have enough time for it. · C She would like to try it.
Recording script (heard twice)
  • Lina: Karim, someone told me you spend your Saturdays at the animal shelter now. Was that your science teacher's idea? She's always telling us to do volunteer work.
  • Karim: Everyone thinks that, but no. My cousin works there, and last month half the staff were off sick. She asked if I could help out for one weekend, and I just never stopped going.
  • Lina: So are you planning to study to be a vet?
  • Karim: People keep asking me that! I honestly don't know yet. I just like being around the animals.
  • Lina: I'd love to see what it's like. I thought I'd get too sad seeing animals without a home, but you obviously cope with it. Could I come with you one Saturday?
  • Karim: Of course. Saturdays are busy, so they'd be glad of an extra pair of hands.
QuestionOptionWhy it is right or wrong
9ALina suggests it, but Karim rejects it: 'Everyone thinks that, but no.' (mentioned but rejected)
9BLina asks about it later, and Karim says 'I honestly don't know yet', so it was not his reason.
9CCorrect. His cousin asked for help when staff were off sick. 'She asked if I could help out' = his cousin needed help.
10AThis was her old belief: 'I thought I'd get too sad', but she has changed her mind (change of mind).
10BTime is never her problem. 'Saturdays are busy' is about the shelter, not about Lina: a word trap.
10CCorrect. 'I'd love to see what it's like… Could I come with you?' = she would like to try it.
Exercise 2 traps
  • Questions that one speaker asks are not facts. ('Was it your teacher's idea?' → no.)
  • 'I thought…' or 'I used to think…' often introduces an opinion that has now changed.
  • A word from the recording ('busy') can be attached to the wrong person in the options.

Exercise 3 (Questions 19-26): one longer talk, sentence completion

What you hear: one speaker giving a talk or presentation (for example to students, at a club or on the radio). What you do: complete eight sentences by choosing the correct ending from three options (A-C). 8 marks. Because there is only one speaker, the challenge is to keep your place: the sentences follow the order of the talk.

  1. Read the eight sentence beginnings first. Together, they tell you the story of the talk before you hear it.
  2. Underline the key words of each beginning (for example 'The speaker first became interested in…'). When you hear those ideas, the answer is near.
  3. Do not wait for the exact words of the sentence. The speaker will paraphrase it.
  4. If you miss one, leave it and go to the next sentence immediately. You will get it on the second listening.
  5. Check that your completed sentence is true according to the speaker, not just possible.
Questions 19-22: You will hear a wildlife photographer called Adam giving a talk to students.
  • 19 Adam first became interested in photography when he… A received a camera as a present. · B saw an exhibition. · C borrowed his father's phone.
  • 20 On his first trip to the desert, Adam's main problem was… A the heat. · B the sand. · C the lack of animals.
  • 21 Adam says the most important skill for a wildlife photographer is… A technical knowledge. · B patience. · C good eyesight.
  • 22 Adam's advice to beginners is to… A buy an expensive lens. · B travel to famous places. · C photograph animals near their home.
Recording script (heard twice)
  • Adam: Thanks for having me. People often assume I was given a camera as a child, but my family couldn't afford one. When I was twelve, my father let me use his old phone at weekends, and I started taking pictures of the birds in our garden. A year later I visited a photography exhibition in Cairo and I thought, 'This is what I want to do,' but by then I was already hooked.
  • My first real trip was to the Western Desert. Everyone warned me about the heat, and it was hot, but I was ready for that: I carried plenty of water and worked early in the morning. What I hadn't prepared for was the sand. It got inside my camera bag, my lens, everywhere, and I lost two days of work cleaning my equipment. And the animals? I saw more than I expected, especially at sunrise.
  • Students often ask me what makes a good wildlife photographer. You do need to know your camera, of course, and sharp eyes help. But honestly, anyone can learn the technical side from a book in a few weeks. What you can't learn quickly is how to wait, sometimes for six or seven hours, for one moment that lasts a second. That's what really matters.
  • So my advice to beginners? Don't spend your money on a huge lens, and don't think you need to fly to Africa. Start with the cats, pigeons and insects on your own street. You'll learn everything you need there.
QuestionAnswerExplanation of the options
19CA is what 'people often assume', then rejected ('couldn't afford one'). B happened a year later, when he was 'already hooked', so it was not the first interest. C: 'my father let me use his old phone' = borrowed his father's phone.
20BA is the expected problem, but he was 'ready for that'. C is contradicted: he 'saw more than I expected'. B: 'What I hadn't prepared for was the sand… I lost two days'.
21BA and C are mentioned ('know your camera', 'sharp eyes help') but given less importance ('anyone can learn the technical side'). 'How to wait… That's what really matters' = patience.
22CA and B are both rejected with 'Don't'. 'Start with the… insects on your own street' = near their home.
Notice the pattern

In this talk, every wrong option was mentioned. The talk is written so that the wrong ideas are heard first and then corrected. This is typical of Exercise 3. The phrases to follow are: *people often assume… but*, *a year later*, *what I hadn't prepared for*, *but honestly*, *don't…*.

Exercise 4 (Questions 27-32): six speakers, multiple matching

What you hear: six short monologues. Six different people talk about the same topic (for example, a hobby, a journey or a type of holiday). What you do: match each speaker to the statement (A-H) that expresses his or her main idea. There are eight statements, so two are not used. Each letter can be used only once. 6 marks.

  1. Read all eight statements before the first speaker. Underline the key idea in each one (cost, comfort, people, time, feelings, environment…).
  2. Paraphrase each statement in your head: 'It's less stressful than I expected' → 'I thought I'd be nervous, but I was relaxed'.
  3. Each speaker usually touches two or three of the statements. Only one is his or her real point. The others are traps.
  4. First listening: write one or two possible letters for each speaker. Second listening: decide, and cross out letters you have used.
  5. At the end, check: six different letters, two letters left over.

Here is a mini version with four speakers and six statements (two extra). The real exam has six speakers and eight statements, but the method is the same.

You will hear four students talking about joining a school club. Which idea does each speaker express?
  • A I joined because of a friend.
  • B It has helped me in my school subjects.
  • C I nearly gave up at the beginning.
  • D It takes up more time than I expected.
  • E It has made me more confident.
  • F It costs more than other clubs.
Recording script (heard twice)
  • Speaker 1 (Youssef): I joined the debating club because my best friend was in it, or that's what I tell people. Really, I'd seen the team win a competition on stage and I wanted that too. I used to go red if a teacher even asked me a question. Now I can stand in front of two hundred people and argue my case. That change is the best thing about it.
  • Speaker 2 (Hana): The robotics club isn't cheap. You pay for the kits. But the surprise for me was how much it improved my maths. I'd always been weak at it, and suddenly equations made sense, because I needed them to make the robot move. My last two maths tests were my best ever.
  • Speaker 3 (Ali): I thought the photography club would be one hour a week. In reality, there are trips at weekends, editing at home, exhibitions to prepare. I don't regret joining, but I've had to give up football to fit it all in.
  • Speaker 4 (Mariam): The first two weeks of the drama club were awful. I didn't know anyone, the others had all acted before, and I went home and told my mum I wasn't going back. She persuaded me to try one more week, and that week I got a small part in the play. Now I'm there every day.
SpeakerAnswerWhy, and which traps were there
1EA is a trap: 'my best friend was in it, or that's what I tell people. Really…' (rejected). 'I used to go red… now I can stand in front of two hundred people' = more confident.
2BF is a trap: she says the club 'isn't cheap', but she does not compare it with other clubs, and it is not her main point. 'How much it improved my maths… my best ever' = helped in her school subjects.
3D'I thought… one hour a week. In reality… trips, editing, exhibitions' = more time than expected.
4C'I wasn't going back… she persuaded me to try one more week' = she nearly gave up. A is a trap: she 'didn't know anyone'.
Not usedA, FBoth were mentioned as traps, which is exactly why they are there.
Exercise 4 traps
  • A statement can be partly true for a speaker but not his or her main idea.
  • Comparisons matter: 'It costs more than other clubs' needs a comparison, not just 'it isn't cheap'.
  • Do not use a letter twice. If two speakers seem to fit the same letter, listen again for which one fits better and look for another letter for the other speaker.

Exercise 5 (Questions 33-40): a longer interview

What you hear: an interview between two speakers, for example a radio presenter and a guest. What you do: answer eight questions with three options (A-C) about the main speaker (the guest). 8 marks. This exercise tests detail, opinion, attitude and implied meaning, so it is usually the most demanding part of the paper.

  1. Read all eight questions first and note what each one asks: a fact, a reason, a feeling, an opinion?
  2. The interviewer's question usually introduces the topic of the next exam question. Use it as a signpost: 'So, what made you…?' → the answer to the 'why' question is coming.
  3. Remember the questions are about the guest. The interviewer may suggest an idea that the guest then corrects: that idea is a distractor.
  4. Listen for agreement and disagreement: 'Not exactly…', 'That's partly true, but…', 'Absolutely'.
  5. Feelings and attitudes are often implied. 'I didn't sleep the night before' implies nervousness.
Questions 33-36: You will hear an interview with a young engineer called Tarek, who builds low-cost water filters.
  • 33 How did Tarek get the idea for his first water filter? A from a university project · B from a problem in his grandparents' village · C from a television programme
  • 34 How did Tarek feel when his first filter failed? A He wanted to stop the project. · B He blamed the materials he used. · C He saw it as a useful lesson.
  • 35 What does Tarek say about the presenter's idea that the filters should be sold in shops? A He agrees completely. · B He thinks it would make them too expensive. · C He plans to do it next year.
  • 36 What does Tarek say is his biggest achievement so far? A winning an international prize · B training local people to make the filters · C getting a job with a large company
Recording script (heard twice)
  • Presenter: My guest today is Tarek, who's only twenty-three but has already made water filters that are used in more than forty villages. Tarek, was this a university project?
  • Tarek: Everyone assumes so, but no. I was studying engineering, yes, but the idea came from my grandparents' village. Every summer the children there got ill from the water, and one year my little cousin ended up in hospital. I remember thinking: I'm an engineering student, I should be able to fix this.
  • Presenter: And did it work first time?
  • Tarek: Not at all! The first filter fell apart in about a week. My friends said I'd chosen the wrong materials and should give up. Honestly, I was quite pleased, in a strange way, because now I knew exactly what didn't work. I changed the design and the second one lasted a year.
  • Presenter: People listening might think: why not sell them in every supermarket?
  • Tarek: I see why you'd say that, and lots of companies have asked me. But the moment you add packaging, transport and a shop's profit, the price triples, and the families who need them most can't pay. So, no, that's not the plan, not next year or any year.
  • Presenter: You've won a big prize in Germany. Is that the proudest moment?
  • Tarek: The prize was lovely, and I was offered some great jobs afterwards, which I turned down. But what I'm proudest of is that in each village there are now people who can build and repair the filters themselves. They don't need me any more. That was always the aim.
QuestionAnswerExplanation of the options
33BA is suggested by the presenter and rejected: 'Everyone assumes so, but no.' C is never mentioned. 'The idea came from my grandparents' village… children got ill from the water' = a problem in the village.
34CA is what his friends said, not what he felt. B: the friends blamed the materials, but Tarek did not. 'I was quite pleased… now I knew exactly what didn't work' = a useful lesson (implied attitude).
35B'I see why you'd say that' sounds like agreement, but 'But… the price triples' shows disagreement, so A is wrong. C is directly denied: 'not next year or any year'.
36BA is mentioned but 'what I'm proudest of' comes after 'But'. C is false: he 'turned down' the jobs. 'People who can build and repair the filters themselves' = trained local people.
Polite disagreement is the hardest trap

In interviews, speakers rarely say 'No, you are wrong.' They say *I see why you'd say that, but…*, *That's partly true…*, *Not exactly…*, *I wouldn't go that far*. The first half sounds like agreement. The meaning is always in the part after but.

Extra practice: signal words in action

Each item is a one-line script. Decide what the speaker's attitude, opinion or decision is.

  1. *'I wasn't too keen on the idea of a cooking course at first, but I've come round to it.'* How does the speaker feel about the course now?

    Show answer
    Positive. 'I've come round to it' means she has changed her mind and now accepts or likes the idea. 'Wasn't too keen' was her first attitude.

  2. *'You've got a point about the cost, but I still think the trip is worth it.'* Does the speaker agree with the other person?

    Show answer
    Only partly. He accepts the point about the cost ('you've got a point') but disagrees with the conclusion: he still thinks the trip is worth it.

  3. *'Oh, brilliant. The bus is late again.'* (said in a flat, tired voice) What is the speaker's attitude?

    Show answer
    Annoyed or frustrated. 'Brilliant' is used sarcastically: the tone shows the opposite meaning.

  4. *'We'd booked a table for eight, but in the end we just got a takeaway and ate at home.'* Where did they eat?

    Show answer
    At home. The booking was the original plan, but 'in the end' gives what really happened.

  5. *'I'd hardly call it a holiday. We spent every day visiting relatives.'* What does the speaker imply?

    Show answer
    That the trip was not relaxing or enjoyable as a holiday. 'Hardly' is a negative: it means 'not really'.

  6. *'If you ask me, the old library was more comfortable, though the new one is certainly brighter.'* Which library does the speaker prefer?

    Show answer
    The old library. 'If you ask me' introduces her opinion. She admits one advantage of the new one ('brighter') with 'though', but her preference is clear.

  7. *'I couldn't agree more. Exams should be later in the day.'* What is the speaker's attitude to the other person's idea?

    Show answer
    Complete agreement. 'I couldn't agree more' = I agree 100%.

  8. *'I was dreading the interview, but the moment I sat down, they made me feel at home.'* How did the speaker feel during the interview?

    Show answer
    Relaxed and comfortable. 'Dreading' describes her feeling before, while 'made me feel at home' describes the interview itself.
Before you move on
  • I know the task, the number of questions and the options for each exercise.
  • I can explain why a 'mentioned' option can still be wrong.
  • I recognise phrases for attitude, opinion, agreement, disagreement and changes of mind.
  • I have practised at least one script on this page with someone reading it aloud twice.
