The Exam: Overview

Everything about the Year 6 Cambridge Primary Checkpoint English exam (0058): both papers, timings, marks, the kinds of reading questions and writing tasks, and a 12-week study plan.

Welcome to the Year 6 English course. This page is your map of the Cambridge Primary Checkpoint English exam. Read it first, before any other page. It tells you what the two papers look like, how many marks each part is worth, what kinds of questions and writing tasks to expect, and how to use this course week by week. Parents: there is a short summary in Arabic at the end of the page.

When you know the shape of the exam, nothing on the day will surprise you. Every lesson you study will have a clear place in your head: "This helps me with the reading questions" or "This helps me with the writing task."

What is Cambridge Primary Checkpoint?

Cambridge Primary Checkpoint is a test that students take at the end of Stage 6 (Year 6), the last year of Cambridge Primary. The English test has the code 0058. Before 2023 it had a different code (0844), and it followed an older curriculum. If your teacher gives you an old 0844 paper, the reading and writing skills are still useful practice, but the newer 0058 papers show the current style best.

  • Checkpoint papers are marked by Cambridge, not by your own teachers.
  • There are two papers: Paper 1 is about non-fiction (real-life texts such as reports, articles and reviews) and Paper 2 is about fiction (stories).
  • Each paper tests reading and writing. Speaking and listening are part of your lessons at school, but they are not tested in Checkpoint.
Key facts to remember
  • Two papers: Paper 1 Non-fiction (0058/01) and Paper 2 Fiction (0058/02).
  • Each paper lasts 1 hour and is worth 50 marks.
  • Each paper has Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks).
  • The suggested time is about 30 minutes for Reading and about 30 minutes for Writing.
  • Total for both papers: 100 marks (Reading 50 + Writing 50).

Why 30 + 30 matters

Reading and Writing are worth the same: 25 marks each. If you spend 45 minutes on the reading questions, you will have only 15 minutes for a 25-mark writing task. At the start of the exam, look at the clock and write the time to start Section B in the corner of your paper (start time + 30 minutes). When you reach that time, move to the writing, even if you have a question left. You can come back to it at the end.

Section A: Reading — what the questions ask

In Section A you read a text and answer a set of numbered questions. Some questions have parts (a), (b), (c). Most questions are worth 1 or 2 marks; a few longer ones are worth up to 4 marks. The questions usually follow the order of the text, so the answer to question 1 is near the start and the answer to the last question may need the whole text.

The reading paper is also a grammar paper

Many students think the reading section only checks if you understood the story or the article. In fact, many marks test grammar, punctuation and structure inside the text: word classes, clauses, verb forms, commas, paragraph links. That is why this course starts with the grammar pages. Learn the grammar words exactly. If a question asks for an abstract noun or the main clause, an answer that is "nearly right" scores nothing.

Let the marks guide your answer. The number of marks in brackets tells you how much to write:

MarksWhat a full answer looks like
[1]One clear point, a single word, a tick or a short phrase.
[2]Two points, OR one point + a quotation, OR one point + an explanation.
[4]Usually two points, each with a short quotation from the text and an explanation in your own words.

Section B: Writing — what the tasks ask

Section B is one writing task worth 25 marks. There is a space on the paper to plan. The plan does not get marks, but a quick plan makes your writing organised, and organisation (Structure of texts) is worth 7 marks on its own.

Paper 1 (non-fiction). Recent papers have asked for a review (of something you enjoyed when you were younger, written for a children's magazine), a news report for a school newspaper about an event at school or in the local area, and a balanced argument that gives both sides of a local question and the views of people who live there. Other non-fiction types can also appear, for example a persuasive letter, a leaflet or advert, an information text (non-chronological report), instructions or an explanation, a recount, a diary, a biography and an article. This course has a pack for each of them.

Paper 2 (fiction). Recent papers have asked for the opening of a story about characters who travel a long way to help someone, a continuation of the story in the reading extract using your own ideas, and a story about a special place where something is discovered. So the fiction task can be a story opening, a story continuation, or a complete story on a theme. Good stories describe the setting and the characters, not only the events.

Both writing tasks are marked in the same five categories: Creation of texts 5, Vocabulary and language 3, Structure of texts 7, Grammar and punctuation 7, Word structure (spelling) 3. The next page, How your writing is marked, explains each one in simple words and shows you how to reach the top.

Rules that can wipe out your writing mark
  • A piece of 20 words or fewer gets 0.
  • A piece of 21 to 60 words can get 7 out of 25 at most, even if every word is perfect. Sixty words is only about eight lines of handwriting.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example a story when the task asks for a news report), your marks for Creation of texts and Structure are kept very low.
  • So: read the task carefully, write the right text type, and write a full answer. In this course we train you to write about 250–350 words in 30 minutes.

When is the exam?

Checkpoint is taken on fixed days set by Cambridge, in exam series during the year (recent papers come from series in March, April and October). Your school chooses the series and gives you your exact timetable. The papers, marks and timings are the same in every series.

Some schools also give a Progression Test for Stage 6 during the year. It usually follows the same pattern as the Checkpoint papers (Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction), and it is marked by teachers at your school. Ask your school if you will take one. This course prepares you for both.

What the curriculum covers

Cambridge Primary English is organised into strands (groups of skills). The Checkpoint papers test the Reading and Writing strands. You will see these names in your school reports, and the writing strands are the same names used to mark your writing.

How to use this course

  1. Start here (2 pages). This overview and How your writing is marked. Learn the five writing categories and the two planning techniques, G.A.A.P. and G.C.S.P.P.
  2. Reading: grammar and punctuation (6 pages). Parts of speech, phrases and clauses, sentence types, connectives, punctuation, grammar in use. Do these first: they win reading marks and they also raise your writing marks.
  3. Reading: understanding texts (6 pages). Text types and features, purpose and viewpoint, inference and themes, language effects, vocabulary skills, and reading exam technique.
  4. Paper 1 writing (18 packs). One pack for each non-fiction genre: what it is, its features, how to structure it, useful phrases and words, a strong sample with notes, common mistakes and a practice task.
  5. Paper 2 writing (11 packs). Story-writing basics, describing a setting, describing a character and creating suspense, then a pack for each fiction genre with a strong sample.

On every reading page: read the explanation slowly, cover the answers, try every practice question, then open the answers and read why each one is right. On every writing pack: learn the structure and ten of the vocabulary words, read the sample and its notes, then write the practice task by hand in 30 minutes and check it with the five categories.

Write, get feedback, then write again

Never start a new piece of writing before you have had feedback on the last one. Give your writing to your teacher, read every correction, and make sure you understand why it was a mistake. Writing again and again without feedback only makes the same mistakes stronger.

A 12-week study plan

Use this plan in the last twelve weeks before the exam. Each week needs about 3–4 hours: two or three short reading sessions (30–40 minutes each) and one timed writing task. Short, regular study is better than one long session at the weekend. If you have more time, do the plan twice and add full papers the second time.

Keep a mistakes notebook

Every time you lose a mark, write down four things: the question type, your answer, the right answer, and the reason. Read the notebook every week. Most students lose marks on the same few question types again and again. Your notebook shows you which ones are yours, so you can fix them.

On the exam day

  • Bring two pens that work, a pencil and an eraser. Write neatly: the examiner must be able to read your answers.
  • Read the whole text once before you answer any question. Then read each question twice and underline the key words (find, explain, own words, quotation, tick one).
  • Answer every question. A sensible guess can earn a mark; an empty space never does.
  • Move to the writing after about 30 minutes. Spend 3–4 minutes planning, about 22 minutes writing, and 3–4 minutes checking.
  • In the writing, use paragraphs, cover every point in the task, and check your spelling and full stops at the end.

Check yourself

  1. How long is each Checkpoint 0058 paper, and how many marks is it worth?

    Show answer
    Each paper lasts 1 hour and is worth 50 marks: Reading 25 and Writing 25.

  2. Which paper is about stories?

    Show answer
    Paper 2 Fiction. Paper 1 is Non-fiction.

  3. How much time should you spend on the reading section?

    Show answer
    About 30 minutes, then about 30 minutes on the writing.

  4. A student writes 55 words in the writing section. What is the highest mark possible?

    Show answer
    7 out of 25. Pieces of 21–60 words can score 7 at most, however good they are.

  5. Name the five writing categories and their marks.

    Show answer
    Creation of texts 5, Vocabulary and language 3, Structure of texts 7, Grammar and punctuation 7, Word structure (spelling) 3. Total 25.

  6. A question says [2]. What does a full answer need?

    Show answer
    Two points, or one point with a quotation or an explanation.

  7. Give three kinds of fiction task that have appeared in recent papers.

    Show answer
    A story opening, a continuation of the story in the extract, and a complete story on a theme (for example a special place and a discovery).

  8. Are speaking and listening tested in Checkpoint?

    Show answer
    No. They are part of the curriculum, but Checkpoint tests reading and writing only.
لأولياء الأمور: ما هو امتحان Checkpoint لطلاب Year 6؟

امتحان Cambridge Primary Checkpoint English (كود 0058) يؤديه الطالب في نهاية Year 6، وتصححه جامعة كامبريدج نفسها وليس مدرسو المدرسة. الامتحان ورقتان: الورقة الأولى نصوص واقعية غير قصصية (Non-fiction) مثل التقارير والمقالات والمراجعات، والورقة الثانية قصة (Fiction). كل ورقة مدتها ساعة واحدة و50 درجة: 25 للقراءة و25 للكتابة، والمجموع 100 درجة. يُنصح الطالب بـ30 دقيقة للقراءة و30 دقيقة للكتابة. لا يوجد امتحان استماع أو تحدث.

كيف تساعد ابنك أو ابنتك؟
  • ابدأ معه بدروس القواعد (أقسام الكلام، العبارات والجمل، علامات الترقيم) لأن أسئلة القراءة تعتمد عليها كثيرًا.
  • اطلب منه أن يكتب موضوعًا كاملًا كل أسبوع في 30 دقيقة بالضبط، بخط اليد، ثم يعرضه على المدرس.
  • انتبه: إذا كتب 60 كلمة أو أقل فلن يحصل على أكثر من 7 من 25 في الكتابة، وإذا كتب نوع نص خطأ (قصة بدل تقرير مثلًا) ستقل درجته جدًا.
  • شجّعه على تخصيص كراسة للأخطاء يكتب فيها كل سؤال أخطأ فيه وسبب الخطأ، ويراجعها كل أسبوع.
