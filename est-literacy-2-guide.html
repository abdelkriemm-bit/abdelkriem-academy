EST Literacy Test 2: Reading

Every reading skill on the test, a method and pacing plan for 52 questions in 65 minutes, and an original passage with ten practice questions.

American Diploma › EST I Literacy — Complete Guide

Literacy Test 2 is the Reading part of EST I. You read texts — stories, articles, arguments, sometimes with a table or a second text — and answer questions about what they say, what they suggest and how they are built. This guide explains each skill, gives you a reading method and a pacing plan, and ends with a full original passage and ten questions.

What this test tests

As published by schools, Literacy Test 2 has 52 multiple-choice questions in 65 minutes. Every question has four choices. The answer is always supported by the text — never by your outside knowledge or opinion.

Important

These figures are as published by schools; check the latest official EST candidate guide on est.moe.gov.eg before your test. The exact passage lengths and question order are not given in the published school information, so practise both short texts (SAT style) and long passages (ACT style) on this site.

SkillWhat the question asks (our wording)How to find the answer
Main idea"Which choice best states the main idea of the passage?"Sum up the whole text in one sentence before you look at the choices.
Detail"According to the passage, why did…?"Go back to the lines; the answer is stated, often in different words.
Inference"It can most reasonably be inferred that…"Choose what must be true from the text — the smallest safe step.
Vocabulary in context"As used in line X, the word … most nearly means"Cover the word, put in your own word, then match. The common meaning is often the trap.
Purpose"The main purpose of the third paragraph is to…"Ask: why did the writer put this here? Use verbs: introduce, explain, contrast, illustrate, conclude.
Evidence"Which quotation best supports the claim that…?"The right quotation proves the exact claim, not just the topic.
Data and graphics"Which choice uses data from the table to support…?"Check the numbers AND that they prove the stated point.
Paired texts"How would the author of Text 2 respond to…?"Find each author's main view, then the point where they agree or disagree.

Strategy: 52 questions in 65 minutes

65 ÷ 52 = 1.25 minutes = 75 seconds per question, and this time includes reading the passages. If a passage has several questions, the reading time is shared between them, so a good first read saves time on every question that follows.

The reading method, step by step

  1. Read the introduction line (if there is one): who wrote it, when, about what.
  2. Read the passage once, actively. After each paragraph, write two or three words on your scrap paper: "problem", "survey results", "warning".
  3. After the passage, say the main idea in one sentence — before you look at any question.
  4. Read the question and find the line it refers to. Read a little before and after.
  5. Answer in your own words first, then look for the choice that matches.
  6. Eliminate by trap type: too broad, too narrow, too extreme, opposite, true but not the answer, off-topic.
  7. Prove it. Point to the words in the text that support your choice. If you cannot point, it is probably wrong.
القاعدة الذهبية في الـ Reading

الإجابة الصح دايمًا موجودة في النص — مش في معلوماتك ولا رأيك. قبل ما تختار، اسأل نفسك: أقدر أشاور على الكلام اللي في النص اللي يثبت الإجابة دي؟ لو مش قادر، غالبًا الإجابة غلط.

Worked examples

Example 1 (vocabulary in context). Text: "The coach was famous for her economy with words: after a win she said 'Good,' and after a loss she said nothing at all." Question: As used in the text, "economy" most nearly means A) wealth B) careful, limited use C) business system D) low price

Answer: B. She uses very few words, so "economy" means careful, limited use. A and C are the common meanings of "economy" — the classic vocabulary trap. D belongs to shopping ("economy class"), not to speech.

Example 2 (inference). Text: "By the time Karim reached the platform, the train doors were closing. He stopped running, checked the board, and sat down on a bench with his book." Question: What can most reasonably be inferred about Karim? A) He was angry with the driver. B) He decided to wait for a later train. C) He had missed his train many times before. D) He did not want to travel at all.

Answer: B. He checks the board and sits down to read — he is waiting for the next train. A invents an emotion. C is not supported ("many times" is extreme). D contradicts his running to catch the train.

Example 3 (data). A student uses this table to support the claim "The chess club grew more than any other club."

ClubMembers 2024Members 2025
Chess1830
Drama2524
Robotics1221

Which choice best supports the claim? A) Drama had the most members in 2024. B) Chess grew by 12 members, while robotics grew by 9 and drama lost one. C) Robotics almost doubled its membership. D) Chess had 30 members in 2025.

Answer: B. Only B compares the growth of all clubs. A is true but about drama. C is true but supports a different claim. D gives one number with no comparison — half the evidence.

Classic traps
  • Too extreme: always, never, all, completely — the text rarely says this.
  • Too broad / too narrow: a main idea choice that covers more than the text, or only one paragraph.
  • True but not the answer: a fact from the passage that does not answer this question.
  • Opposite: uses the passage's words but reverses the meaning.
  • Common meaning: in vocabulary questions, the everyday meaning of the word is often wrong in context.
  • Half the data: a correct number that does not prove the comparison the question asks for.
Fast strategy for test day
  • One active read per passage; note the main idea of each paragraph in two or three words.
  • Answer in your own words before reading the choices.
  • Find the line, then prove your choice with words from the text.
  • Checkpoints: Q13 at 16 min, Q26 at 32, Q39 at 48.
  • Flag and move on after about 90 seconds on one question.

Practice

Read the passage once using the method above, then answer the ten questions. The last question adds a short second text. Untimed — but note how long you take: about 12–13 minutes would be on pace.

Remember
  • Literacy Test 2 = Reading, 52 questions in 65 minutes as published by schools (check the official candidate guide): 75 seconds per question including reading.
  • Read actively once, note each paragraph's point, and state the main idea before the questions.
  • Answer in your own words, then match — and prove your choice with words from the text.
  • Watch for extreme, too broad, opposite and true-but-not-the-answer choices.
  • For data questions, the right choice proves the exact comparison asked for.
