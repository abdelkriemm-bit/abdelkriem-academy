Verb Finiteness: Gerunds, Participles and Infinitives

Make sure every sentence has a finite main verb, spot the long-subject trap, and know when an -ing word, a to-verb or a participle is the right choice.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

A sentence can be long, full of facts and still be broken, because it has no real main verb. Finiteness questions test exactly this. The four choices are forms of one verb — *attracts, attracting, to attract, having attracted* — and you must decide whether the blank needs a finite verb (one that can be the main verb of a clause) or a non-finite form (an -ing word, a to-verb or a participle that only describes or adds information). Once you know the test, these questions become some of the fastest on the module.

What this question type tests

These questions belong to Standard English Conventions, which is about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (about 11–15 questions across the two modules). Inside that domain, the official skill group Form, Structure, and Sense covers verb finiteness — the official list names gerunds, participles and infinitives versus finite verbs — together with tense, agreement and the other verb skills. Convention questions usually come in the later part of each module, after Information and Ideas.

The stem is: "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?" You know it is a finiteness question when the choices mix finite and non-finite forms of the same verb:

  • give / giving / to give / having given
  • was moved / moving / to be moved / having moved
  • it sent / sent / sending / sends

Finite and non-finite: the difference

A finite verb shows tense (and agrees with a subject). It can be the main verb of a clause on its own: *walks, walked, is walking, has walked, will walk, can walk, was built*. A non-finite form has no tense of its own and cannot be the main verb alone:

Non-finite formLooks likeMain verb alone?✗ Fragment✓ Fixed
Infinitiveto + verb (to protect)NoThe new wall to protect the village.The new wall protects the village.
-ing form (gerund / present participle)protectingNoThe engineers testing the bridge for months.The engineers tested the bridge for months.
Past participleprotected, written, builtNo (without has / had / is / was)The ancient city, built near the river.The ancient city was built near the river.
Perfect participlehaving + past participleNoThe team having completed the survey.The team had completed the survey.
"being"being + anythingNoThe temple being the oldest in the region.The temple is the oldest in the region.

Remember: a helping verb turns a non-finite form into part of a finite verb. *Testing* alone is non-finite; *were testing* is finite. *Built* alone is non-finite; *was built* and *has built* are finite.

يعني إيه finite؟

الفعل الـ finite هو الفعل اللي فيه زمن وينفع يبقى الفعل الأساسي للجملة: moves / moved / is moving / was built / can move. أما ‎-ing لوحدها، و to + فعل، والتصريف التالت من غير is/was/has، و having، و being — دول مينفعوش يبقوا فعل أساسي لوحدهم. كل جملة (وكل clause) لازم يكون فيها فاعل + فعل finite.

The finiteness test: subject → verb

  1. Find the subject of the clause that contains the blank.
  2. Look for its finite verb. Strip away everything between commas, *of*-phrases, and *who/which/that* clauses — their verbs belong to those small clauses, not to the main subject.
  3. No finite verb yet? The blank must supply one: choose the finite choice (then check tense and agreement).
  4. Finite verb already there? The blank must be non-finite (an -ing phrase, a to-verb, a participle) or a choice with a joining word. A second subject + verb after a comma, with no joining word, is a comma splice.

The long-subject trap

The SAT often builds a very long subject full of participles and extra information, so that you forget the main verb is still missing. Strip the sentence down:

  • "The Great Library of Alexandria, founded in the third century BCE and supported by the Ptolemaic kings, ______ scholars from across the Mediterranean."
  • Cross out the extra information between the commas: "The Great Library ______ scholars."
  • The subject *Library* has no verb yet → the blank needs a finite verb: attracted. *Founded* and *supported* are participles inside the modifier, not main verbs.

The same trap works with relative clauses. In "The volunteers who cleaned the beaches ______ two tonnes of plastic", *cleaned* is the verb of the small clause *who cleaned the beaches*. The subject *volunteers* still needs its own verb: collected.

Bait verb (not the main verb)Where it sitsStrip it to find the main subject
founded, built, located, known, discovereda participle phrase after the subjectThe library, founded in …, ______ → The library ______
who studied, which opened, that was founda relative clauseThe scientists who studied the reef ______ → The scientists ______
including, such as, along witha list or extra informationSeveral temples, including Philae, ______ → Several temples ______
of + nouna prepositional phraseThe population of ibex ______ → The population ______

Every clause needs its own finite verb

A clause that starts with a subordinating word — *because, although, when, while, if, since, after, before, that* — must have its own subject and finite verb, and the sentence still needs a main clause.

  • ✗ Although the tortoise moving slowly, it covers long distances. → ✓ Although the tortoise moves slowly, …
  • ✗ Because the locust changing its color in crowds, scientists were confused. → ✓ Because the locust changes its color …
  • ✗ When the rains arriving in summer. (no main clause either) → ✓ When the rains arrive in summer, the river rises.
  • Verbs joined by and must be parallel: ✓ "Mangroves filter salt and release it" · ✗ "filter salt and releasing it".
فخ الجملة الطويلة

لما الفاعل يكون طويل ومعاه كلام كتير بين فاصلتين أو بعد who / which، شيل الكلام ده واسأل: الفاعل الأساسي فعله فين؟ لو مفيش فعل لسه، يبقى الفراغ محتاج فعل كامل (finite). لكن لو الجملة فيها فعلها خلاص، يبقى الفراغ غالبًا ‎-ing أو to + فعل، ولو حطيت فاعل + فعل بعد فاصلة هتعمل comma splice.

When a non-finite form IS correct

Non-finite forms are not mistakes — they are the right choice whenever the clause already has its main verb and the blank only adds information.

Situation✓ ExampleWhy it works
After the main clause, a comma + -ing phrase shows a result or the mannerThe river flooded, forcing hundreds of families to leave."flooded" is the main verb; "forcing" describes the result.
An opening phrase describes the subjectWorking through the night, the engineers finished the repair.The main verb is "finished".
Purpose (= in order to)Farmers flood the fields to stop weeds."flood" is the main verb; "to stop" gives the reason.
After verbs and adjectives that take an infinitive or -ingThey decided to build … · She avoided using … · The pump is designed to run on solar power.The finite verbs are decided, avoided, is designed.
A participle right after a noun (a shortened relative clause)The tablets found at Amarna are letters. (= that were found)"are" is the main verb.
A gerund (or infinitive) as the subjectRestoring a reef can take decades.The gerund is the subject; "can take" is the main verb.
After a helping or modal verbhas built, was building, can build, will have builtThe helping verb makes the form finite.

-ing or -ed participle? An *-ing* participle is active (the noun does the action): "the volunteers hoping to help". A past participle is passive (the noun receives the action): "the papyri found in the cave" — the papyri did not find anything, they were found.

The method for test day

  1. Read the choices. Finite forms mixed with -ing / to / having / being? It is a finiteness question.
  2. Strip the sentence: cross out comma extras, of-phrases and who/which/that clauses. Find the main subject.
  3. Ask: does this subject (or this although/because clause) already have a finite verb?
  4. No → choose the finite verb, then check tense and agreement. Yes → choose the non-finite form; reject any choice that adds a new subject + verb after a comma.
  5. Read the whole sentence once: one complete sentence — no fragment, no comma splice, parallel verbs after *and*.

Worked examples

Worked example 1: a long subject still needs its verb

Passage: "The Great Library of Alexandria, founded in the third century BCE and supported by the Ptolemaic kings, ______ scholars from across the Mediterranean who came to study its enormous collection of scrolls."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) attracting
  • B) to attract
  • C) attracted
  • D) having attracted

Answer: C) attracted. Cross out the modifier between the commas: "The Great Library ______ scholars." The subject has no main verb yet, so the blank must be finite. Only "attracted" is finite, and the past tense fits the historical context.

  • A) attracting — An -ing form cannot be the main verb alone; the sentence becomes a fragment.
  • B) to attract — An infinitive cannot be the main verb; still a fragment.
  • D) having attracted — "Having attracted" is a perfect participle; there is still no main verb.

Worked example 2: the main verb is already there

Passage: "Last spring, volunteers planted thousands of mangrove seedlings along the Red Sea coast near Hurghada, ______ to protect the shoreline from erosion and to give young fish a safe place to grow."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) they hoped
  • B) hoping
  • C) hopes
  • D) and hoping

Answer: B) hoping. The sentence already has its subject and finite verb: "volunteers planted". After the comma we need a phrase that describes the volunteers, not a second main verb. The participle "hoping" does this.

  • A) they hoped — "They hoped" is a complete clause after a comma with no joining word: a comma splice.
  • C) hopes — "Hopes" is finite but has no subject of its own; it is also singular and present.
  • D) and hoping — "And hoping" is not parallel: "planted … and hoping" joins a finite verb with an -ing form.

Worked example 3: when the participle is correct

Passage: "The clay tablets ______ at Amarna in 1887 are letters written in cuneiform script. Many of them were sent to the pharaoh by rulers of kingdoms in Western Asia."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) were discovered
  • B) discovered
  • C) they discovered
  • D) discover

Answer: B) discovered. The main subject and verb are already there: "The clay tablets … are letters." The blank only describes the tablets, so a non-finite participle is needed: "the tablets discovered at Amarna" (= that were discovered). The past participle is passive, which fits, because the tablets were found by someone.

  • A) were discovered — "Were discovered" is finite; it creates two main verbs ("were discovered … are") with no joining word.
  • C) they discovered — "They discovered" adds a new subject and verb; the sentence would have two clauses and no connection.
  • D) discover — "Discover" is a finite present verb with no proper subject; it breaks the sentence and the time (1887).

Worked example 4: a relative clause is not the main verb

Passage: "The engineers who designed the new solar plant near Aswan ______ panels that turn slowly during the day to follow the sun across the sky, which increases the amount of electricity they produce."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) choosing
  • B) chose
  • C) having chosen
  • D) to choose

Answer: B) chose. Strip the relative clause "who designed the new solar plant near Aswan": "The engineers ______ panels." "Designed" belongs to the small clause, so the main subject "engineers" still needs a finite verb: "chose".

  • A) choosing — "Choosing" is non-finite: "The engineers … choosing panels" is a fragment.
  • C) having chosen — "Having chosen" is a participle; still no main verb.
  • D) to choose — "To choose" is an infinitive; the sentence would still have no main verb.
Classic traps
  • The fragment trap: a long subject with participles (founded, built, located, including) or a who/which clause, and no main verb. Choose the finite verb.
  • The comma-splice trap: the sentence already has its main verb, and a choice adds a new subject + verb after a comma (", they hoped", ", it sent"). Choose the -ing or to-form.
  • The double-verb trap: a finite choice like "were found" where the sentence already has a main verb later ("… are letters").
  • The being / having trap: "being" and "having + past participle" are almost never the main verb the SAT wants.
  • The parallel trap: after *and*, match the other verb: "filter … and release", "sending … and forcing".
  • The although/because trap: a subordinate clause needs its own finite verb too.
Fast strategy for test day
  • First ask: "Does this clause already have its main verb?" — it decides finite vs non-finite in five seconds.
  • Strip comma extras, of-phrases and who/which clauses before you look for the verb.
  • If two choices are both finite, the question becomes tense or agreement: use the passage's time clues and the subject.
  • Read the sentence once with your choice: one complete sentence, no fragment, no splice.
  • Target time: about 30–40 seconds.

Practice

Ten original questions in Digital SAT format. Strip each sentence, find the main subject, and decide whether its finite verb is already there.

Remember
  • Every clause needs a finite verb. -ing, to + verb, a past participle alone, having … and being cannot be the main verb.
  • Strip comma extras, of-phrases and who/which/that clauses to find the main subject — their verbs do not count.
  • If the clause already has its main verb, the blank is non-finite; a new subject + verb after a comma is a comma splice.
  • Non-finite is right for results (, forcing …), purpose (to stop …), descriptions (the tablets found …) and gerund subjects (Restoring a reef …).
  • Check finiteness first, then tense, then agreement.
