The Digital SAT: Complete Overview

How the Digital SAT is built, how the adaptive modules and scoring work, what every Reading and Writing question looks like, and a 6–8 week plan to use this course.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

This page explains the whole Digital SAT in one place: how the test is built, how the adaptive modules work, what every Reading and Writing question looks like, how scoring works, and how to use this course to prepare in 6–8 weeks. Read it once carefully before you start the lessons. Parents can read it too: everything here is written so that you can follow your son's or daughter's preparation.

The test at a glance

The Digital SAT has two sections. Reading and Writing (RW) always comes first. After it there is a 10-minute break, and then the Math section. The whole test is 134 minutes long and has 98 questions. This course covers the Reading and Writing section completely.

Two numbers to remember for the Reading and Writing section: 27 questions in 32 minutes per module, which is about 1 minute 11 seconds per question. That is enough time if you work steadily, but not enough time to read every passage three times. The strategy page that follows this one shows you how to spend those seconds well.

Unscored questions

Each module contains 2 pretest questions that do not count towards your score. You cannot tell which ones they are, so treat every question as if it counts.

How the adaptive modules work

The Digital SAT is adaptive by module (the official name is multistage adaptive). This means the computer does not change the difficulty after every single question. Instead, it changes the difficulty of the whole second module:

  • Module 1 is the same kind of broad mix for everyone: easy, medium and hard questions.
  • When Module 1 ends, your performance decides which version of Module 2 you get: a harder one or an easier one.
  • So Module 1 matters a lot: careless mistakes on easy questions there can decide which Module 2 you receive.
  • You cannot go back to Module 1 once Module 2 starts.
  • Inside a module you can move back and forth freely, change answers, and mark questions for review to come back to them before the module's time runs out.

A common myth

Some students think: "If Module 2 feels hard, I am doing badly." The opposite is usually true. A hard Module 2 normally means you did well in Module 1. Stay calm and keep working.

Where you take it: the Bluebook app

The test is taken in College Board's Bluebook app on a laptop or tablet. In Bluebook you answer the questions on screen, you can see the time remaining, and you can mark any question for review. There is a built-in Desmos graphing calculator, but it only matters for the Math section.

Before test day, install Bluebook and take the official full-length practice tests in it, so that the screen, the timer and the review tools feel familiar. The official information page is satsuite.collegeboard.org.

The four content domains of Reading and Writing

Every scored Reading and Writing question belongs to one of four content domains. Questions from all four domains appear in both modules. The table shows the approximate share of the 50 scored questions and the question types (skills) inside each domain.

DomainShare of scored questionsAbout how manyQuestion types inside it
Craft and Structure≈ 28%13–15Words in Context; Text Structure and Purpose; Cross-Text Connections
Information and Ideas≈ 26%12–14Central Ideas and Details; Command of Evidence — Textual; Command of Evidence — Quantitative (tables and graphs); Inferences
Standard English Conventions≈ 26%11–15Boundaries (punctuation between and inside sentences); Form, Structure, and Sense (agreement, verb forms, tense, modifiers, possessives and plurals)
Expression of Ideas≈ 20%8–12Transitions; Rhetorical Synthesis (using a student's notes to write a sentence that achieves a goal)

Two of these domains are mainly reading (Craft and Structure, Information and Ideas) and two are mainly writing (Standard English Conventions, Expression of Ideas). Together the reading domains are a little more than half of the section, so a strong reader has a big advantage. But the grammar domain is the fastest one to improve: there is a fixed list of rules, and once you know them, those questions become quick, sure points.

What a Reading and Writing question looks like

The old paper SAT had long passages with many questions each. The Digital SAT is different: every question has its own short passage. The passage is 25 to 150 words long (sometimes a pair of short passages), and it is followed by one multiple-choice question with four options. Passages come from literature, history and social studies, the humanities, and science. Some include a table or a graph.

Here is the shape of a typical item (original example):

Passage: "For decades, historians assumed that medieval trade routes across the Sahara carried mostly gold and salt. Recent excavations of caravan stops, however, have uncovered large amounts of glass beads, copper tools and cotton cloth, suggesting that the routes carried a far ______ range of goods than once believed."

Question: Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase? A) narrower B) wider C) costlier D) older

Answer: B. The passage contrasts the old belief ("mostly gold and salt") with a discovery of many other goods, so the range was wider. "Narrower" reverses the logic; "costlier" and "older" are not supported by anything in the text.

Notice three things. First, the passage is short, so you can read all of it — and you should. Second, the question stem tells you exactly what job to do. Third, the correct answer is always supported by the words on the screen, not by your outside knowledge.

The usual order of question types in a module

Inside each module, questions are grouped by type. The order is usually the same in every module, so after a little practice you will always know what is coming next:

  1. Craft and Structure — Words in Context first, then Text Structure and Purpose, then Cross-Text Connections (two short passages).
  2. Information and Ideas — Central Ideas and Details, Command of Evidence (textual and quantitative), and Inferences.
  3. Standard English Conventions — Boundaries and Form, Structure, and Sense (the grammar questions).
  4. Expression of Ideas — Transitions, and usually last of all, Rhetorical Synthesis (the "student's notes" questions).

This order helps your time plan: the grammar and transitions questions near the end of the module are usually faster than the inference and evidence questions in the middle, so never let the clock run out before you reach them.

A 6–8 week study plan

Plan for about 45–60 minutes a day, 5 days a week. If you have 8 weeks, use the extra two weeks for the full tests and for the weakest domain in your mistake notebook. If you only have 6 weeks, follow the plan as it is and do not skip the full tests.

WeekFocusWhat to do
1Format, strategy, vocabularyThis page and the time-strategy page. Words in Context and the Vocabulary Toolkit; learn about 20 words a day from the toolkit.
2Craft and StructureText Structure and Purpose, Cross-Text Connections. Keep revising vocabulary every day.
3Grammar part 1Sentence boundaries, commas and extra information, colons, dashes and semicolons.
4Grammar part 2Subject–verb agreement, pronouns, verb tense and finiteness, modifiers, possessives and plurals.
5Information and Ideas + Expression of IdeasCentral ideas, textual and quantitative evidence, inferences, transitions, rhetorical synthesis.

8 (optional)Full test 3 + official practicePractice Test 3, then an official full-length practice test in Bluebook. Light review only in the last two days.
Daily routine that works
  • 10 minutes: vocabulary revision (yesterday's words, then today's).
  • 30–40 minutes: one lesson section or one quiz.
  • 10 minutes: write your mistakes and the trap names in your notebook.
  • Once a week: redo last week's mistakes without looking at the answers.
Mistakes students make in preparation
  • Doing hundreds of questions without reading the explanations. You learn from the explanation, not from the question.
  • Leaving grammar until the end. It is the fastest domain to improve, so start it early.
  • Never practising with a timer. From week 3, do at least one timed quiz per week (about 71 seconds per question).
  • Taking the first full test only a few days before the real one. Take it by week 6 at the latest.

Check your understanding

This short untimed test has two parts. Questions 1–8 check that you understand how the test works. Questions 9–12 are sample Reading and Writing items, one from each content domain, so you can see real test format before you start the lessons.

Remember
  • Reading and Writing: 2 modules × 27 questions × 32 minutes ≈ 71 seconds per question.
  • Module 1 decides whether Module 2 is harder or easier; you cannot go back to Module 1.
  • Every question = one short passage (25–150 words) + one question with 4 choices. The answer is always supported by the text.
  • Four domains: Craft and Structure ≈28%, Information and Ideas ≈26%, Standard English Conventions ≈26%, Expression of Ideas ≈20%.
  • No penalty for wrong answers: answer every question. Scores: 200–800 per section, 400–1600 total.
