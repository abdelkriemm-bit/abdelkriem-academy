Oxford ESL 9280 · Writing

Extended Writing (Question 4)

How to plan and write a full-mark 100-150 word Question 4 for the OxfordAQA 9280 Writing paper: every purpose, a 3-minute plan, how to reach Language 13-15, an upgrade table and two model answers.

British & IGCSE › Oxford International GCSE English as a Second Language

1. What Question 4 is and where it sits in the paper

Question 4 is the last and biggest question on the Writing paper (9280/W, 1 hour 15 minutes, 60 marks). You write an extended piece of 100-150 words for 23 marks: Content and Communication 8 + Language 15. That is more than Question 1 and Question 2 together, so it deserves your best planning and about 26 minutes of your time.

Question 4 is called an open-purpose task because the purpose changes from series to series. You may be asked to explain, inform, argue, persuade or describe why/how. A form is sometimes named. In recent papers it has very often been an article (see our Article pack), but the task can also simply say 'Explain…' or 'Write about…'. The topic can come from any of the three themes: Identity and culture, Local, national, international and global areas of interest, and Current and future study and employment.

FeatureQuestion 4
Words100-150 (no penalty for going over, but long answers lose focus and short answers are thin)
Marks23 = Content and Communication 8 + Language 15
Bullet pointsTwo, and they are prompts only. You may follow them, change their order, add to them or use other relevant ideas. Extra relevant ideas are rewarded.
ReaderAssume an adult reader, such as your teacher or the examiner, even when the task mentions a school magazine
RegisterClear, mature and respectful. Lively is fine, slang is not
Suggested timeAbout 26 minutes: plan 3-5, write 18-20, check 3
What the examiner is really looking for
  • Content first. Your ideas are the vehicle for your language. A coherent, developed argument naturally shows off your English.
  • A journey. The best answers unfold their ideas in a logical order and end with a conclusion that clinches the point.
  • Sustained accuracy and variety. Question 4 needs no new 'special' feature. It needs the same accurate, varied English as Questions 2 and 3, kept up over a longer piece.
  • Sophisticated = the best choice. Precise, appropriate words used accurately, not rare words for show.
  • No memorised paragraphs. Learn structures and phrases, not whole answers. Pre-learnt text rarely fits the question.

3. How the 23 marks work (bands in plain English)

Two separate grids are used. The examiner finds the best-fit band by working upwards, then chooses a mark inside it. The descriptions below are our own paraphrase, with a column showing what each band looks like in practice.

Content and Communication (8 marks):

BandMarksDescriptionIn practice
47-8Excellent: fully relevant, detailed, wide range of ideas. Clear and coherent.3-4 developed ideas (bullets + your own), logical order, strong opening and conclusion.
35-6Good: almost always relevant, a range of detail. Generally clear with occasional ambiguity.Ideas are there but one is thin, or one sentence is confusing.
23-4Satisfactory: generally relevant, some detail. Meaning sometimes breaks down.Bullets repeated with little development. Some unclear sentences.
11-2Limited: some relevant information. Often unclear.Very short or hard to follow.

Language (15 marks):

BandMarksDescriptionIn practice
513-15Sophisticated vocabulary and structures for effect. Complex sentences handled confidently. Fluent and effective. Very high accuracy.Every sentence is controlled. Varied complex sentences (although, which, if, not only… but also). Precise words. Only the odd tiny slip.

4. How to get full marks: checklist

  1. Underline the command word, the topic and any form (article, etc.).
  2. Plan for 3 minutes (never more than 5): 3-4 ideas, an example for each, an order, an opening and an ending.
  3. Use the two bullets as a starting point and add at least one idea of your own.
  4. Write 4-5 paragraphs: introduction, two or three body paragraphs, conclusion.
  5. Start each body paragraph with a clear topic sentence.
  6. Develop each idea: point → reason → example → result.
  7. Use at least four kinds of complex sentence across the piece (see section 7).
  8. Use cohesive devices inside and between paragraphs.
  9. Choose precise vocabulary you can spell. Avoid repeating 'good', 'bad', 'very', 'thing'.
  10. Write for an adult reader: mature, polite, no slang.
  11. End with a conclusion that clinches your point (summary in fresh words + final thought or call to action).
  12. Aim for 130-150 words, then check spelling, punctuation, tenses and agreement.

5. How to plan in 3 minutes

MinuteWhat you doExample (task: Explain why friendships matter to teenagers)
0-1Decode. Circle the command word, underline the topic, note the reader (adult).EXPLAIN + why + friendships + teenagers. Reader: teacher/examiner.
1-2Brainstorm 4 ideas fast. Keep the best 3. Add an example to each.support in hard times (exam stress). Confidence and social skills (team projects). Fun and belonging (weekend football). [own idea] friends shape habits, good or bad.
2-3Order and upgrade. Number the ideas, choose an opening type and an ending, and note 3 'upgrade' features.Order: support → skills → habits. Open: short scene. End: 'choose friends wisely'. Upgrades: Although…, not only… but also, which-clause.
Write the plan as notes, not sentences

Your plan should fit in four or five lines in the margin. Single words and arrows are enough. Cross it out neatly afterwards. The plan is not marked, but it saves time and keeps your answer coherent.

7. How to reach Language 13-15

a) A range of complex sentences. Use several of these across your answer (not all in one paragraph):

StructureExample
Concession: Although / Even thoughAlthough homework is important, students also need time to rest.
Relative clause: which / who / whereCooking at home, which costs far less than eating out, also brings families together.
Conditional: If… will / wouldIf schools offered more clubs, fewer teenagers would feel bored.
Not only… but alsoVolunteering not only helps others but also builds your confidence.
Participle openerHaving tried both, I can say that reading beats scrolling.
Cause and effect: As a result / so… thatThe town grew so quickly that the old market disappeared.
PassiveMany local traditions are still passed down from grandparents.
Fronted adverbialWithout a doubt, this is the most valuable skill of all.
Short sentence for impact (after a long one)It works.

b) Sophisticated vocabulary, used accurately. Choose words that are precise for your topic and that you can spell. One well-placed word ('invaluable', 'thrive') is worth more than five misused ones.

c) Cohesive devices. These glue the piece together and help both Content (coherence) and Language.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
Firstly / To begin withintroduce the first point
Moreover / Furthermore / In additionadd a point of equal weight
However / On the other hand / Neverthelessintroduce contrast
As a result / Consequently / Thereforeshow a result
For instance / A clear example of this isgive an example
This means that / In other wordsexplain further
Admittedly / It is true that… butaccept part of the other side
Ultimately / All things considered / In shortconclude
this / these / such + nounpoint back: 'This habit…', 'Such changes…' (avoids repetition)

d) Accuracy. In the last 3 minutes, check these five things in order:

  1. Full stops: no sentences joined only with commas.
  2. Verb tenses: consistent, and correct past forms (taught, bought, chose).
  3. Subject-verb agreement: 'Many people are', 'Everyone knows'.
  4. Articles and plurals: 'a skill', 'many skills', 'the environment'.
  5. Spelling of your 'upgrade' words and of words copied from the task.

Upgrade with care

Use the 'best' column only when the word fits the meaning exactly and you are sure of the spelling. A sentence such as 'Cooking is a paramount endeavour for the juvenile populace' is not sophisticated: it is unclear and unnatural. 'Cooking is an essential skill for young people' is better.

9. How to start

  • Short scene: 'It is midnight, and Karim is still scrolling. Tomorrow he has a test.'
  • Rhetorical question: 'What does it really take to be ready for adult life?'
  • Bold claim: 'Few skills are as useful, or as underrated, as cooking.'
  • Common belief + twist: 'Many people believe that work starts after university. In reality, it should start much earlier.'
  • 'Imagine…': 'Imagine walking to school along a street lined with trees instead of cars.'
  • Personal angle (describe/explain): 'When I was ten, my street had one small shop. Today it has fifteen.'

10. How to end

  • Clinching sentence: 'In short, a week in the workplace teaches lessons that no textbook can.'
  • Call to action (persuade): 'Leave the car at home tomorrow. Your body and your city will thank you.'
  • Look to the future: 'If we learn these skills now, we will be ready for whatever comes next.'
  • Echo the opening: 'So, next time you are tempted to scroll at midnight, close the screen and open a book.'
  • Balanced judgement (explain/inform): 'All things considered, the benefits clearly outweigh the drawbacks.'

11. Model answers

Model 1: Explain150 words · Content and Communication 8/8 + Language 15/15 = 23/23
Task: Explain why learning to cook is a valuable skill for young people. You could explain: • why cooking is a useful skill • how young people can learn to cook. You should write between 100 and 150 words. [23 marks]

Many teenagers leave home for university without knowing how to boil an egg. This is a pity, because cooking is one of the most valuable skills a young person can learn.

The first reason is health. Young people who can cook are far less likely to rely on fast food, which is often high in salt, sugar and fat. When you prepare your own meals, you control exactly what goes into them.

Cooking also saves money and builds independence. A pot of lentil soup costs a fraction of the price of a takeaway, yet it can feed a whole family. Better still, a teenager who can cook never depends on others for a decent meal.

Fortunately, learning is not difficult. Teenagers can start by helping a parent at weekends, following simple recipes or joining a cookery club. If they master five basic dishes, they will be ready for adult life.

Why this gets the marks
  • Purpose (explain): every paragraph answers 'why' (health, money, independence) or 'how' (helping a parent, recipes, a club).
  • Bullets used and extended: bullet 1 is developed into two separate reasons (health, then money and independence). Bullet 2 becomes a practical final paragraph with three methods.
  • Wide range of detail: concrete examples: 'boil an egg', 'salt, sugar and fat', 'lentil soup', 'a decent meal', 'five basic dishes'.
  • Coherent journey: problem in the opening → two reasons → solution → confident final sentence that clinches the point.
  • Complex sentences handled confidently: 'because' clause, relative clauses ('which is often high in…', 'a teenager who can cook…'), 'When…' clause, contrast with 'yet', conditional ('If they master…, they will…').
  • Sophisticated, natural vocabulary: 'rely on', 'a fraction of', 'builds independence', 'master'.
  • Adult audience: mature, clear tone with no slang. Humour ('without knowing how to boil an egg') used lightly for effect.
  • Accuracy: correct commas in the list, correct use of 'far less likely', consistent present tense.
Model 2: Argue (article)148 words · Content and Communication 8/8 + Language 15/15 = 23/23
Task: Write an article to argue that all students should do work experience before they leave school. You could write about: • why work experience is valuable • how schools could organise it. Your article should be between 100 and 150 words. [23 marks]

The Lesson Every Student Needs

Can a classroom alone prepare teenagers for the world of work? In my view, it cannot, which is why every student should complete a work placement before leaving school.

Firstly, a placement shows students what a job is really like. A pupil who dreams of becoming a doctor may discover, after a week in a clinic, that the career suits them perfectly, or that another path would be better.

Furthermore, work experience builds confidence. Speaking politely to customers and arriving on time are skills that are learnt by doing, not by reading.

Critics claim that placements waste valuable study time. However, a single week in the summer holiday would not harm anyone's grades.

Schools could easily build links with local businesses and hospitals, and parents could offer placements at their own workplaces. Ultimately, a week at work teaches lessons that no textbook can.

Why this gets the marks
  • Purpose (argue): a clear position at the end of the opening ('every student should…'), two supporting arguments, a counter-argument that is answered, and a firm conclusion.
  • Bullets used and extended: bullet 1 → paragraphs 2 and 3 (career choice and confidence). Bullet 2 → the final paragraph (links with businesses and hospitals, placements with parents). The answer to the critics (a week in the holidays) is an extra idea.
  • Detail: the doctor/clinic example makes the abstract argument real.
  • Coherence: linking devices ('Firstly', 'Furthermore', 'However', 'Ultimately') take the reader on a journey.
  • Sophisticated structures for effect: opening rhetorical question. Relative clauses ('which is why…', 'who dreams of…'). An embedded phrase ('after a week in a clinic'). Passive ('are learnt'). Contrast 'by doing, not by reading'. Conditional 'would not harm'.
  • Precise vocabulary: placement, career, suits them perfectly, build links, valuable.
  • Accuracy and British spelling: 'learnt', the apostrophe in 'anyone's', commas around the embedded phrase 'after a week in a clinic'.

12. Weaker vs stronger: one paragraph compared

Task: Explain why teenagers need enough sleep. Here is the same idea written twice.

Weaker version (about Language band 2, 4-6): 'Sleep is very important. It is very good for students. If you dont sleep you will be tired and you cant study good. Also sleep is good for health. So students must sleep 8 hours every day because it is very important.'

Stronger version (Language band 5, 13-15): 'Sleep is far more than a break from studying; it is when the brain stores what we have learnt during the day. A teenager who regularly goes to bed after midnight will struggle to concentrate in morning lessons, and their marks may suffer as a result. Although it is tempting to watch one more episode, switching off screens an hour before bed is a small change that brings significant rewards.'

FeatureWeakerStronger
VocabularyRepeats 'very important', 'good'. 'study good' is wrongPrecise: 'stores', 'struggle to concentrate', 'significant rewards'
SentencesShort and simple. Ideas joined with 'and', 'also', 'so'A semicolon joining two related clauses, relative clauses ('who…', 'that…'), concession ('Although…'), result ('as a result')
DetailGeneral: 'you will be tired'Specific: after midnight, morning lessons, one more episode, an hour before bed
DevelopmentPoints listed, not explainedPoint → reason → effect → practical advice
AccuracyMissing apostrophes (dont, cant). 'good' used as an adverbAccurate throughout
EndingRepeats the first sentenceEnds with a useful, memorable idea

14. Practice tasks with plans

خطة الدقائق الثلاث
  • الدقيقة الأولى: حدّد فعل السؤال (explain / argue / persuade / inform / describe) والموضوع.
  • الدقيقة الثانية: اكتب 3 أو 4 أفكار ومثالاً لكل فكرة، وواحدة منها على الأقل من عندك.
  • الدقيقة الثالثة: رتّب الأفكار، واختر بداية ونهاية، وحدّد 3 تراكيب قوية ستستخدمها.

  1. Task 1 (explain): Explain why friendships are important for teenagers. You could explain: why friends matter, how teenagers can be good friends. Write a plan.

    Show answer
    Opening: short scene (a student alone at break vs laughing with friends). P1: support during stress, e.g. before exams or family problems. P2: social skills and confidence: teamwork, listening. P3 (own idea): friends shape habits, so choose wisely. P4 (how): listen, keep promises, make time offline. Conclusion: 'Good friends do not just make school bearable; they make us better people.' Upgrades: relative clause, 'not only… but also', 'Although…'.

  2. Task 2 (persuade): Write an article to persuade adults to walk or cycle for short journeys instead of driving. You could write about: the benefits for health, the benefits for the town. Write a plan.

    Show answer
    Headline: 'Leave the Keys at Home'. Opening: 'Imagine reaching work feeling energised rather than stuck in traffic.' P1 (you): exercise without a gym, better mood, saves fuel money. P2 (town): quieter, cleaner streets. Safer for children. P3 (objection): 'too hot/too far' → early mornings, only trips under 2 km, shaded routes. Call to action: 'Try it for one week.' Final line: 'Your body and your city will thank you.'

  3. Task 3 (inform): Write an article informing visitors about a traditional celebration in your country. You could write about: what happens during the celebration, why it is important to people. Write a plan.

    Show answer
    Opening: what and when (e.g. Sham El-Nessim in spring). P1: what people do: picnics in parks and by the Nile, traditional foods, coloured eggs for children. P2: why it matters: one of the oldest celebrations, shared by everyone, family time. P3: advice for visitors: go early, parks get crowded, try the traditional dishes carefully. Conclusion: invitation to join in. Neutral, informative tone with passives ('eggs are painted').

  4. Task 4 (argue): Write an article to argue that young people should learn a practical skill, such as first aid or repairing things, at school. You could write about: why practical skills are useful, how schools could teach them. Write a plan.

    Show answer
    Opening: rhetorical question 'Could you help if a classmate fainted?'. P1: real-life emergencies → first aid saves lives. P2: independence and saving money (repairs, basic maintenance). P3: counter: 'exams matter more' → one lesson a fortnight. Practical skills also build confidence. P4 (how): visiting experts, a weekly workshop, certificates. Conclusion: 'Knowledge is powerful, but knowing what to do is priceless.'

  5. Task 5 (describe how): Describe how your town or neighbourhood has changed in recent years. You could write about: what it used to be like, what it is like now. Write a plan.

    Show answer
    Opening: personal angle 'When I was ten, my street had one small shop.' P1 (then): quiet, open land, children playing outside. P2 (now): new buildings, cafés, traffic. Contrast with 'whereas'. P3 (feelings/why): more convenience but less green space. Neighbours know each other less. Conclusion: balanced reflection + hope for parks. Use past simple/'used to' vs present perfect ('has become').

  6. Upgrade this sentence to Language band 5 without making it unclear: 'Sport is good. It makes you healthy and it makes you happy and you can make friends.'

    Show answer
    Possible answer: 'Regular sport not only keeps the body healthy but also lifts our mood, and it often leads to lasting friendships.' ('not only… but also', precise verbs 'lifts', 'leads to', no repetition of 'makes'.)

  7. A student writes 220 words for Question 4. Will they lose marks for length?

    Show answer
    Not directly: every word is credited and there is no penalty for going over. But long answers usually lose focus, contain more errors and take time from checking, so aim for 130-150 words.
Final self-check for Question 4
  • Command word answered in every paragraph?
  • At least one idea beyond the two bullets?
  • Clear opening, 2-3 developed body paragraphs, clinching conclusion?
  • Four or more types of complex sentence?
  • Precise vocabulary, used accurately?
  • Cohesive devices between paragraphs?
  • Adult reader in mind? 100-150 words? Five-point accuracy check done?
