The Reading Paper (9280/R)

How the Reading paper works: four texts, 60 marks in 75 minutes, how marks are given, a minute-by-minute timing plan and a step-by-step method.

British & IGCSE › Oxford International GCSE English as a Second Language

The Reading paper is worth 30% of the whole qualification, the same as Writing. The good news: it is the paper where careful technique gives the biggest improvement. You do not need perfect English to score well. You need to find the right information, understand it and show that you understood it. This page explains how the paper works and how to tackle it from the first minute to the last.

The four texts

The texts get longer and harder, and the marks increase: 6, then 12, then 18, then 24. That means Text 4 alone is worth 40% of the paper. Many students spend too long on the easy texts and rush the most valuable one. Do not make that mistake.

TextTypical typeLengthMarksSkills tested
Text 1A short everyday text, e.g. an email to a friend200-250 words6R1, R2
Text 2e.g. an advert, brochure, guide or information article400-500 words12R1, R2, R3, R4
Text 3e.g. an opinion article from a newspaper, magazine or website400-600 words18R1, R2, R3, R4
Text 4Literary non-fiction, e.g. travel writing, a memoir or an autobiography extract400-600 words24R2, R3, R4

Text types you may meet across the paper include emails, instructions, public notices, adverts, brochures, guides, letters, newspaper and magazine articles, websites and literary texts. The last text is always literary non-fiction.

What is literary non-fiction?

It is a true story or experience, written in a descriptive, personal way, like a novel. The writer uses 'I', describes places and feelings, and uses imagery (comparisons, strong adjectives, figurative phrases). Examples: a travel writer describing a desert journey, a memoir about a grandparent or an autobiography of an athlete. Questions on Text 4 often ask you to explain descriptive phrases and to understand the writer's feelings.

In real papers, longer texts are often printed in parts (for example, 'Read the first part of the article. Answer questions 07 to 10.'). The questions usually follow the order of the text, and the paragraphs are numbered so the question can send you to the right place ('In paragraph 3, ...').

The four reading skills (strands) in simple words

StrandWhat it meansTypical question
R1 Identify and selectFind a specific detail: a date, a name, a place, a price, a reason.'In which city do the classes take place?'
R2 Understand vocabulary, ideas and purposeKnow what words and phrases mean, what a paragraph is mainly about and why the text was written.'Explain in your own words what ... means.' 'What is the main purpose of the text?'
R3 Collate, organise and presentCollect several details from different places and list them.'List three things the writer says about the market.'
R4 Infer and deduceWork out ideas that are suggested but not stated directly: feelings, attitudes, hidden meaning.'Which two statements are correct?' 'How does the writer feel when ...?'

How the marks are given

  • Understanding is what counts. This is a reading test, not a writing test. If your answer shows the right meaning, you get the mark, even with grammar mistakes.
  • Spelling errors are accepted when the meaning is not lost and the word, said aloud, would still be understood. ('Recieve' for 'receive' is fine. A word that becomes a different word may not be.)
  • Full sentences are not needed. A short phrase is enough: 'the town hall' or 'by bus' can score the mark.
  • Lists: you get one mark for each correct, different point. Points on the same line can still be credited, but writing each point on its own numbered line is clearer and safer.
  • Explain-a-phrase questions: the number of marks tells you the number of ideas. A 2-mark phrase has two ideas to explain (often one for each key word); a 3-mark sentence has three.
  • Multiple choice: you write the letter(s) in the box(es). The number of boxes equals the number of marks. A 4-mark question needs exactly four letters, in any order.
  • Do not over-answer. If a question asks for two things, give two. Extra answers do not earn extra marks, and a wrong extra idea can cancel or change the meaning of a correct one.
One small word can lose the mark

Be careful with words that change meaning: not, never, only, little/a little, few/a few, no longer, except. 'He gave little help' (almost none) is the opposite of 'He gave a little help' (some). If you copy a phrase, copy it exactly and do not drop these words.

Timing plan: 75 minutes

A simple rule: roughly one minute per mark, plus reading time for each text, plus a few minutes to check. Here is a plan that works for most students.

StageMarksSuggested timeNotes
Look through the whole paper2 minutesSee the four texts and their marks. Remind yourself that Text 4 is worth 24.
Text 167 minutesShort and direct. Do not over-check easy answers.
Text 21214 minutesMany details (prices, dates, ages). Scan carefully.
Text 31820 minutesOpinions and arguments. Watch for good/bad points and the writer's view.
Text 42427 minutesThe hardest text and the most marks. Read slowly; explain phrases idea by idea.
Check5 minutesEmpty boxes, number of letters, number of list points, small words like 'not'.
Total6075 minutes
If you are a slower reader

Write the finishing time for each text at the top of the paper before you start (for example, if you start at 9:00: Text 1 by 9:09, Text 2 by 9:23, Text 3 by 9:43, Text 4 by 10:10). If you are stuck on one question, leave it, put a mark in the margin, and come back at the end. One hard 1-mark question must not steal the time of an easy 4-mark question.

How to tackle the paper, step by step

  1. Read the title and the line that introduces the text ('Read the first part of an email from Lina to her cousin.'). It tells you who is writing, to whom and why. That helps with purpose questions later.
  2. Read the questions for this part before reading the text closely. Underline the key words in each question: the question word (who, when, why, how many), the paragraph number and the main noun.
  3. Read the text once for general meaning. Do not stop at every unknown word. Ask: what is this text about? What is each paragraph about?
  4. Answer the questions in order. Go to the paragraph named in the question. Scan for the key word or a synonym of it. Read the sentences around it carefully.
  5. Decide the answer format: a letter in a box, a short phrase, a list, or an explanation in your own words.
  6. For MCQs, check every option against the text. Cross out options that are wrong. Be careful: wrong options often use words that DO appear in the text.
  7. For explain-a-phrase questions, count the marks and explain that many ideas. Use simpler words (synonyms), not the same words from the text.
  8. For list questions, give exactly the number asked, one point per line, each point different.
  9. Answer the 'whole text' question last (main purpose / which statement best describes the text). By then you understand the whole text.
  10. Check at the end: every box filled, right number of letters, nothing left blank.

Answering open questions well

Open questions ask you to write your own short answer. Keep it short, precise and from the text. You may use words from the text for detail questions, but for 'explain in your own words' you must use different words.

QuestionWeak answerStrong answerWhy
When does the museum close on Fridays? [1]In the evening it is closing late on some days.9 pmExact detail. Short is best.
Why did Karim stop playing football? [1]Football.He injured his knee.Answers the 'why' with a reason, not a topic.
List two things volunteers do at the shelter. [2]They help a lot and they are kind and they do things.1 feed the animals 2 clean the cagesTwo specific, different actions.
Explain in your own words 'a breathtaking view'. [2]A breathtaking view.Something you can see from a place that is extremely beautiful/amazing.Two ideas explained: 'view' (what you see) + 'breathtaking' (extremely beautiful).

Answering multiple-choice questions well

  • Read the question, then try to answer it in your head before you look at the options. Then find the option that matches your idea.
  • Every wrong option is designed to look possible. Often it uses a real word from the text but changes a detail (the day, the number, the person) or makes it too general or too extreme.
  • 'Main point of paragraph X': choose the option that covers the WHOLE paragraph, not just one sentence.
  • 'Main purpose of the text': think about why the writer wrote it: to inform, to advise, to persuade, to invite, to thank, to describe an experience. Often two purposes are combined ('to thank ... and tell her about ...').
  • 'Which two/four statements are correct?': check each statement against the text. Write exactly the number of letters asked. The order does not matter.
  • Write the letter clearly in capitals. If you change your mind, cross out the old letter neatly so the examiner can see your final answer.

The top 10 mistakes (and how to avoid them)

MistakeWhat to do instead
Spending 20 minutes on Text 1 and rushing Text 4Follow the timing plan. Text 4 is worth 24 marks.
Answering from your own knowledge, not the textThe answer must come from the text, even if you know more about the topic.
Looking in the wrong paragraphAlways check the paragraph number in the question.
Copying the same word in 'explain in your own words'Use a synonym or a short explanation for each key word.
Explaining only one idea in a 2- or 3-mark phraseCount the marks; explain that many ideas.
Writing three letters when the question needs twoCount the boxes; they equal the marks.
Giving the same point twice in a listEach point must be different. 'Cheap' and 'not expensive' are the same point.
Missing a negative or a limiting wordUnderline not, never, only, except, no longer in both the question and the text.
Choosing an MCQ option because it contains words from the textCheck that the whole option matches the meaning, not just one word.
Leaving blanksAlways write a sensible answer. A blank cannot score.

Quick check: test yourself

  1. How many marks is Text 3 worth, and how long is the text?

    Show answer
    18 marks, and the text is 400-600 words long.

  2. What type of text is always the last text?

    Show answer
    Literary non-fiction.

  3. A multiple-choice question is worth 2 marks. How many letters do you write?

    Show answer
    Exactly two.

  4. Will you lose the mark if you spell 'beautiful' as 'beautifull'?

    Show answer
    No. Spelling errors are accepted when the meaning is clear.

  5. A question asks you to explain 'a hectic schedule' for 2 marks. How many ideas do you need?

    Show answer
    Two: 'hectic' (very busy, rushed) and 'schedule' (plan / timetable of activities).

  6. How long should you spend on Text 4?

    Show answer
    About 25-28 minutes: it is worth 24 marks, the biggest share of the paper.

  7. Which question should you answer last for each text, and why?

    Show answer
    The 'main purpose' or 'which statement best describes the whole text' question, because you understand the whole text by then.
كيف تحصل على الدرجة النهائية في ورقة القراءة؟
  • الامتحان يقيس الفهم وليس الكتابة: الإجابة القصيرة الصحيحة تأخذ الدرجة حتى لو فيها أخطاء نحوية أو إملائية بسيطة.
  • عدد الدرجات = عدد الأفكار المطلوبة. سؤال شرح عبارة عليه درجتان؟ اشرح كلمتين/فكرتين بكلمات من عندك.
  • في أسئلة الاختيار اكتب عدد الحروف بالضبط مثل عدد المربعات، ولا تكتب حرفًا زيادة.
  • النص الرابع عليه 24 درجة (40% من الورقة)، فلا تضيّع الوقت في النص الأول السهل.
  • خذ الإجابة من النص فقط، وليس من معلوماتك العامة.
أكثر الأخطاء شيوعًا

البحث في فقرة غير المطلوبة، ونسيان كلمات مثل not و only، وتكرار نفس الكلمة من النص في سؤال 'اشرح بكلماتك'، وترك أسئلة بدون إجابة. ضع خطًا تحت الكلمات المهمة في السؤال قبل أن تجيب.
