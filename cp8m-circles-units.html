Circumference of a Circle, and Miles and Kilometres

Discover π as circumference ÷ diameter, use C = πd and C = 2πr (forwards and backwards), and convert between miles and kilometres with 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km and 1 km ≈ 5/8 mile.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Understand that π (pi) is the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter (circumference ÷ diameter). Know and use the formula for the circumference of a circle: C = πd (or C = 2πr). (8Gg.02)
  • Know that distances can be measured in miles or kilometres: 1 mile is about 1.6 km, and 1 km is about 5/8 of a mile. Use these facts to convert. (8Gg.03)

2. What is π?

Take any circle: a coin, a cup, a plate, a bicycle wheel. Measure its circumference (wrap a string round it) and its diameter. Then work out circumference ÷ diameter.

ObjectDiameter d (cm)Circumference C (cm)C ÷ d (2 d.p.)
coin2.57.83.12
cup8.025.23.15
plate26.081.63.14
bicycle wheel66.0207.53.14

For every circle, big or small, C ÷ d gives the same number, just over 3. (The small differences in the table come from measuring errors.) This number is called π (the Greek letter pi). Its decimal digits never end and never repeat: π = 3.14159… We usually use π ≈ 3.14 or the π key on a calculator.

The formulae
  • π = C ÷ d (π is the ratio of circumference to diameter).
  • So C = π × d, usually written C = πd.
  • Because d = 2r, also C = 2 × π × r = 2πr.
  • To find the diameter from the circumference: d = C ÷ π. Then r = d ÷ 2.

لو قسمت محيط أي دائرة على قطرها هيطلع لك نفس الرقم دايمًا، تقريبًا 3.14 — ده اسمه π. يعني المحيط أطول من القطر حوالي 3 مرات وشوية. القانون: المحيط = π × القطر (C = πd)، ولو معاك نصف القطر: المحيط = 2 × π × نصف القطر. وخلي بالك: لو السؤال إداك نصف القطر، اضربه في 2 الأول.

3. Finding the circumference, step by step

  1. Read the question: is the length a radius or a diameter? If it is a radius, double it (or use C = 2πr).
  2. Write the formula: C = πd.
  3. Substitute: for example C = π × 12.
  4. Calculate. With a calculator use the π key. Without a calculator use the value the question gives (often 3.14).
  5. Round as the question asks (for example to 1 decimal place) and write the unit (cm, m…). Circumference is a length, so the unit is NOT squared.
  6. Check: the answer should be a bit more than 3 × the diameter.

Perimeter of a semicircle = half the circumference + the diameter (the straight edge). Many students forget the straight edge.

4. Miles and kilometres

Distances between towns are measured in kilometres in most countries (Egypt uses km), but in some countries, such as the UK and the USA, road signs and speeds use miles. A mile is longer than a kilometre.

The facts to learn (8Gg.03)
  • 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km → miles to km: multiply by 1.6 (or × 8 ÷ 5).
  • 1 km ≈ 5/8 of a mile → km to miles: multiply by 5/8 (divide by 8, then multiply by 5).
  • Easy pair to remember: 5 miles ≈ 8 km.
  • Both facts are approximate (a mile is really a little more than 1.6 km), so use ≈ and 'about'.

Check your answer makes sense: the number of kilometres is always bigger than the number of miles for the same distance (because a kilometre is the shorter unit, you need more of them).

Because miles and kilometres are in direct proportion, a conversion graph is a straight line through (0, 0). To use it, go up from the value on one axis to the line, then across to the other axis.

5. Worked examples

Worked example 1 [2 marks] (calculator) A circular clock face has diameter 12 cm. Calculate its circumference. Give your answer correct to 1 decimal place.

  1. C = πd = π × 12
  2. = 37.699… (calculator)
  3. Answer: 37.7 cm (1 mark for π × 12 seen, 1 mark for 37.7).
  4. Check: 3 × 12 = 36, and 37.7 is a bit more. ✓

Worked example 2 [2 marks] (calculator) A circular flower bed has radius 4.5 m. Calculate its circumference to 1 decimal place.

  1. Radius given, so d = 2 × 4.5 = 9 m (or use C = 2πr).
  2. C = π × 9 = 28.274…
  3. Answer: 28.3 m.

Common mistake: π × 4.5 = 14.1 m. That uses the radius as if it were the diameter, giving only half the circumference.

Worked example 3 [1 mark] (no calculator) Use π = 3.14 to work out the circumference of a circle with diameter 10 cm.

C = 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm. (Multiplying by 10 moves each digit one place to the left.)

Worked example 4 [2 marks] (calculator) The circumference of a round tray is 50 cm. Work out its diameter, correct to 1 decimal place.

  1. C = πd, so d = C ÷ π.
  2. d = 50 ÷ π = 15.915…
  3. Answer: 15.9 cm. (If the question asks for the radius: 15.915… ÷ 2 = 7.96 → 8.0 cm.)

Worked example 5 [3 marks] (calculator) The diagram shows a semicircle with diameter 8 cm. Calculate its perimeter to 1 decimal place.

  1. Full circumference = π × 8 = 25.132…
  2. Curved part = half of that = 12.566…
  3. Perimeter = curved part + straight diameter = 12.566… + 8 = 20.566…
  4. Answer: 20.6 cm. (1 mark for π × 8 ÷ 2, 1 mark for adding 8, 1 mark for 20.6.)

Worked example 6 [3 marks] (calculator) A bicycle wheel has diameter 70 cm. How far does the bicycle travel when the wheel turns 100 times? Give your answer in metres, to the nearest metre.

  1. One turn moves the bicycle one circumference: π × 70 = 219.91… cm.
  2. 100 turns: 219.91… × 100 = 21 991 cm.
  3. Convert: 21 991 ÷ 100 = 219.9 m.
  4. Answer: 220 m.

Worked example 7 [1 mark] A road sign in England says 'London 45 miles'. About how many kilometres is this? 45 × 1.6 = 45 + (45 × 0.6) = 45 + 27 = 72 km.

Worked example 8 [2 marks] A journey is 120 km. Use 1 km ≈ 5/8 mile to change this to miles. 120 ÷ 8 = 15, then 15 × 5 = 75 miles. Check: 75 is smaller than 120 ✓ (miles are fewer).

Worked example 9 [2 marks] Route A is 50 miles long. Route B is 85 km long. Which route is shorter, and by how many kilometres? Change to the same unit: 50 miles ≈ 50 × 1.6 = 80 km. 80 km < 85 km, so Route A is shorter, by about 5 km.

Common mistake: comparing 50 with 85 directly. You must change both distances to the same unit first.

Common mistakes
  • Using the radius in C = πd. If you are given r, double it first, or use C = 2πr.
  • Mixing up circumference and area. Circumference is a length (cm), not cm².
  • Forgetting the straight edge (the diameter) when finding the perimeter of a semicircle.
  • Rounding too early. Keep the full calculator value until the last step, then round.
  • Converting the wrong way: dividing by 1.6 to change miles to km. Ask yourself: should the number get bigger or smaller?
  • Writing 1 km = 1.6 miles. It is 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • "Calculate the circumference… Give your answer correct to 1 decimal place." Show the substitution (π × 12) for the method mark.
  • Paper 1 (no calculator) gives you a value such as π = 3.14, or asks you to estimate with π ≈ 3. Paper 2 (calculator): use the π key.
  • Reverse questions: the circumference is given, find the diameter or radius (divide by π).
  • Real contexts: wheels turning, running tracks, fences round circular gardens, semicircular windows.
  • "Draw a ring around" the best estimate of a distance in miles, or complete a conversion table.
  • Conversion graphs: read across and up carefully; use the grid squares to find the scale first.
  • "Explain" questions: 'π is circumference ÷ diameter, so…' and 'kilometres are shorter than miles, so the number is bigger'.

Practice

Remember
  • π = circumference ÷ diameter ≈ 3.14, the same for every circle.
  • C = πd = 2πr. If you know C, then d = C ÷ π.
  • Semicircle perimeter = half of πd + the diameter.
  • 1 mile ≈ 1.6 km and 1 km ≈ 5/8 mile (5 miles ≈ 8 km).
  • Miles → km: × 1.6 (number gets bigger). km → miles: ÷ 8 × 5 (number gets smaller).
