IGCSE 0510 · Paper 1 Writing

Writing a Report (Exercise 6)

Everything you need to write a full-mark Exercise 6 report: the four report types, headings, impersonal language, passives, reporting verbs, recommendations, and two annotated model reports.

British & IGCSE › Cambridge IGCSE English as a Second Language (0510)

A report is one of the four text types that can appear in Exercise 6 of Paper 1. It is the most ‘businesslike’ of them: you are not trying to entertain the reader, you are giving a person in charge clear information and sensible suggestions so that they can make a decision. If you learn the layout, the formal phrases and the four common types on this page, a report can become your safest 15 marks in the whole paper.

1. What the task is and where it sits in the paper

ItemWhat you need to know
PaperPaper 1 Reading and Writing (2 hours, 60 marks). Exercises 1–4 are reading (30 marks). Exercises 5–6 are writing (30 marks).
ExerciseExercise 6 – the last question in the paper (Question 17).
Text types that can be setArticle, report, essay or review. You do not choose: the question tells you which one to write.
RegisterFormal or semi-formal. A report is normally at the formal end, because it is written for a head teacher, a manager, a council or the organisers of an event.
LengthAbout 120–160 words.
Marks15: Content up to 6 + Language up to 9.
What the question gives youA short situation, the reader of the report, what the report must cover, and some comments (sometimes a picture). You use some of these ideas and add ideas of your own.

What is a report? A report is written for a superior (for example, your head teacher) or for a group (for example, the organisers of an event). It describes an event, an experience or a situation, gives the facts, and ends with suggestions or recommendations.

How a report task usually looks (original example)
  • Situation: ‘Your school recently held an International Food Day. The head teacher has asked you to write a report about the day.’
  • What to cover: ‘In your report, say what went well and suggest how the day could be improved next year.’
  • Comments: four short opinions from students, e.g. ‘The Lebanese stall sold out in an hour!’ / ‘The queues were far too long.’
  • Instruction: ‘Now write a report for the head teacher. The comments above may give you some ideas, and you should also use some ideas of your own. Write about 120 to 160 words.’

Underline three things before you write a single word: WHO reads it (the head teacher → formal), WHAT you must cover (usually two parts, e.g. ‘what went well’ + ‘how to improve’) and WHY it is being written (so that someone can make a decision).

2. Marks: what each band needs

Exercise 5 and Exercise 6 are marked with the same two tables. The examiner chooses the band that fits best, then a mark inside it.

Content (6 marks)What the examiner is looking for in a report
5–6The task is completely fulfilled and everything is relevant. The style is right for a report from start to finish (formal, impersonal, headings). The reader and the purpose are always clear. Ideas are well developed with reasons, details and consequences.
3–4The task is generally fulfilled. Most content is relevant, the style is generally suitable, and ideas are developed to some extent – but perhaps one part of the task is thin, or the tone slips into a chatty email.
1–2Only part of the task is done (e.g. no recommendations at all), some content is irrelevant, the style is not suitable, and ideas are hardly developed.
0Nothing that can be credited.
Language (9 marks)What the examiner is looking for
7–9Ideas are communicated skilfully. A wide range of common and less common vocabulary, and a wide range of simple and complex structures (passives, relative clauses, conditionals). High accuracy – a few small slips do not get in the way. Well organised, with a wide range of linking devices.
4–6Clear communication. Common vocabulary with some attempts at less common words. Mainly simple structures with some complex ones. Generally accurate. Generally well organised with some linking.
1–3Meaning is there but not always clear. Only common words and simple sentences. Errors often get in the way. Little organisation or linking.
0Nothing that can be credited.
What ‘development’ means in a report

Each point needs a second layer. Weak: ‘The food was good.’ Developed: ‘The food stalls were very popular, especially the Lebanese stall, which had sold out by eleven o’clock. This suggests that more food should be ordered next year.’ Point → detail or example → result or suggestion.

3. How to get full marks: checklist

  1. A clear title that says what the report is about (e.g. ‘Report on the International Food Day’).
  2. Short headings for each section (Introduction / What went well / Problems / Recommendations).
  3. An introduction that states the purpose of the report and, if possible, how the information was collected (questionnaire, interviews, your own visit).
  4. Both parts of the task covered in roughly equal detail.
  5. At least one or two ideas from the comments + at least one or two of your own ideas.
  6. Every point developed with a reason, an example or a result.
  7. Impersonal, formal tone: ‘Most students felt…’, ‘It was suggested that…’ – not ‘I loved it!!’.
  8. Passive voice at least twice (‘the stalls were set up’, ‘it is recommended that…’).
  9. Reporting verbs (said, complained, pointed out, suggested, admitted) and some reported speech.
  10. Recommendations that match the problems you described.
  11. Formal linking words: in addition, however, as a result, therefore, overall.
  12. No contractions (do not, it was not), no slang, no exclamation marks, no emojis.
  13. About 120–160 words – count your lines, not every word.

4. Step-by-step structure

StepHeading (example)What to writeApprox. words

Headings: keep them short

Headings are part of the layout, not decoration. Two to four words each is enough. Do not write headings as questions, and do not use more than four or five: with only 160 words, every heading costs you space.

5. The four types of report you should be ready for

TypeTypical situationReaderMiddle headingsLanguage you need most
(a) Event / trip / festivalA school trip, a sports day, a charity fair, a cultural festivalHead teacher, organisers, a teacherWhat went well · Problems · RecommendationsPast tenses, past passive (‘the coaches were booked’), reporting verbs (‘students complained that…’)
(b) Survey / research reportYou asked students about sleep, travel, screen time, eating habitsHead teacher, school council, a teacherMethod · Main findings · RecommendationsFigures in words (‘nearly two-thirds’, ‘a small minority’), present simple, comparison
(c) Place / facilityThe library, the canteen, sports facilities, a park or youth centre in your areaHead teacher, local councilCurrent situation · Strengths · Weaknesses · RecommendationsPresent simple and present perfect, ‘there is / there are not enough…’, description
(d) Proposal-style reportHow to make the school greener. Ideas for a new student common room. Spending a small budgetHead teacher, school council, a companyBackground · Suggestions (one heading each) · ConclusionModal verbs (‘could’, ‘should’), conditionals (‘If…, students would…’), persuading politely

Type (a): Report on an event, trip or festival

Audience: the head teacher or the people who organised the event. They were probably not with you all day, so they need to know what really happened. Purpose: to say what worked and what did not, so that next time is better.

  • Introduction: name the event, the date, who asked for the report and where your information comes from (e.g. ‘comments collected from thirty students’).
  • What went well: two developed positives (activity + why it worked + effect on students).
  • Problems: one or two negatives, presented fairly (‘Although most students enjoyed…, some felt…’).
  • Recommendations: one suggestion for each problem + an overall verdict (‘worth repeating’).

Type (b): Survey or research report

Audience: usually the head teacher or a teacher who wants evidence before changing something. Purpose: to present findings honestly and recommend action based on them.

  • Introduction / Method: what you wanted to find out, how many people answered, how (questionnaire, interviews), and when.
  • Main findings: the most important results first, using figures in words and comparison. Add a reason that students gave.
  • Further findings / Concerns: a smaller result or a worrying detail.
  • Recommendations: actions that follow logically from the numbers.
Instead of…Write…

about 25%roughly a quarter / one in four
90%the vast majority / almost all

Type (c): Report on a place or facility

Audience: often the head teacher (for a school facility) or the local council (for a park, sports centre or youth club). Purpose: to describe the present situation and recommend improvements, usually because money is going to be spent.

  • Introduction: which place, why you are reporting on it, how you found out (visited it, spoke to users).
  • Strengths / Current situation: what the place offers and who uses it.
  • Weaknesses: what is missing, broken, unsafe or unpopular, and the effect on users.
  • Recommendations: specific, realistic changes (not ‘build a cinema’) and who would benefit.

Type (d): Proposal-style report suggesting improvements

Audience: someone who will decide whether to accept your ideas – the head teacher, the school council, a local business. Purpose: to persuade politely. A proposal looks forward (what we should do), while the other types look back (what happened).

  • Introduction: what the proposal is about and why change is needed.
  • One heading for each suggestion (e.g. ‘Recycling bins’, ‘A school garden’): what to do + how + the benefit.
  • Possible difficulty: (optional) one problem and how to solve it. This shows mature thinking.
  • Conclusion: why the plan is worth doing. A polite final recommendation.

6. How to start: openings for the introduction

Purpose of the report

  • As requested, this report summarises [the Year 9 trip to…] and suggests how it could be improved.
  • The purpose of this report is to describe [the facilities in…] and to recommend some changes.
  • This report aims to present the main strengths and weaknesses of [the event] as seen by the students who took part.
  • Following the [science fair] held on [date], I have been asked to report on how successful it was.

How the information was collected

  • It is based on a short questionnaire completed by [thirty] students.
  • In order to write this report, I spoke to a number of students who attended.
  • The information below comes from interviews with students and staff, and from my own visit.
  • A survey of [eighty] students from Years 7 to 11 was carried out during form time.

7. Commonly used phrases and clauses for each section

Positive points (what went well)

  • The majority of students reported that they particularly enjoyed…, because it gave them the opportunity to…
  • One of the most successful aspects of the day was said to be…
  • Several students mentioned that they found… especially useful, since it helped them to…
  • It was widely agreed that… was the highlight of the [trip/event].
  • …was praised by almost everyone, mainly because…

Negative points (problems)

  • However, a significant number of students expressed disappointment with…
  • Although the event was enjoyable overall, some students complained that…, which meant that…
  • It was pointed out that…
  • A number of students felt that… did not meet their expectations.
  • It was noted that… caused difficulties, particularly when…

Survey findings

  • The survey shows that the vast majority of students…
  • Nearly two-thirds of those questioned said that…, while only a small minority…
  • Interestingly, more students… than…
  • The most common reason given was that…
  • When asked about…, most respondents admitted that…

Recommendations

  • I would therefore recommend that…
  • It is recommended that [the trip] be [moved to the morning]. (formal: be + past participle)
  • It would be advisable to…, as this would…
  • The school could consider…
  • If these changes were made, [the event] would be even more successful.
  • To sum up, while most students valued…, greater attention should be given to…

Formal linking

  • Adding: in addition, moreover, furthermore, what is more, another positive aspect was that…
  • Contrasting: however, on the other hand, nevertheless, despite this, although…
  • Cause and result: as a result, therefore, consequently, this was mainly because…, owing to…
  • Summing up: overall, in conclusion, to sum up, on the whole

8. How to end: closings for the recommendations

  • Overall, the trip was a great success and is well worth repeating, provided that the changes above are made.
  • In conclusion, the survey suggests that most students would change their habits if the school made it easier for them to do so.
  • If these recommendations are followed, the library could become a place that students actually want to use.
  • To sum up, the proposal is realistic, low-cost and would benefit the whole school community.
  • I am confident that these small changes would make next year’s festival even more enjoyable.

MistakeWhy it loses marksFix
Writing a story of your day (‘First we got on the bus, then…’)A report is not a narrative. Content becomes a summary instead of an evaluation.Organise by topic (positives / problems / recommendations), not by time.
No headings or no titleThe format is not appropriate for a report (Content).Title + 3–4 short headings.
Forgetting the second part of the taskTask only partly fulfilled – you cannot reach 5–6 for Content.Tick each part of the question as you cover it.

Personal, emotional tone (‘It was sooo boring!!’)Wrong register for the reader.Use impersonal phrases and reporting verbs.
Recommendations that do not match the problemsWeak sense of purpose.For every problem, give one clear solution.

10. Timing

A sensible plan (our advice, not an exam rule) is about 25 minutes for Exercise 6, so never leave it with only ten minutes to spare:

MinutesWhat to do
0–1Read the task twice. Underline the reader, the two parts of the task, and the comments you will use.
1–4Plan: title, 3–4 headings, 2 points per section, 1 idea of your own, matching recommendations.
4–21Write. Keep sections short and leave a space between them.
21–25Check: passives, verb tenses, subject–verb agreement, spelling of key words, no contractions.

11. Model answer 1 – Report on a school trip (type a)

Report on a school trip (event type)159 words · Content 6/6 + Language 9/9 = 15/15
Task: Last month your class went on a day trip to the Fayoum desert to see the Valley of the Whales and the waterfalls at Wadi El-Rayan. Your head teacher has asked you to write a report about the trip. In your report, say what students enjoyed and suggest how a similar trip could be improved. Comments from students: ‘The guide made the fossils really interesting.’ / ‘We spent half the day on the coach!’ / ‘The waterfalls were amazing.’ / ‘There was nowhere shady to eat lunch.’ Now write a report for your head teacher. Write about 120 to 160 words.

Report on the Year 9 Trip to Fayoum

Introduction

As requested, this report summarises our class trip to Fayoum on 12th March. It is based on a short questionnaire completed by twenty-six students.

What students enjoyed

The trip was considered a great success. Most students praised the guide, who explained how the whale fossils had been formed when the desert was covered by the sea. In addition, the walk to the waterfalls was described as the highlight of the day, since few students had seen such a landscape before.

Problems

However, several students complained that the coach journey took almost four hours, which left little time for exploring. It was also pointed out that there was no shade at lunchtime, so some students felt unwell in the heat.

Recommendations

I would therefore recommend leaving at six o’clock and booking a picnic area with covered seating. If these changes are made, the trip will be well worth repeating next year.

Why this gets the marks
  • Format (Content): a clear title and four short headings. No greeting or signature – the reader instantly recognises a report.
  • Purpose and audience (Content): the first sentence says who asked for it (‘As requested’) and what it covers. The source of information (questionnaire) makes it sound like a genuine report for a head teacher.
  • Task fulfilment (Content): both parts are covered – what students enjoyed (guide, waterfalls) and how to improve (journey, shade) – and every recommendation matches a problem.
  • Use of prompts + own ideas (Content): three comments are reported in new words. Own ideas include the questionnaire, the explanation of how the fossils formed, ‘felt unwell in the heat’ and the six o’clock start.
  • Development (Content): each point has a reason or a result (‘which left little time for exploring’, ‘since few students had seen such a landscape’).
  • Range of structures (Language): passives (‘was considered’, ‘had been formed’, ‘was described’, ‘it was also pointed out’, ‘are made’), relative clauses (‘who explained…’, ‘which left…’), a first conditional, reporting verbs (praised, complained, pointed out).
  • Vocabulary (Language): less common words used precisely – summarises, questionnaire, praised, highlight, landscape, covered seating, well worth repeating.
  • Organisation (Language): formal linking – In addition, However, also, so, therefore, If…. One topic per section.
  • Word count: 159 words including the title and headings (153 in the sections themselves) – inside the 120–160 guide.

12. Model answer 2 – Survey report (type b)

Report on a student survey (survey type)150 words · Content 6/6 + Language 9/9 = 15/15
Task: Your head teacher is worried about traffic outside the school in the mornings. You carried out a survey about how students travel to school. Write a report for the head teacher. In your report, explain what you found out and recommend ways to encourage more students to walk, cycle or use the school bus. Comments from students: ‘My dad drives me because it’s quicker.’ / ‘I’d cycle if I had somewhere safe to leave my bike.’ / ‘The school bus costs too much for my family.’ / ‘Walking wakes me up before lessons.’ Write about 120 to 160 words.

Report on How Students Travel to School

Purpose

This report presents the results of a survey on travel to school. Eighty students from Years 7 to 11 answered a questionnaire last week.

Main findings

Nearly two-thirds of those questioned are driven to school by their parents, while roughly a quarter use the school bus. Only a small minority walk or cycle. Many respondents admitted that they are often late because of the queue of cars outside the main gate.

Interestingly, several students said they would cycle if bicycles could be left somewhere safe. However, others felt that the bus fare was too high for families with more than one child.

Recommendations

It is recommended that a covered bicycle shelter be built near the sports hall. In addition, the school could offer a reduced fare for brothers and sisters. These measures would reduce traffic and help students to arrive on time.

Why this gets the marks
  • Format (Content): title plus three headings that suit a survey report (Purpose / Main findings / Recommendations).
  • Method stated (Content): the introduction says how many students answered and how – exactly what a head teacher would want to know before acting.
  • Figures in words (Language): ‘nearly two-thirds’, ‘roughly a quarter’, ‘only a small minority’ – less common vocabulary that also shows comparison.
  • Use of prompts + own ideas (Content): the cycling and bus-fare comments are reported in new words. The late-arrival problem, the queue at the gate, the shelter near the sports hall and the sibling discount are the writer’s own ideas.
  • Purpose (Content): findings lead logically to recommendations, and the last sentence explains the benefit for the head teacher’s problem (traffic, punctuality).
  • Range of structures (Language): passives (‘are driven’, ‘could be left’, ‘be built’), the formal subjunctive ‘It is recommended that… be built’, a second conditional (‘would cycle if…’), reporting verbs (admitted, said, felt).
  • Organisation (Language): Interestingly, However, In addition, while, because of – a wide range of linking devices. One paragraph per idea.
  • Register (Content): impersonal throughout – no ‘I’ except the implied writer. No contractions.
  • Word count: 150 words including the title and headings – inside the 120–160 guide.

14. Practice tasks

Write one of these in about 25 minutes. Then open the answer to compare your plan with ours.

  1. Task 1 (event): Your school held a charity fun run to raise money for a children’s hospital. The organisers have asked you for a report. Say what was successful and how the event could be improved. Comments: ‘Great atmosphere – everyone was cheering.’ / ‘We raised more money than last year.’ / ‘There wasn’t enough water at the finish.’ / ‘It started an hour late.’ Write about 120 to 160 words. What would a good plan look like?

    Show answer
    Title: Report on the Charity Fun Run. Introduction: purpose + based on comments from runners and volunteers. What went well: lively atmosphere – parents and teachers cheered along the route, which encouraged younger runners to finish. More money raised than last year (own idea: thanks to online sponsorship forms). Problems: start delayed by an hour because registration was slow. Not enough water at the finish on a hot day. Recommendations: online registration the week before. Water stations every kilometre, sponsored by a local shop. Overall verdict: worth repeating. Language to include: ‘It was widely agreed that…’, ‘was delayed’, ‘It is recommended that…’.

  2. Task 2 (survey): Your teacher asked you to find out how much sleep students in your year get on school nights. Write a report for your teacher about what you found and what students could do. What should the introduction contain, and give three findings written with figures in words.

    Show answer
    Introduction: what you wanted to find out (hours of sleep on school nights), how (questionnaire), how many students (e.g. sixty Year 9 students), when (last week). Findings, e.g.: ‘Fewer than half of the students questioned sleep for eight hours or more.’ / ‘Roughly one in four go to bed after midnight, mainly because of phones or online games.’ / ‘The vast majority admitted that they feel tired during the first two lessons.’ Recommendations could be: an assembly on sleep, a ‘phones out of the bedroom’ challenge, and reducing homework on the night before tests.

  3. Task 3 (facility): The head teacher is planning to improve the school library and has asked students for a report. Rewrite these chatty sentences in report style: (a) ‘The library is really boring and nobody goes.’ (b) ‘They should get new books.’ (c) ‘Loads of people said the chairs hurt their backs.’

    Show answer
    (a) ‘The library is rarely used, as many students feel that it is not an attractive place to spend time.’ (b) ‘It is recommended that more up-to-date books be purchased.’ / ‘The book collection should be updated.’ (c) ‘A large number of students complained that the chairs were uncomfortable, particularly during long study periods.’

  4. Task 4 (proposal): The school has a small amount of money to improve the playground. Write a report for the head teacher suggesting how to spend it. List three headings you could use and one sentence with a conditional for each.

    Show answer
    Possible headings: ‘Shade’ – ‘If shade sails were put up over the benches, students could stay outside during hot break times.’ ‘Seating’ – ‘If more benches were added, students would have somewhere to sit and revise.’ ‘Sports equipment’ – ‘If basketball nets were replaced, more students would take part in games instead of staying indoors.’ Finish with a conclusion recommending the most useful option first.

  5. Quick check: find four things wrong with this report opening. ‘Dear Head Teacher, I’m writing to tell you about the trip!! It was super fun and we did loads of stuff.’

    Show answer
    (1) A report has a title and headings, not a letter greeting. (2) Contractions (I’m) are too informal. (3) Exclamation marks and slang (‘super fun’, ‘loads of stuff’) are the wrong register. (4) It does not state the purpose clearly or how the information was collected. Better: ‘Report on the Year 9 Trip – Introduction: As requested, this report summarises the trip to… It is based on comments collected from…’
