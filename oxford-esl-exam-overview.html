The Exam: Overview

Everything about the OxfordAQA International GCSE ESL (9280) exam: the four components, marks, timings, what each paper tests, the three themes and a 10-week study plan.

British & IGCSE › Oxford International GCSE English as a Second Language

This page is your map of the whole exam. Read it first. It tells you what the four papers are, how many marks each one carries, what the examiners want, and how to use the rest of this course to prepare. Parents can also read the Arabic box at the end for a short explanation.

The qualification in one paragraph

The OxfordAQA International GCSE English as a Second Language has the code 9280. It tests the four language skills: Reading, Writing, Listening and Speaking. Each skill has its own paper (called a component). Every student takes all four components, and all four must be taken in the same exam series. Your final grade is worked out from your total mark out of 200.

Key facts to remember
  • Four components: Reading (9280/R), Writing (9280/W), Listening (9280/L) and Speaking (9280/S).
  • Total: 200 marks. Grades 9 to 1 (9 is the highest). U means unclassified (no grade).
  • Untiered: everybody sits the same papers. There is no 'core' or 'extended' choice.
  • Linear: you take all four components at the end of the course, in one series.
  • No dictionaries are allowed in any component.
  • Exams are offered every May/June and every November.

The marks are not scaled: a mark of 45 in Reading counts as 45 of your 200. This means Reading and Writing together are worth more than half the whole qualification (120 out of 200). Listening and Speaking are worth 80 together.

Paper 1 or Paper 2?

Some official documents call Reading 'Paper 1' and Writing 'Paper 2', while others do the opposite. Do not worry about this. Use the codes: /R is Reading, /W is Writing, /L is Listening, /S is Speaking. Your timetable from school will show the code.

Grades, levels and what they mean

Grades go from 9 (top) down to 1. Grade boundaries (how many marks you need for each grade) are set on the 200-mark total after each exam series, so they can change a little from one series to the next. Nobody can tell you the exact boundary before the exam.

OxfordAQA has compared the grades with the international CEFR levels (the A1 to C2 scale used around the world for language learners). In simple terms:

GradeRoughly equal to
Grade 4About B1 in every skill (an independent user who can deal with everyday topics)
Grades 5 to 8About B2 (a confident user who can understand the main ideas of complex texts)
Grade 9C1 in Reading, Writing and Speaking, and B2 in Listening
Linear and untiered: what this means for you
  • Untiered means there is only one set of papers. Strong and weaker students sit the same exam. The questions start easy and get harder, so everybody can score marks at the start.
  • Linear means you cannot resit one paper on its own. If you want a better grade, you retake the whole qualification.
  • The Speaking result is the one exception: it can be carried forward to a later series.
  • Speaking is done at school with your teacher during a 5-week window (April to May for the summer series). It is recorded and sent to an OxfordAQA examiner, who marks it. You get one attempt.

What each paper tests: the four Assessment Objectives

An Assessment Objective (AO) is a skill the examiner is checking. There is one AO for each paper. Each AO is broken into four smaller skills called strands. Here they are in simple words.

AO and paperWeightWhat you must show (in simple words)
AO1 Reading30%R1 find the right detail · R2 understand words, ideas and the writer's purpose · R3 collect and organise several details · R4 'read between the lines' (inference) and understand hidden meaning
AO2 Writing30%W1 write short texts that give information clearly · W2 write longer, clear, well-organised texts · W3 use accurate vocabulary, grammar, spelling and punctuation · W4 use English fluently and creatively for different purposes
AO3 Listening20%L1 catch key details · L2 understand the main message · L3 understand and collect information, including harder language · L4 recognise purpose, attitudes, feelings and implied meaning
AO4 Speaking20%S1 communicate clearly for the situation · S2 use a range of accurate vocabulary and grammar · S3 speak at length, answer questions and give opinions naturally · S4 good pronunciation and intonation

The three themes: the topics behind every paper

Every text, task, recording and photocard in the exam is linked to one of three themes. If you know the vocabulary and ideas for each theme, you will understand the texts faster and have more to say and write.

ThemeTopics
Theme 1: Identity and cultureMe, my family, friends and people I know · Technology in everyday life (social media, mobile technology) · Free-time activities (music, cinema and TV, sport, customs and festivals)
Theme 2: Local, national, international and global areas of interestHome, town, neighbourhood and region · Social issues (charity/voluntary work, healthy/unhealthy living) · Global issues (the environment, poverty) · Travel and tourism
Theme 3: Current and future study and employmentMy studies · Life at school/college · Education post-16 · Jobs, career choices and ambitions

The next page, Themes and topics, gives every topic with a word bank and ready-made ideas you can use in all four papers. Study it early and come back to it often.

Inside each paper: questions, tasks and marks

Reading (9280/R): 4 texts, 60 marks. The texts get longer and harder. You answer short open questions, multiple-choice questions and collation (list) questions. The last text is always literary non-fiction (a true story told in a personal, descriptive way, such as travel writing or a memoir).

TextTypical text typeLength of textMarksMain skills

Text 3e.g. an opinion article400-600 words18R1, R2, R3, R4
Text 4Literary non-fiction400-600 words24R2, R3, R4
Total60

Writing (9280/W): 4 compulsory questions, 60 marks. Each question is worth more than the one before. Questions 1 to 3 are each based on one theme, and Question 4 can draw on any theme. Every question has a Content mark and a separate Language mark.

QuestionTaskWordsMarks
Q1Picture description: describe a photograph30-509 (Content 6 + Language 3, spelling and punctuation only)

Q4An extended open-purpose piece (explain, inform, argue or persuade), with 2 bullet points as prompts only100-15023 (Content 8 + Language 15)

Word counts

The word ranges are the same in every series. Examiners read and credit every word you write, and there is no penalty for a small difference in length. But staying close to the range is the smart choice: too short usually means missing content, and too long wastes time you need for Question 4.

How the papers are marked

  • Reading and Listening use a point-based mark scheme. You get the mark when your answer shows you understood, even if your English has small errors. Spelling mistakes are accepted if the meaning is still clear.
  • In multiple-choice questions you write letters in boxes. The number of boxes matches the number of marks, so a 2-mark question needs exactly 2 letters.
  • Writing and Speaking are marked in bands (levels). The examiner decides which band description fits your work best, then gives a mark inside that band.
  • In Writing, Content (did you do the task?) and Language (how good is your English?) are marked separately. A beautiful answer that misses a compulsory bullet point loses Content marks.

How to use this course

  1. Start here: read this page and the Themes and topics page. Learn ten new theme words every day.
  2. Reading (9280/R): read the Reading paper guide, then the question types page (one worked example of every type), then the vocabulary-in-context page. Finish with the full practice paper and mark it yourself.
  3. Writing (9280/W): work through the packs in exam order: picture description, letter/email, blog/diary, article, report and the extended piece. Each pack has a structure, openings, endings and a full-mark model answer.
  4. Listening (9280/L): learn the five task types and practise with recordings on the themes.
  5. Speaking (9280/S): prepare answers for each theme, practise photocard questions aloud and record yourself.
  6. Every week: do one timed task under exam conditions, check it with the answers, and write your mistakes in a notebook. Your mistakes notebook is the most useful revision book you will ever have.

A 10-week study plan

This plan is for the last ten weeks before the exam, but you can stretch it over a whole term. Spend about 4-5 hours a week. Each week has one main focus plus a little vocabulary and speaking every day.

Final advice from your teacher

  • Read in English every day, even for 15 minutes: news for teenagers, travel blogs, opinion articles. These are exactly the text types in the Reading paper.
  • Learn vocabulary by theme, not as random lists. Write each word in a sentence about your own life.
  • Answer the question that is asked. Underline the key words in every question before you answer.
  • Never leave a blank. A sensible guess can score, but a blank never does.
  • Speak English outside class: describe your day aloud, explain your opinions to a friend, record voice notes.

أهم نصيحة للطالب

ركِّز على القراءة والكتابة لأنهما 120 درجة من 200. في القراءة: الإجابة تُحسب إذا فهمت المعنى حتى لو فيها أخطاء لغوية بسيطة. في الكتابة: لازم تغطي كل النقاط المطلوبة في السؤال، وإلا ستخسر درجات المحتوى مهما كانت لغتك ممتازة.
