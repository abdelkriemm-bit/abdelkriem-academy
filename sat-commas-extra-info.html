Extra Information: Commas, Dashes and Brackets in Pairs

Essential vs extra information: the removal test, matching pairs, appositives, names and titles, and which / that / who clauses — with 10 practice questions.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Extra-information questions are part of the Boundaries skill in the Standard English Conventions domain (about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, 11–15 questions in total). The College Board lists supplements — appositives and parenthetical elements — as one of the things this skill checks, together with knowing when no punctuation is needed.

You see one short passage (25–150 words) with a blank and the question “Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?” The four choices usually have the same words with commas, dashes or brackets in different places — or no punctuation at all. To answer, you must decide two things: (1) is this piece of information essential or extra? and (2) if it is extra, where does it start and where does it end?

اسأل نفسك: لو شلت الكلام ده، الجملة تفضل صح ومعناها الأساسي ما يتغيرش؟ لو آه، يبقى معلومة إضافية ولازم تتحط بين علامتين متشابهتين: فاصلة…فاصلة أو شرطة…شرطة أو قوس…قوس. لو المعنى اتغير أو بقى مش واضح، يبقى معلومة أساسية ومن غير أي علامات.

Rule 1: The removal test — essential or extra?

Extra (non-essential) information adds a detail, but the sentence is still complete and keeps its main meaning without it. It is set off with punctuation. Essential information tells us *which one* we mean; without it the meaning changes or becomes unclear. Essential information takes no punctuation.

  1. Find the words that might be extra (a description, a name, a which/who clause, a phrase starting with a past participle).
  2. Cover them with your finger and read the rest of the sentence.
  3. Still complete AND still the same main meaning → extra → a matching pair of marks around it.
  4. The sentence breaks, or now means something different or unclear → essential → no marks.
SentenceRemoval testResult
My grandfather, a retired engineer, still repairs radios.“My grandfather still repairs radios.” Same meaning.Extra → commas ✓
Students who finish early may leave.“Students may leave.” Now ALL students may leave — meaning changed.Essential → no commas ✓
The Nile, which flows north, ends in the Mediterranean.“The Nile ends in the Mediterranean.” Still clear — there is only one Nile.Extra → commas ✓
The phone that I bought yesterday is broken.“The phone is broken.” Which phone? Unclear.Essential → no commas ✓
The bridge, built in 1902, is still used today.“The bridge is still used today.” Clear if the text has only one bridge.Extra → commas ✓
Of the two bridges, the bridge built in 1902 is older.“Of the two bridges, the bridge is older.” Which one? Nonsense.Essential → no commas ✓
The context decides

The same words can be essential in one passage and extra in another. “The bridge built in 1902” needs no commas if the passage talks about two bridges, but takes commas if there is only one. Always read the sentence before the blank: it often tells you whether there is one thing or many.

Rule 2: Matching pairs — comma…comma, dash…dash, bracket…bracket

Extra information in the middle of a sentence needs a mark on both sides, and the two marks must match. Commas, dashes and brackets are all correct; you may not open with one and close with another.

  • ✓ The festival, held every spring, attracts thousands of visitors.
  • ✓ The festival—held every spring—attracts thousands of visitors.
  • ✓ The festival (held every spring) attracts thousands of visitors.
  • ✗ The festival, held every spring—attracts thousands of visitors. (comma … dash)
  • ✗ The festival—held every spring, attracts thousands of visitors. (dash … comma)
  • ✗ The festival (held every spring, attracts thousands of visitors. (bracket never closed)
  • ✗ The festival, held every spring attracts thousands of visitors. (pair not closed — now one comma splits the subject from its verb)
  • ✗ The festival held every spring, attracts thousands of visitors. (pair not opened)

If the extra information comes at the end of the sentence, you need only the opening mark; the period closes it: “The prize went to Hana Fouad, a young engineer from Aswan.” If it comes at the very start, you need only the closing mark: “A retired engineer, my grandfather still repairs radios.”

Which mark should you choose? All three are correct, so the test will not ask you to choose between a correct pair of commas and a correct pair of dashes. The question is always: which mark did the writer already use? Dashes are especially useful when the extra information itself contains commas: “The three winners—Hana, Omar, and Rana—received medals.”

Look back for the first mark

When the blank comes right after some extra information, look back to where that information began. A comma, a dash or a bracket there tells you exactly which mark must close it. When the blank comes at the start of the extra information, look forward for the closing mark.

Rule 3: Appositives — a noun that renames a noun

An appositive is a noun phrase placed next to another noun to rename or describe it: “Naguib Mahfouz, a Nobel Prize winner, was born in Cairo.” Most appositives are extra and take a matching pair. But an appositive can also be essential, when it tells us which one of several things we mean.

SentenceWhyPunctuation
Aswan, a city on the Nile, is famous for its granite.There is one Aswan; the description is extra.Pair of commas ✓
Mahfouz's novel Palace Walk was published in 1956.Mahfouz wrote many novels; the title tells us which one.No commas ✓
Mahfouz's last novel, Qushtumur, was published in 1988.A writer has only one last novel; the title is extra.Pair of commas ✓
My friend Omar lent me his calculator.I have many friends; the name says which one.No commas ✓
My oldest friend, Omar, lent me his calculator.Only one person can be my oldest friend.Pair of commas ✓
  • ✗ Aswan a city on the Nile, is famous for its granite. (opening comma missing)
  • ✗ Mahfouz's novel, Palace Walk, was published in 1956. (commas suggest he wrote only one novel)

Rule 4: Names and titles — “the biologist Maria Lopez” vs “the biologist, Maria Lopez,”

This is one of the most tested comma rules on the SAT, because your ear wants a pause that grammar does not allow. Ask: does the label before the name already point to one single person?

PatternPunctuationExample
Job title or label directly before a name, no “the” or “a”No commas✓ Biologist Maria Lopez studied the reef. / ✓ Egyptian author Naguib Mahfouz …
“the” + general label + name (many people have that job)No commas — the name is essential✓ The biologist Maria Lopez studied the reef.
A description that fits only one person + namePair of commas — the name is extra✓ The team's only biologist, Maria Lopez, studied the reef.
Name first, then the descriptionPair of commas around the description✓ Maria Lopez, a biologist, studied the reef.
Only one comma between label and name, or after the name✗ Never✗ Biologist, Maria Lopez studied … / ✗ The biologist Maria Lopez, studied …

Words that make a description unique: only, first, last, oldest, current, the director of, the founder of, her husband, his mother. Words such as an, one of, a famous, the Egyptian usually mean the label is general, so the name is essential.

  • ✓ The museum's director, Amal Saad, opened the exhibition. (a museum has one director)
  • ✓ Director Amal Saad opened the exhibition. (title before name)
  • ✓ One of the curators, Samy Fathy, gave the tour. (the name is extra after a complete description)
  • ✗ The curator, Samy Fathy gave the tour. (pair not closed — comma between subject and verb)
The label + name trap

Choices such as “author, Ahmed Khaled” or “painter Laila Said,” look natural but are wrong. A label directly before a name takes no comma, and a comma after the name with no partner before it splits the subject from its verb.

Rule 5: Relative clauses — which, that, who, whose, where

WordUsePunctuationExample
whichExtra information about thingsComma before it (and after the clause if the sentence continues)✓ The Cairo Metro, which opened in 1987, carries millions of passengers.
thatEssential information about thingsNever a comma before “that”✓ The line that runs to Helwan is the oldest.
whoPeople — essential or extraCommas only when extra✓ Students who study daily improve fastest. / ✓ Dr. Nadia Samir, who leads the project, …
whose / whereEssential or extraSame removal test✓ Siwa, where olives grow well, is far from Cairo. / ✓ The town where I grew up is small.
  • ✗ The line, that runs to Helwan, is the oldest. (“that” never takes commas on the test)
  • ✗ The Cairo Metro which opened in 1987, carries millions of passengers. (pair not opened)
  • ✓ After a person's name, a who-clause is almost always extra, because the name already tells us who: “Dr. Nadia Samir, who leads the project, …”
  • ✗ Students, who study daily, improve fastest. (now it says ALL students study daily)

Rule 6: Interrupters and participle phrases

Short phrases that interrupt a clause are also extra information and need a matching pair: however, for example, in fact, of course, according to the report, as a result. Phrases that begin with a past participle (built, held, known, written) or an -ing word follow the removal test too.

  • ✓ The final results, however, were different.
  • ✗ The final results, however were different. (pair not closed)
  • ✓ The survey, according to its authors, is the largest of its kind.
  • ✓ The stadium, completed in 2019, holds 90,000 people.
  • ✓ The houses built before 1950 need new wiring. (essential: which houses?)

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — closing a dash pair

Text: Many visitors to Egypt never travel west of the Nile Valley, but those who do are rarely disappointed. The Siwa Oasis—famous for its dates, its olives, and the ruins of an ancient oracle temple ______ lies about 50 kilometers from the Libyan border.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) temple,
  • B) temple—
  • C) temple
  • D) temple;

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: The extra information opened with a dash after “Oasis,” so a comma cannot close it (mismatched pair). Commas also appear inside the list, which is exactly why the writer chose dashes.
  • B) ✓ Correct: A dash closes the dash pair. Removal test: “The Siwa Oasis lies about 50 kilometers from the Libyan border.” ✓
  • C) ✗ Wrong: With no closing mark, the reader cannot see where the extra information ends; the sentence becomes confusing.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon needs a complete sentence before it; “The Siwa Oasis—famous for …” has no verb.

Worked example 2 — label + name

Text: Rising water temperatures are a growing concern for fish farmers along the Nile. In 2015, ______ completed a study of how Nile tilapia respond to warmer water, and her results are now used by farms across the region.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) marine biologist Hana Fouad
  • B) marine biologist, Hana Fouad
  • C) marine biologist Hana Fouad,
  • D) marine, biologist Hana Fouad

Answer: A

  • A) ✓ Correct: “Marine biologist Hana Fouad” works like a title + name: no comma. The subject goes straight to its verb “completed.”
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Trap: a comma between a label and the name that follows it. There is also no closing comma, so “Hana Fouad” would be cut off from its sentence.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Trap: the lonely comma after the name separates the subject from its verb “completed.”
  • D) ✗ Wrong: “Marine biologist” is one job label; a comma between the adjective and its noun breaks it.

Worked example 3 — which-clause

Text: Alexandria was home to the most famous library of the ancient world. Today, the city has a modern successor. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina ______ opened in 2002 near the site of the ancient library, has space for millions of books.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) , that
  • B) which
  • C) , which
  • D) that

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: “That” never takes a comma, and it introduces essential information. Here the clause is closed by a comma after “library,” so it is extra.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Trap: the pair is closed after “library” but never opened.
  • C) ✓ Correct: “Which” introduces extra information, and the opening comma matches the closing comma. Removal: “The Bibliotheca Alexandrina has space for millions of books.” ✓
  • D) ✗ Wrong: No opening comma, and “that” cannot introduce information that is closed by a comma.

Worked example 4 — unique description + name

Text: Flying was still new and dangerous in the early 1930s, and very few women anywhere had learned to fly. Egypt's first woman pilot ______ earned her license in 1933.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) , Lotfia El Nadi
  • B) Lotfia El Nadi,
  • C) , Lotfia El Nadi,
  • D) Lotfia El Nadi

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Trap: the pair is opened but never closed, so a single comma splits the name from the verb “earned.”
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Trap: a closing comma with no opening comma — again a lonely comma between subject and verb.
  • C) ✓ Correct: “Egypt's first woman pilot” can only be one person, so the name is extra and takes a matching pair of commas. Removal: “Egypt's first woman pilot earned her license in 1933.” ✓
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Trap: your ear accepts it, but the description already identifies one person, so the name is extra and must be set off.
Classic traps
  • The lonely comma: one comma between a long subject and its verb (“The researchers who studied the samples, concluded …”).
  • The mixed pair: extra information that opens with a dash and closes with a comma, or the reverse.
  • The missing partner: an opening mark with no closing mark (or a closing mark with no opening one).
  • The title trap: “author, Ahmed Khaled” — a comma between a label and a name.
  • “, that”: a comma before “that” is wrong on the test.
  • Commas around essential information: “Students, who study daily, improve fastest” changes the meaning.
  • Forgetting the context: check whether the passage talks about one thing or several before you decide.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Find the skeleton: subject → verb → object. Nothing may separate those parts except a complete matching pair.
  • Find the extra information and its two edges. Look back (or forward) for the mark that is already there and match it.
  • Use the removal test for names, appositives and which/who/that clauses.
  • Label directly before a name = no comma. Unique description + name = pair of commas.
  • When in doubt between “with commas” and “without commas,” ask: does the sentence before tell me there is only one?

Remember
  • Removal test: if the sentence still works and means the same without the words, they are extra → matching pair (, … , / — … — / ( … )).
  • Never mix the pair, and never forget the opening or closing mark.
  • Label directly before a name = no comma; unique description + name = pair of commas.
  • “which” + commas = extra; “that” + no commas = essential; after a name, “who” takes commas.
  • Read the context: one thing (extra) or one of several (essential)?
