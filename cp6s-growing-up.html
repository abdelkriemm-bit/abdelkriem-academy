Human Reproductive System and Puberty

The parts of the male and female reproductive systems and what each one does, and the body changes of puberty — explained calmly and clearly, with growth-graph practice in Checkpoint style.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 Science — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Name the parts of the human reproductive system and say simply what each part does (6Bs.03)
  • Describe the physical (body) changes that happen to boys and girls during puberty (6Bp.01)

This page covers a topic that every person goes through: growing from a child into an adult. Scientists study it in the same calm, factual way that they study the heart or the lungs. Learn the correct scientific names — Checkpoint expects them, and using the proper words is the respectful way to talk about the human body.

1. The human life cycle

Every human goes through the same stages of life. The stage between being a child and being an adult is called adolescence. During adolescence the body goes through a set of changes called puberty. Puberty prepares the body so that, as an adult, a person is able to reproduce (have children). Reproduction is one of the life processes of all living things.

2. Parts of the female reproductive system

The female reproductive system is inside the lower part of the body (the abdomen). Learn each part with its job. Checkpoint usually asks you to name a part from its job, or to match a part to its job.

PartWhat it does
ovary (two ovaries)Makes and stores egg cells. After puberty, an egg cell is usually released about once a month.
oviduct (also called the egg tube or fallopian tube)A tube that carries the egg cell from the ovary to the uterus.
uterus (also called the womb)A muscular organ where a baby grows and develops during pregnancy.
cervixA ring of muscle at the lower end (the neck) of the uterus.
vaginaA muscular tube that leads from the cervix to the outside of the body.
Memory chain (female)

Follow the path of the egg cell: ovary → oviduct → uterus. The cervix and vagina are below the uterus. If you remember where the egg cell is made and where it travels, you can place every part in order.

3. Parts of the male reproductive system

PartWhat it does
testis (plural testes, also called testicles)Make sperm cells (from puberty onwards).
scrotumA bag of skin that holds the testes outside the body, where it is slightly cooler. Sperm cells develop best a little below body temperature.
sperm ductA tube that carries sperm cells from the testes towards the penis.
glandsAdd a fluid to the sperm cells. The sperm cells and fluid together are called semen.
urethraA tube inside the penis. It carries urine out of the body, and in adults it can also carry semen — but never both at the same time.
penisThe organ that the urethra passes through.
Memory chain (male)

Follow the path of the sperm cell: testis → sperm duct → urethra (inside the penis). Glands add fluid on the way.

4. Puberty: what changes and why

Puberty is started by hormones. Hormones are chemicals made in the body that travel in the blood and tell organs to change. Puberty usually begins between about 8 and 13 years old in girls and between about 9 and 14 in boys, and it lasts a few years. Everyone is different: starting earlier or later than a friend is completely normal.

Some changes happen to both boys and girls, and some happen to only one. Checkpoint often gives a list of changes and asks you to sort them, so learn the table below.

Changes in both boys and girlsChanges in girls onlyChanges in boys only
a growth spurt — growing taller quicklybreasts developvoice becomes deeper (the voice 'breaks') because the voice box grows
hair grows under the arms and in the pubic areahips become widerhair grows on the face (beard and moustache)
skin becomes more oily; spots (acne) may appearperiods (menstruation) beginshoulders and chest become broader; muscles grow bigger
more sweat is produced, so body odour can startthe ovaries begin to release egg cellsthe testes begin to make sperm cells

Puberty can also bring emotional changes, such as mood changes or caring more about how you look. These are real and normal, but the objective asks about physical changes — so if a question says 'physical change', do not write a feeling.

Good hygiene during puberty

Because sweat and oil increase, it becomes more important to wash every day, wear clean clothes, use deodorant if you wish, and wash your face to help with spots. This links to the Diseases and Hygiene page.

5. Reading a growth graph

Checkpoint likes to join a biology topic with Thinking and Working Scientifically. A very common link here is a growth graph. The line graph below shows the height of one boy, Karim, measured on his birthday each year. (This is example data for practice.)

Age (years)8910111213141516
Height (cm)128133138143150158165169171
  1. Read the axes first: x-axis = age in years, y-axis = height in cm. Each small square on the height axis is 5 cm.
  2. To find the growth in one year, subtract: height at the later age − height at the earlier age.
  3. The steepest part of the line shows the fastest growth — this is the growth spurt of puberty.
  4. Near the end the line becomes flatter: growth slows down as the body reaches adult height.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Name the organ in a female that makes egg cells. [1 mark]

  1. Egg cells are the female sex cells.
  2. They are made and stored in the ovaries.

Answer: ovary. Common mistake: writing 'uterus' or 'womb'. The uterus is where a baby develops — it does not make eggs.

Worked example 2 — Draw a line to match each part to its job. Parts: testis, sperm duct, scrotum. Jobs: (i) carries sperm cells away from the testes; (ii) makes sperm cells; (iii) holds the testes outside the body. [2 marks]

  1. Testis → makes sperm cells (ii).
  2. Sperm duct → carries sperm cells away (i). The word 'duct' means tube.
  3. Scrotum → holds the testes outside the body (iii).

Answer: testis–(ii), sperm duct–(i), scrotum–(iii). Mark schemes often give 2 marks for all three correct and 1 mark for one correct.

Worked example 3 — Here are four changes: voice gets deeper; growth spurt; breasts develop; hair grows under the arms. Which two changes happen to both boys and girls? [1 mark]

  1. Voice getting deeper — boys only.
  2. Breasts develop — girls only.
  3. Growth spurt and underarm hair — both.

Answer: growth spurt and hair grows under the arms (both needed for the mark).

Worked example 4 — Use the table of Karim's heights. (a) How much did Karim grow between age 12 and age 13? (b) Between which two birthdays did he grow the most? [2 marks]

  1. (a) 158 − 150 = 8 cm.
  2. (b) Work out every yearly increase: 5, 5, 5, 7, 8, 7, 4, 2 cm. The largest is 8 cm.
  3. So the biggest growth was between ages 12 and 13.

Answer: (a) 8 cm (b) between 12 and 13. Common mistake: choosing the highest point on the graph (age 16). The tallest height is not the fastest growth — look for the biggest increase.

Worked example 5 — Explain why the growth line on Karim's graph becomes flatter after age 15. [1 mark]

Answer: puberty is nearly finished, so his growth is slowing down / he is reaching his adult height. (Do not write 'he stopped eating' — use the science of puberty.)

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up ovary (makes eggs) and uterus (where a baby develops).
  • Writing 'testicle' and 'testes' wrongly: one testis, two testes (testicles is also accepted).
  • Mixing up the urethra (tube through the penis) with the sperm duct (tube from the testis).
  • Giving a feeling (such as 'moody') when the question asks for a physical change.
  • Thinking that all changes happen to everyone: periods and breasts are girls only; a deeper voice and facial hair are boys only.
  • On a growth graph, choosing the tallest point instead of the biggest increase.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Name the part that does a job — one word, spelt clearly (phonetic spelling is usually credited, but learn the correct spelling).
  • Match parts to their jobs with lines — check that every line goes to only one box.
  • Sort changes into a table: boys only / girls only / both. Tick every box that applies.
  • Read a growth graph or table: work out a change in height and show the subtraction.
  • Explain a change in one short sentence, using a key word such as 'hormones' or 'puberty'.

Practice

Remember
  • Female: ovary (makes eggs) → oviduct (carries egg) → uterus (baby develops) → cervix → vagina.
  • Male: testis (makes sperm) in the scrotum → sperm duct → urethra in the penis; glands add fluid.
  • Puberty is the set of body changes in adolescence, started by hormones.
  • Both: growth spurt, body hair, oilier skin, more sweat. Girls only: breasts, wider hips, periods. Boys only: deeper voice, facial hair, broader shoulders.
  • On a growth graph, the steepest part = fastest growth (the growth spurt).
