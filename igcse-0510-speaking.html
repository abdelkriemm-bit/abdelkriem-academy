IGCSE 0510 · Speaking

The Speaking Test

The Speaking test explained: the three marked parts and their timings, the four criteria, how to develop answers, one-minute talk planning, discussion skills and full model answers.

British & IGCSE › Cambridge IGCSE English as a Second Language (0510)

This page prepares you for the Speaking test (Paper 3) step by step: what happens in each part, how the 40 marks are given, how to make every answer longer and better, how to plan a short talk in one minute, how to discuss ideas like an adult, and full model answers on original topics. Read the model answers aloud: speaking is learnt with the mouth, not only with the eyes.

Does Speaking count towards my grade?
  • 0510: Speaking is separately endorsed. It does not count towards your overall A*-G grade. Your certificate shows a separate Speaking grade from 1 (highest) to 5 (lowest).
  • 0511: Speaking counts in: it is 25% of the overall grade.
  • 0993 (9-1 version): Speaking is separately endorsed as Distinction, Merit or Pass.
  • All candidates take the Speaking test. Even when it does not change the grade, the endorsement appears on your certificate, and universities and schools can see it, so treat it seriously.

The test at a glance

The Speaking test is a one-to-one conversation with your teacher or an examiner. It lasts approximately 10-15 minutes and the whole test is recorded. Your teacher or examiner marks it, and Cambridge then checks (moderates) a sample of the marking. You may not use written notes. Only the three main parts are marked.

PartAbout how longWhat happensMarked?
Introductionabout 1 minuteThe examiner introduces the test and checks your details for the recording.No
Warm-upabout 1-2 minutesFriendly general questions to help you relax and start speaking.No
Part 1 Interviewabout 2-3 minutesThe examiner asks you a set of questions on a topic. You do not see these questions; they are in the examiner's notes.Yes
Part 2 Short talkabout 3-4 minutes, including 1 minute to prepareYou receive a Speaking Assessment card with a topic. You have one minute to think, then you talk on your own.Yes
Part 3 Discussionabout 3-4 minutesThe examiner asks discussion questions related to the Part 2 topic. You exchange ideas and opinions.Yes

How the 40 marks are given

You are marked on four criteria, 10 marks each. For each criterion the examiner decides which level fits your performance best (9-10, 7-8, 5-6, 3-4, 1-2, or 0) and then gives a mark. The descriptions below are in my own words, to show what each criterion is looking for.

CriterionMarksWhat the examiner listens forHow you show it
Grammar10A range of structures, simple and complex, used with control. Mistakes should be few and should not stop the listener understanding.Mix tenses naturally (I used to… / I've been… / I'm hoping to…). Use conditionals (If I had more time, I'd…), relative clauses (…which was…), comparatives, modals (might, should, could).
Vocabulary10A wide range of words and expressions, including less common ones, used accurately and appropriately for the topic.Use precise words (exhausting, not very tired), topic words (independence, routine), collocations (save money, make a difference) and natural phrases (to be honest, on the other hand).
Development10How well you develop your ideas, give full and relevant answers and keep the conversation going without long silences or help.Answer, then give reasons and examples. Link ideas, respond to the examiner's questions naturally and continue confidently in Parts 2 and 3.
Pronunciation10Clear individual sounds, correct word and sentence stress, and natural intonation (the music of your voice), so you are easy to understand.Speak at a steady speed, stress the important words, pause at the end of ideas, let your voice rise and fall.
Development is where most students lose marks

Most students know enough grammar and words. They lose marks because their answers are too short. A two-word answer gives the examiner nothing to mark. Every answer you give is a chance to show your grammar, vocabulary and pronunciation, so give the examiner enough language to reward.

How to develop your answers: the A-R-E-E method

Use this simple formula for every answer in Part 1 and Part 3. It turns a one-line answer into a full-mark one in about 20-40 seconds.

LetterMeaningExample (question: Do you like reading?)
AAnswer the question directlyYes, I do, especially adventure novels.
RGive a ReasonBecause they help me escape from school stress for a while.
EGive an Example (often from your own life)Last month I read a story about a boy who sailed around the world alone, and I couldn't put it down.
EAdd an Extra: a contrast, a feeling, the past or the futureAlthough I read less during exams, I always take a book with me on holiday.
Weak answerDeveloped answer
*Do you like your school?* Yes. It is good.Yes, I really do. The teachers are supportive, and there's a great atmosphere. For example, when I was struggling with chemistry last year, my teacher stayed after class to help me. If I could change one thing, though, I'd make the school day start a bit later.
*How do you travel to school?* By bus.Usually by the school bus. It takes about forty minutes, which is quite long, but I don't mind because I chat with my friends or listen to podcasts on the way. When I'm older, I'm hoping to cycle, as there's a new bike path near my house.
Do not over-do it
  • Developed does not mean endless. In Part 1, about 20-40 seconds per answer is enough.
  • Stay relevant: if the question is about free time, do not start talking about your exams.
  • Never memorise a whole speech and recite it. It sounds unnatural, and it usually does not answer the exact question.

Part 1: the interview

In Part 1 the examiner asks you questions on a topic, one by one. The questions are usually about your own life and experience, and they often move from simple (what, where) to more thoughtful (has it changed? do you think…?). You do not see the questions, so listen carefully to the tense and the question word.

  • Listen to the tense. 'What did you do last weekend?' needs the past, while 'What would you like to do?' needs *would* or *I'd love to*.
  • Answer the exact question first, then develop with A-R-E-E.
  • Use real details: names of places, times, people (a friend, my uncle). Details make answers natural and easy to develop.
  • If you do not understand, ask politely: *Sorry, could you repeat the question, please?* or *Do you mean…?* This is natural and not a problem.
  • Do not answer with a question, and do not wait for the examiner to help you continue.
Model interview (Topic 1: Where you live and your free time)296 words · Target: Grammar 10 + Vocabulary 10 + Development 10 (Pronunciation is judged on how you say it)
Task: Part 1 Interview. The examiner asks five questions about where you live and how you spend your free time.

Q1. Where do you live, and what is your neighbourhood like? I live in a fairly new city on the eastern edge of Cairo. What I like most is that it's quieter and greener than the city centre: there are parks between the buildings, and you can actually hear birds in the morning. The only downside is that it's quite far from everything, so we spend a lot of time in the car.

Q2. What do you usually do in your free time? It depends on the day. During the week I don't have much free time because of school and homework, so I usually just play football with my neighbours for an hour before dinner. At weekends, though, I try to do something different. Last Friday, for example, my cousins and I cycled around the lake in the park near us, which was great fun.

Q3. Has the way you spend your free time changed since you were younger? Yes, quite a lot. When I was about eight, I spent hours playing video games, and my parents were always telling me to switch them off. Now I find them a bit repetitive, to be honest. I'd rather be outside or meet my friends in person, maybe because I spend so much time sitting at a desk studying.

Q4. Is there a new hobby you would like to try? I'd really love to learn to swim properly. I can swim a little, but not well enough to feel safe in the sea. Every summer we go to Alexandria, and I always stay close to the beach while my brother swims much further out. So this year I'm hoping to join a course at our sports club.

Q5. Do you think young people today have enough free time? Honestly, I don't think we do. Between school, private lessons and homework, a lot of my friends finish studying at ten at night. I understand that exams are important, but if students never have time to relax, they get tired and actually learn less. I think a good balance would help everyone, including our teachers.

Why this gets the marks
  • Grammar: a range of tenses used accurately: present simple (I live), past simple (cycled), used to / was always telling, past compared with present ('When I was about eight… Now I find…'), future plans (I'm hoping to join), conditionals and preferences (I'd rather, I'd really love to).
  • Complex structures: relative clauses ('which was great fun'), reason clauses ('because I spend so much time…'), 'if students never have time to relax, they get tired' (conditional), 'not well enough to feel safe'.
  • Vocabulary: precise and natural ('on the eastern edge', 'the only downside', 'a bit repetitive', 'stay close to the beach', 'a good balance') rather than only 'good' and 'nice'.
  • Development: every answer follows Answer → Reason → Example → Extra (Q2: depends on the day → reason (school) → example (last Friday) → contrast (at weekends, though)).
  • Relevance: each answer responds to the exact question and tense. Q5 gives a clear opinion and a balanced reason ('I understand that… but…').
  • Length: 20-40 seconds of speech per answer, with no memorised speech.

  • استخدم A-R-E-E: جاوب السؤال، وبعدين قول السبب، وبعدين مثال من حياتك، وبعدين معلومة زيادة (زمان / المستقبل / عكس الفكرة).

Part 2: the short talk

In Part 2 you receive a Speaking Assessment card with a topic. You have one minute to prepare, and then you talk on your own. The whole part, including the preparation, lasts about 3-4 minutes. Remember: you may not write notes, so you must plan in your head. A clear structure is what makes this possible.

How to plan in one minute (without notes)

TimeWhat to do in your head
0-10 secondsRead the card carefully. What exactly is the topic? Underline it mentally: one idea, not a general area.
10-25 secondsDecide your main opinion or angle in one sentence (for example: 'Cooking is the most useful skill because of health and independence').
25-45 secondsChoose two or three points and one example for each, ideally from your own life. Give each point a one-word label to remember it (Health · Independence · Time).
45-60 secondsPrepare your first sentence and your last sentence. If you know how you will start and finish, the middle will follow.
Part of the talkWhat it doesUseful language
Introduction (about 15 seconds)Says the topic and your main idea, and tells the listener your pointsI'm going to talk about… · For me, the answer is… for two main reasons: … and …
Point 1 + exampleFirst reason, explained, with a real exampleFirst of all, … · For instance, … · A friend of mine…
Point 2 + exampleSecond reason, explained, with an exampleMy second point is… · Another reason is that… · Last year, I…
(Optional) Point 3 or counter-argumentA third reason, or another view and your answer to itSome people say… and that's partly true. However, …
Conclusion (about 15 seconds)Repeats the main idea in new words and can add a recommendation or a look to the futureSo, to sum up, … · That's why I believe… · In the future, I'd like to…
Model short talk (Topic 2: A skill everyone should learn)265 words · Target: Grammar 10 + Vocabulary 10 + Development 10 (Pronunciation is judged on how you say it)
Task: Speaking Assessment card (original): 'A skill that everyone should learn'. You could talk about: what the skill is · why it is useful · how you learnt it or would like to learn it · whether schools should teach it. One minute to prepare, then talk on your own (about 2-3 minutes).

I'm going to talk about a skill that I believe everyone should learn, and for me that skill is cooking. It might sound simple, but I think it's one of the most useful things a young person can learn, for two main reasons: health and independence.

First of all, health. If you can't cook, you depend on fast food and delivery apps, and most of that food is full of salt, sugar and oil. A friend of mine used to order burgers almost every day when his parents were travelling. When he learnt to make a few easy meals, like pasta with vegetables or grilled chicken, he told me he felt much more energetic and he even saved money.

My second point is independence. Sooner or later, most of us will leave home, maybe to go to university or to work in another city. Cooking is part of looking after yourself. I started helping my mother in the kitchen last year, and at first I was hopeless: I burnt the rice twice! But now I can cook a full meal for the family, and honestly, I feel proud when everyone enjoys it.

Some people say cooking takes too much time, and that's partly true. However, a simple meal often takes less than thirty minutes, which is about the same as waiting for a delivery.

So, to sum up, I believe cooking should be taught to every teenager, because it keeps us healthy, it saves money and it prepares us for adult life. In fact, I think schools could even offer short cooking classes. Thank you.

Why this gets the marks
  • Structure: a clear introduction that announces two reasons (health and independence), one paragraph for each with a personal example, a counter-argument, and a conclusion that sums up and adds a recommendation.
  • Development: each point is explained and supported by a concrete example (the friend who ordered burgers, burning the rice); the counter-argument ('that's partly true. However…') shows mature thinking.
  • Grammar: used to (used to order), past simple and past continuous (were travelling), can for present ability ('now I can cook'), future (will leave), conditional ('If you can't cook, you depend on…'), relative clause (which is about the same as…), modals (could, should).
  • Vocabulary: less common, topic-specific words and collocations: independence, depend on, energetic, sooner or later, looking after yourself, hopeless, adult life, offer short cooking classes.
  • Cohesion: clear signposting (First of all, My second point is, However, So, to sum up, In fact) helps the listener follow without notes.
  • Length: about 265 words, which is roughly two to two and a half minutes at a calm, natural speed. Part 2 lasts about 3-4 minutes including the minute of preparation, so a talk of about 2-3 minutes fits.
If you run out of ideas in the middle
  • Go back to your labels: 'Another thing about health is…'
  • Give a second example: 'Another example is my cousin, who…'
  • Add the opposite view: 'Of course, not everyone agrees. Some people think…'
  • Talk about the future: 'In ten years' time, I think…'

Part 3: the discussion

In Part 3 the examiner asks questions related to the Part 2 topic, but wider and more general: about society, young people, the future, advantages and disadvantages. There is no single correct answer. The examiner wants to hear you give and support opinions, compare, agree or disagree politely and speculate (talk about what might happen).

SkillWhat it meansExample language
Give an opinion with a reasonSay what you think and whyI'd say… because… · In my view, … The main reason is…
Agree or disagree politelyReact to an idea in the question without being rudeI partly agree. · That's true to some extent, but… · I see the point, although…
CompareLook at two sides, two times or two groupsCompared with my parents' generation, … · Whereas…, …
SpeculateTalk about possibilities and the futureIt's hard to say for certain, but I imagine… · It might / could… · I suspect that…
Give a balanced viewShow both sides, then decideOn the one hand… on the other hand… · Overall, I'd say…
Gain thinking time naturallyAvoid silence while you thinkThat's a difficult question. · Let me think about that for a second. · I've never really thought about it, but…
Model discussion (follow-up to Topic 2)234 words · Target: Grammar 10 + Vocabulary 10 + Development 10 (Pronunciation is judged on how you say it)
Task: Part 3 Discussion. The examiner asks four questions related to the short talk on life skills.

Examiner: Should schools teach practical skills like cooking, or should they focus on academic subjects? I think they should do both, but I'd say the balance at the moment is wrong. Academic subjects are essential, of course, because we need them for university. However, a lot of students finish school without knowing how to cook a meal, manage money or fill in a form. Even one lesson a week on practical skills could make a huge difference.

Examiner: Some people say young people today are less independent than their parents were. Do you agree? I partly agree. My father says that at my age he was already travelling alone by train to visit his grandparents, while my parents still drive me almost everywhere. On the other hand, we're independent in different ways. For example, we can find information, learn new skills online and organise things with our phones much faster than our parents could.

Examiner: Who do you think is more responsible for teaching life skills, parents or schools? That's a difficult question. I'd say parents have the bigger role, because life skills are learnt by doing them every day at home, not by reading about them. But not every parent has the time, especially if both of them work long hours, so schools should support families rather than replace them.

Examiner: How might the skills young people need change in the future? It's hard to say for certain, but I imagine technology will do more of the routine work for us, so skills like cooking or repairing things might become less necessary. Then again, I suspect that skills like communicating, working in a team and dealing with problems will matter even more, because machines probably can't do those as well as people.

Why this gets the marks
  • Discussion skills: partial agreement ('I partly agree'), balance ('On the other hand'), thinking time ('That's a difficult question'), and speculation ('It's hard to say for certain, but I imagine…', 'I suspect that…', 'might become').
  • Development: every answer gives an opinion, a reason and an example (the father travelling alone by train, parents working long hours).
  • Grammar: comparisons (than… could), past continuous and past simple (was already travelling), modals for possibility (might, probably can't), future (will do), passive (are learnt), 'rather than'.
  • Vocabulary: abstract and topic vocabulary suited to a discussion: academic subjects, essential, manage money, independent, routine work, dealing with problems, support families rather than replace them.
  • Relevance: each answer responds to the exact question and widens the topic from 'me' to 'society' as Part 3 requires.

Topic 3 for practice: helping in your community

Now try a full Part 2 and Part 3 on your own. Give yourself one minute to plan in your head (no writing), then speak for about 2-3 minutes. Record yourself on your phone (audio only) and listen back. Then look at the plan and the sample discussion answers.

Speaking Assessment card (original practice)
  • Topic: Helping other people in your community
  • You could talk about: ways young people can help others · something helpful you have done or would like to do · how helping others benefits the helper · whether helping should be part of school life

  1. What could a good one-minute plan for this talk look like?

    Show answer
    Main idea: Helping others is good for the community and for us. Point 1 (ways to help): teaching younger children, collecting clothes, helping elderly neighbours. Example: I helped my neighbour carry his shopping every week. Point 2 (benefits for the helper): you feel useful, gain confidence and learn teamwork. Example: organising a clothes collection at school taught me to speak to adults. Counter-view: students are busy, but even one hour a month helps. Conclusion: schools should give students community hours.

  2. Discussion: Why do you think some young people don't help in their community?

    Show answer
    Model answer: I think the main reason is time. Most students have a very full timetable, with school, lessons and homework, so volunteering feels like one more task. Another reason might be that nobody has shown them how to start. If schools or youth clubs organised simple projects, I suspect many more students would join in.

  3. Discussion: Should helping others be compulsory at school?

    Show answer
    Model answer: I have mixed feelings about that. On the one hand, making it compulsory would make sure every student has the experience, and many would discover that they enjoy it. On the other hand, if people are forced to help, they might do it without any real interest. Overall, I'd say a small number of required hours, with a free choice of activity, is a good compromise.

  4. Discussion: How might technology change the way people help each other in the future?

    Show answer
    Model answer: It's hard to predict, but I imagine apps will make it much easier to connect people who need help with people who can give it, for example, a student who could teach English online to children in villages. However, I'd hate to see technology replace face-to-face help completely, because a visit or a conversation means far more to a lonely elderly person than a message.

Pronunciation: what to work on

Pronunciation is not about sounding British or American. It is about being easy to understand. These are the areas where I most often hear problems from students in Egypt.

AreaThe problemWhat to practise
/p/ and /b/'People' sounds like 'beoble'.Hold a paper in front of your mouth: it should move for /p/ (park, happy, people) and not for /b/.
/v/ and /f/'Very' sounds like 'fery'.For /v/, top teeth touch the lower lip and your throat vibrates: very, view, travel, available.
'th' sounds'Think' becomes 'sink' or 'tink', and 'this' becomes 'zis'.Put the tip of your tongue between your teeth: think, three, healthy, this, other, with.
Extra vowels'Street' becomes 'istreet', and 'spring' becomes 'ispring'.Start directly with the consonants: s-treet, s-pring, s-chool, s-tudents.
-ed endings'Walked' said as 'walk-ed'.Three sounds: /t/ (walked, helped), /d/ (played, cleaned), /id/ only after t or d (wanted, needed).
Word stress'DEvelopment' or 'ENvironment', with the strong syllable in the wrong place.Learn stress with new words: deVELopment, enVIronment, imPORtant, PHOtograph, phoTOgraphy.
Sentence stress and intonationEvery word said with the same force, so the speech sounds flat.Stress the key words: 'I REALLY think SCHOOLS should do MORE.' Let your voice fall at the end of a statement and rise in yes/no questions.
SpeedSpeaking too fast because of nerves.Pause after each idea. Calm speed with clear pauses always sounds more fluent than rushing.

Common mistakes

  • Very short answers in Part 1 ('Yes.', 'Football.'). Always develop.
  • Reciting a memorised speech. It sounds unnatural and rarely answers the exact question.
  • Talking off topic in Part 2 because you planned a different talk.
  • Using only simple words (good, bad, nice, very). Upgrade: helpful, harmful, impressive, extremely.
  • Wrong tense after the question: 'What did you do last summer?' 'I go to Alexandria.' → 'I went'.
  • Third person -s forgotten: 'My brother play football' → 'plays'.
  • Long silences. Use a thinking phrase instead: 'Let me think about that for a second.'
  • Agreeing with everything in Part 3. A reasoned, polite disagreement shows more language.
  • Speaking too quietly or too fast because of nerves. The recording must be clear.

WeekFocusDaily task (10-15 minutes)
1Part 1 and A-R-E-EAnswer three personal questions aloud each day (home, school, free time, food, travel). Record them and check: did each answer have a reason and an example?
2Part 2 short talksOne card a day: one minute of silent planning, then talk for about 2-3 minutes. Use the five-part structure.

Practice questions

  1. How long is the Speaking test, and which parts are marked?

    Show answer
    Approximately 10-15 minutes. The introduction and warm-up are not marked. Part 1 (Interview), Part 2 (Short talk) and Part 3 (Discussion) are marked.

  2. What are the four criteria and how many marks is each worth?

    Show answer
    Grammar, Vocabulary, Development and Pronunciation, 10 marks each, 40 in total.

  3. Can I write notes during the one-minute preparation for the short talk?

    Show answer
    No. Written notes are not allowed, so you plan in your head: main idea, two or three points with examples, first and last sentence.

  4. Does Speaking count towards the overall 0510 grade?

    Show answer
    No. For 0510 it is separately endorsed (grades 1 to 5 on the certificate). For 0511 it counts as 25% of the overall grade.

  5. Improve this Part 1 answer: 'Do you prefer the city or the countryside?' – 'The city.'

    Show answer
    Possible answer: I'd say the city, mainly because everything is close by: my school, my friends and the sports club. For example, I can walk to the club in ten minutes. Having said that, I love visiting my grandparents in the countryside, because it's so peaceful there, and I might even like to live in a quieter place when I'm older.

  6. Give a polite way to disagree with this statement: 'Young people spend too much time on their phones.'

    Show answer
    For example: 'I see why people say that, and for some teenagers it's true. However, I'd say a lot of that time is useful. I use my phone to study, to listen to English podcasts and to stay in touch with my family, so it's not all wasted time.'

  7. Speculate: 'What will schools be like in twenty years?'

    Show answer
    For example: 'It's hard to say for certain, but I imagine students might spend part of the week learning from home, with lessons online. I suspect teachers will still be essential, though, because students need someone to guide and encourage them, not just a screen.'
Speaking checklist
  • I know the parts: introduction and warm-up (not marked), then Interview, Short talk (with 1 minute to prepare) and Discussion.
  • I develop every answer: Answer, Reason, Example, Extra.
  • I can plan a short talk in one minute without notes: introduction, two or three points with examples, conclusion.
  • I can agree and disagree politely, give reasons and speculate about the future.
  • I use a range of tenses and some less common vocabulary.
  • I speak clearly, at a calm speed, with pauses between ideas.
