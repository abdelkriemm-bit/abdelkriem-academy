Adjectives vs Adverbs

Find the word being described, then choose: adjectives for nouns and after linking verbs, adverbs for verbs, adjectives, adverbs and participles — plus good/well, real/really and the tricky -ly words.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

"She sings beautiful." "The bridge was real old." "I feel badly." All three sound normal in everyday speech, and all three are errors the ACT likes to test. This lesson teaches one simple question that solves almost every adjective–adverb item — what word is being described? — and then the special cases the test uses to catch careful students: linking verbs, good/well, real/really, adverbs before participles, -ly words that are really adjectives, and adverbs that do not end in -ly at all.

What this question type tests

Adjective–adverb questions are usage questions in Conventions of Standard English, the reporting category that makes up 38–43% of the 40 scored ACT English questions (together with punctuation and sentence structure). They look like every other ACT English item: a word or phrase is underlined in the essay and you choose among four versions, usually with NO CHANGE first. There is often no question stem at all.

You can recognise the type with a vertical scan of the choices: they show the same word in different forms — careful / carefully, quick / quickly, good / well, real / really, surprising / surprisingly. As soon as you see that pattern, stop reading for meaning and ask the grammar question below.

Step 1: Find the word being described

Every describing word is attached to another word. Find that word first; its part of speech decides the answer.

The underlined word describes…Use✓ Example
a noun (person, place, thing, idea)adjectivea careful engineer · a quick reply
a pronoun after a linking verbadjectiveShe seems calm. It became dark.
a verb (an action)adverbShe drove carefully. He replied quickly.
an adjectiveadverban extremely quick reply · surprisingly cheap
another adverbadverbShe spoke remarkably clearly.
a participle used as an adjective (built, designed, known)adverba newly built school · a carefully designed plan
the whole sentenceadverbFortunately, nobody was hurt.

Most adverbs are adjective + -ly (slow → slowly, careful → carefully, easy → easily, gentle → gently, basic → basically). But the ending alone is not proof, as you will see in Step 5.

✗ Wrong✓ RightWhy
The guide spoke very clear.The guide spoke very clearly.describes the verb *spoke*
The test was incredible difficult.The test was incredibly difficult.describes the adjective *difficult*
The new model runs quiet and efficient.The new model runs quietly and efficiently.both describe the verb *runs*
Luckily for us, the shop was open late.(correct as written)*Luckily* describes the whole sentence
They made a quickly decision.They made a quick decision.describes the noun *decision*
The plan was explained in a surprising simple way.…in a surprisingly simple way.*surprisingly* describes the adjective *simple*

Step 2: Linking verbs take adjectives

This is the rule the ACT tests most often. A linking verb does not show an action; it connects the subject to a word that describes the subject. So the word after it is an adjective, not an adverb.

  • Common linking verbs: be (is, are, was, were, been), seem, appear, become, remain, stay, prove, grow, turn, and the five sense verbs look, feel, sound, taste, smell.
  • The "is" test: replace the verb with *is/was*. If the sentence still makes sense, the verb is linking → use an adjective. "The soup tasted ___" → "The soup was salty" ✓ → salty.
  • ✓ The room grew quiet. ✗ The room grew quietly. (The room became quiet.)
  • ✓ Her plan sounds reasonable. ✗ Her plan sounds reasonably.
  • ✓ The milk turned sour. ✓ The results proved accurate. ✓ The bread smells fresh.
  • ✓ I feel bad about the mistake. ✗ I feel badly about the mistake. (*Bad* describes how *I* feel. "Feel badly" would describe how your fingers touch things.)

The same verb can be linking or action. Ask whether the verb shows something the subject *does* or simply describes the subject's *state*.

Linking (describes the subject) → adjectiveAction (the subject does something) → adverb
The guard looked angry. (He was angry.)The guard looked angrily at the crowd. (How did he look at them?)
The flowers smell sweet.The dog smelled the bag eagerly.
The music sounded loud.She sounded the alarm loudly.
The coffee tastes bitter.He tasted the sauce carefully.
The sky grew dark.The farmer grew vegetables successfully.
The fabric feels soft.The doctor felt the patient's arm gently.

Tip: if the verb is followed by an object or by a preposition such as *at* (looked at, smelled the bag), it is usually an action verb → adverb.

Step 3: good / well, bad / badly, real / really

WordPart of speech✓ Right✗ Wrong
goodadjective onlya good job · The plan looks good.She did good on the test.
welladverb (how something is done)She did well on the test. He sings well.He sings good.
welladjective meaning *healthy*After a week in bed, I finally feel well.—
badadjectiveThe news is bad. I feel bad.The team played bad.
badlyadverbThe team played badly.I feel badly about it (meaning sorry).
realadjective (= genuine)a real diamond · a real problemThe trip was real long.
reallyadverb (= very, truly)The trip was really long.It was real good.
sure / surelyadjective / adverbI am sure. It will surely rain.It sure is hot. (casual)

Notice that *I feel good* and *I feel well* can both be correct: *good* describes your mood, *well* means you are not ill. The ACT will make the meaning clear from the passage. What it will never accept is did good, played good or real good.

Step 4: Adverbs before participles and adjectives

A participle (built, designed, known, written, painted) can act like an adjective before a noun. The word that describes the participle must be an adverb. This is a favourite hard item because the adjective version "sounds" like a list of two adjectives.

  • ✗ a beautiful decorated room → ✓ a beautifully decorated room (it was decorated beautifully)
  • ✗ a new built museum → ✓ a newly built museum
  • ✗ a careful written report → ✓ a carefully written report
  • ✗ a wide read author → ✓ a widely read author
  • ✓ a beautiful, old house — here both words describe the noun *house*, so two adjectives are correct.
  • Test: put *and* between the two words. "a beautiful and decorated room" means something different → you need the adverb. "a beautiful and old house" works → two adjectives are fine.

Step 5: -ly adjectives and adverbs without -ly

Some words end in -ly but are adjectives: they describe nouns and cannot simply describe a verb. You cannot add another -ly to most of them, so you rewrite with in a … way / manner.

-ly adjective✓ Right✗ Wrong
friendlya friendly neighbour · She smiled in a friendly way.She smiled friendly.
lovelya lovely garden · The garden looks lovely.She sang lovely.
lonely, lively, likelya lonely road · a lively debate · a likely result—
costly, elderly, timelya costly mistake · an elderly man · a timely warningHe answered timely. → He answered in a timely manner.
silly, ugly, deadly, cowardlya silly idea · a deadly stormHe behaved cowardly. → He behaved in a cowardly way.
daily, weekly, early (both!)a daily walk / I walk daily · an early train / We left early—

The opposite group: some adverbs have no -ly form, or the -ly form means something different. The test uses these to trap students who add -ly to everything.

Adverb = adjective✓ RightCareful: the -ly word means something else
fastShe runs fast.*fastly* does not exist
hardThey worked hard.*hardly* = almost not (They hardly worked = they did very little)
lateThe bus arrived late.*lately* = recently
highThe kite flew high.*highly* = very much (highly respected)
nearStay near.*nearly* = almost
straight, early, longGo straight home. We left early.—

Worked examples

Each example is a sentence from an ACT-style essay. The underlined portion is shown in bold.

Worked example 1 — adverb describing an adjective. "The new metro line has made the trip across the city remarkable short."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) remarkable shortly
  • C) remarkably shortly
  • D) remarkably short

Answer: D.

  • D is correct. *Short* describes the noun *trip* (after *made the trip*), so it stays an adjective. *Remarkably* describes the adjective *short* — how short? — so it must be an adverb.
  • A uses the adjective *remarkable* to describe another adjective. Trap: two adjectives side by side often sound acceptable in speech.
  • B reverses the forms: *shortly* means *soon*, and *remarkable* still cannot describe it.
  • C keeps *shortly*, which changes the meaning to "soon" and leaves the trip with no describing word. Trap: adding -ly to every word.

Worked example 2 — linking verb. "After the long argument, the meeting room grew silently, and nobody wanted to speak first."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) more silently
  • C) in silence way
  • D) silent

Answer: D.

  • D is correct. *Grew* here means *became*: the room became silent. The "is" test works (the room was silent), so *grew* is a linking verb and needs the adjective silent.
  • A treats *grew* as an action, as if the room were growing in a silent manner. Trap: the linking-verb trap.
  • B is still an adverb and adds a comparison with nothing to compare.
  • C is not standard English (the idiom would be *in a silent way*, and even that would describe an action, not a state).

Worked example 3 — adverb before a participle. "Visitors to the old house admire its careful restored wooden balconies."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) carefully restored
  • C) carefully restoring
  • D) care-restored

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. The balconies were restored carefully, so *carefully* describes the participle *restored*. An adverb is needed.
  • A uses the adjective *careful*, but the balconies are not careful; the restoring was. Use the *and* test: "careful and restored balconies" makes no sense.
  • C changes the participle to *restoring*, which would mean the balconies are doing the restoring.
  • D is an invented compound.

Worked example 4 — -ly adjective. "The shop owner greeted every customer friendly, even on the busiest days of the festival."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) friendlily
  • C) in a friendly manner
  • D) friendlier

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. The word describes how he *greeted* (an action), so we need an adverb idea. *Friendly* is an adjective, and it has no normal -ly adverb, so standard English uses in a friendly manner (or *in a friendly way*).
  • A uses the adjective to describe a verb. Trap: the -ly ending makes *friendly* look like an adverb.
  • B is an extremely rare, awkward form that the ACT does not accept as standard.
  • D is a comparative with nothing to compare (friendlier than whom?).

Worked example 5 — hard vs hardly. "The volunteers worked hardly all weekend, and by Sunday evening the whole park had been cleaned."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) hard
  • C) harder
  • D) more hardly

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. *Hard* is both an adjective and an adverb: they worked hard = with great effort. The second clause (the whole park was cleaned) confirms the meaning.
  • A means *almost not at all*, which contradicts the result. Trap: the -ly form exists but has a different meaning.
  • C is a comparative with nothing to compare.
  • D combines two errors: *more* + the wrong word.
Classic traps
  • Adjective before an adjective: *incredible fast, surprising large, real good* → the first word must be an adverb (incredibly, surprisingly, really).
  • The linking-verb trap: adverbs after *seem, become, grow, feel, look, sound, taste, smell* → use adjectives (grew quiet, feel bad, sounds reasonable).
  • Action or state? The same verb (look, smell, taste, feel) can be either; check whether the subject is doing something to an object.
  • good for well: did good, played good, sings good → well.
  • Adjective before a participle: *a beautiful decorated hall, a new built bridge* → beautifully, newly.
  • The -ly illusion: friendly, lovely, costly and timely are adjectives; you cannot say *spoke friendly*.
  • The meaning-change -ly: hardly ≠ hard, lately ≠ late, highly ≠ high, nearly ≠ near.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Vertical scan shows careful/carefully, good/well, real/really? Ask: *what word is being described?*
  • Noun → adjective. Verb, adjective, adverb or participle → adverb.
  • Verb before the blank is *is, seem, become, look, feel, sound, taste, smell*? Try *is*. If it works, choose the adjective.
  • Two describing words before a noun? Use the *and* test.
  • Eliminate any choice that adds a comparison (more, -er) when nothing is being compared.
  • If the -ly word changes the meaning (hardly, lately), check the sentence's logic.

Practice

Two short ACT-style essays with 5 questions each. The numbers in brackets match the question numbers. Every question tests adjectives and adverbs.

Remember
  • Find the word being described: noun → adjective; verb, adjective, adverb or participle → adverb.
  • Linking verbs (be, seem, become, grow, look, feel, sound, taste, smell) take adjectives: feel bad, look good, grew quiet.
  • good = adjective; well = adverb (or *healthy*); real = genuine; really = very.
  • friendly, lovely, costly are adjectives → *in a friendly way*.
  • hard, fast, late, high are adverbs too — and hardly, lately, highly mean something else.
