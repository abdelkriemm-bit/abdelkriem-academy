The Enhanced ACT: Complete Overview

How the enhanced ACT is built: sections, timing, the composite score, the English and Reading formats, and a 6-week study plan.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

This page explains the whole enhanced ACT in one place: the sections, the timing, how the composite score is made, what an ACT English passage and an ACT Reading passage look like, and how to plan your preparation. Read it once carefully before you start the lessons, then come back to it the week before your test.

What the enhanced ACT is

The ACT is a college admission test that many American Diploma students take. Since September 2025 every ACT is the enhanced ACT: the same design is used for all tests, online and paper, in the United States and internationally. If you find an old book or video that describes a longer test with more questions, it is describing the old ACT. Use the numbers on this page.

The ACT is not adaptive. Everyone taking the same test form gets the same questions, and the questions do not get easier or harder because of your answers. (This is a big difference from the Digital SAT, where your Module 1 result decides the level of Module 2.) On the ACT a hard question is simply a hard question, and every question in a section is worth the same.

The three core sections (English, Math and Reading) take 125 minutes of testing time in total. This course covers the two verbal core sections, English and Reading.

Unscored questions

Each core section contains some questions that do not count towards your score (10 in English, 4 in Math, 9 in Reading). You cannot rely on knowing which ones they are, so treat every single question as if it counts.

SectionTime per question (all questions)What that means
English35 min ÷ 50 ≈ 42 secondsFast. Most grammar questions should take 20–30 seconds, which saves time for the 'whole passage' questions.
Reading40 min ÷ 36 ≈ 67 secondsThis includes the time to read long passages, so you cannot read slowly and re-read everything.

How the composite score works

Your composite score is on a scale of 1–36. It is the average of your English, Math and Reading scores. Science and Writing are reported separately: they appear on your score report but they do not raise or lower the composite.

  • Because the composite is an average, a strong English score can balance a weaker Math score, and the other way round.
  • English is usually the section where students improve fastest, because the grammar and punctuation rules are limited and they repeat on every test.
  • We do not give target scores or university requirements on this site. Requirements differ from university to university and change from year to year, so check each university's own admissions page.

The optional sections: Science and Writing

Science (40 questions, 40 minutes) and Writing (one essay, 40 minutes) are optional. You choose whether to take them when you register. Neither one changes your composite. Whether you need them depends only on the universities you apply to, so find out early from each university's admissions page and decide before you register.

ACT English: what the section looks like

ACT English is not a list of separate sentences. You read essays and fix them while you read. The scored part of the section is built like this:

Scored passagesLengthQuestions each
3 long essaysabout 340 words10
2 short essaysabout 185 words5
Total5 essays40 scored questions

The essays are of three kinds: informational (explains a topic, a person, a discovery), argumentative (gives an opinion and supports it) and narrative (tells a story, often in the first person). You do not need any background knowledge; everything you need is in the passage.

Underlined portions and NO CHANGE

Most questions refer to an underlined portion of the passage, marked with a question number. The question shows four versions of that portion. The first choice is very often NO CHANGE, which means "keep the original". Many questions have no written question at all: you simply see the four versions and choose the best one. Here is the shape (original example; the underlined portion is shown in bold):

Passage: "The first lighthouse on this coast was built of wood, it burned down [12] within ten years."

Choices: A) NO CHANGE B) wood, and it burned down C) wood it burned down D) wood, burning it down

Answer: B. The original joins two complete sentences with only a comma (a comma splice). B adds "and", which makes the join correct. C has no punctuation at all (a run-on). D changes the meaning: it now says the wood burned the lighthouse down. Notice that NO CHANGE is a real choice: on some questions the original is already the best version, and then A is the answer.

Questions about the passage as a whole

Some questions have no underlined portion. They ask about a sentence, a paragraph or the whole essay. The stem then tells you exactly what to decide, for example:

  • whether the writer should add a new sentence at a certain point, or delete a sentence, and why;
  • where a sentence or a paragraph should be placed so that the essay is logical;
  • which choice gives the most effective introduction, conclusion or transition;
  • whether the essay achieves a goal the writer had in mind ("Suppose the writer's goal had been to…").

For these questions you must understand what the paragraph and the essay are about, which is why you should read the passage as you answer, not jump from underline to underline.

ACT Reading: what the section looks like

The Reading section has 36 questions in 40 minutes. The scored part is built from three passages with 9 questions each:

Scored passagesLengthQuestions
Two passagesabout 750 words each9 questions each
One passageabout 650 words9 questions

In total there is one literary narrative (a story or a memoir) and two informational passages from the humanities, social science or natural science. On each form, one of the sets is special: it is either a pair of passages on the same topic (with questions that compare them) or a passage that comes with charts or graphs (with questions that connect the text and the data).

Reading categoryShare of scored questionsWhat it asks
Key Ideas and Details44–52%Main idea, details, inferences, summaries, cause and effect, sequence
Craft and Structure26–33%Meaning of words in context, the author's purpose and point of view, how a paragraph or sentence functions
Integration of Knowledge and Ideas19–26%Comparing paired passages, using charts or graphs, judging claims and evidence

The Reading part of this course gives you a method for 650–750-word passages and a full lesson on each of the three categories.

Rules that change how you take the test

  • No penalty for wrong answers. A blank and a wrong answer both score zero, so answer every question. Never leave a bubble or a screen empty.
  • Not adaptive. Every question in a section is worth the same, so do not get stuck on a hard one. Answer, mark it if you can, and move on.
  • Online or paper. The enhanced design is the same in both. Practise with a clock either way.
  • The official information is on act.org. If anything there is different from this page, follow act.org.

A 6-week study plan for ACT English and Reading

Plan for about 45–60 minutes a day, 5 days a week. If you have more time, repeat the weeks where your quiz scores were lowest. Keep a mistake notebook: for every wrong answer write the rule or the trap in one line.

WeekPagesGoal
1This overview; How to Attack ACT English; ACT PunctuationKnow the format. Learn every comma, semicolon, colon, dash and apostrophe rule.
2ACT Grammar and Usage; ACT Sentence StructureAgreement, pronouns, verb forms, idioms; fragments, run-ons, modifiers, parallel structure.
3Concision; Relevance (add/delete); Organization and Order; Word Choice and ToneThe 'rhetorical' questions: what to cut, keep, move and choose.

How to study each lesson page
  • Read the rules and the ✓/✗ examples slowly and copy the key rule into your notebook.
  • Cover the answer of each worked example and try it yourself first.
  • Take the quiz at the end without looking back at the lesson.
  • Read the explanation of every question, not only the ones you got wrong. A correct guess is still a gap in your knowledge.
Classic mistakes about the ACT
  • Leaving questions blank. There is no penalty, so a blank is always a lost chance.
  • Reading a whole English essay first and answering afterwards. Answer as you read.
  • Thinking that NO CHANGE is a trick and never choosing it — or choosing it whenever you are unsure. Judge it like any other choice.
  • Spending two minutes on one English question. At about 42 seconds per question, one long fight costs you three easy points later.
  • Studying from old materials that describe the longer pre-2025 test.
Fast strategy for test day
  • English: read until the end of the sentence that contains the underline, then look at the choices.
  • Look down the four choices and ask: what is changing? That tells you which rule is being tested.
  • When two choices are both grammatically correct and say the same thing, the shorter one is usually right.
  • Reading: share the 40 minutes evenly between the passages and check the clock after each one; never spend extra minutes on one question.
  • Answer every question before time is called.

Check your understanding

This short untimed test has two parts. Questions 1–4 check that you understand how the test works. Questions 5–8 are real-format ACT English items on one short narrative essay; the numbers in the passage show which underlined portion each question refers to.

Remember
  • Core sections: English 50 q / 35 min, Math 45 q / 50 min, Reading 36 q / 40 min — 125 minutes in total.
  • Composite (1–36) = average of English, Math and Reading. Science and Writing are optional and separate.
  • English: 5 scored essays (3 × about 340 words with 10 questions, 2 × about 185 words with 5 questions); underlined portions; NO CHANGE is a real option.
  • Reading: 3 scored passages × 9 questions; one literary narrative and two informational; one paired set or one passage with graphs.
  • Not adaptive and no penalty: answer every question.
