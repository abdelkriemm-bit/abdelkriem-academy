What is the Checkpoint 6 Science test?

Cambridge Primary Checkpoint Science is a test taken at the end of Cambridge Primary, that is, at the end of Stage 6. It is set and marked by Cambridge International, not by your school. The test code is 0097, the same code as the Stage 6 science curriculum (framework). Older papers with the code 0846 belong to the previous curriculum, so if you practise with them, some topics will be different.

The curriculum has four content strands — Biology, Chemistry, Physics, Earth and space — plus Thinking and Working Scientifically (how scientists plan, measure and explain). There is also a part called Science in Context (how science is used in your area and how scientific ideas change over time). Cambridge says Science in Context is not directly assessed in the test, but it helps you understand the topics.

Together the two papers give 80 marks in 70 minutes. The cover says no additional materials are needed: everything you need (diagrams, tables, graph grids) is printed in the booklet. You write your answers and any working in the spaces in the booklet.

Recent papers have had about 10 to 13 numbered questions, printed on 16 to 20 pages. Each numbered question is about one context (for example, "Sara investigates how quickly sugar dissolves") and is split into parts (a), (b), (c), (d). A whole question is usually worth 2 to 5 marks. Most parts are worth 1 mark, and some are worth 2 marks. The number of marks is printed next to each part, like this: [1] or [2].

How to use your 35 minutes

40 marks in 35 minutes means you have about 52 seconds per mark (35 × 60 = 2100 seconds; 2100 ÷ 40 = 52.5 seconds). That is fast. Checkpoint science questions are short, so you can do it — but only if you do not stop for too long on one part.

  1. Read the context at the top of each question (the stem). It often contains information you need in part (c) or (d).
  2. Look at the marks. [1] = one fact or one word. [2] = two separate points, or a point + a reason.
  3. Answer every part. A blank space can never get a mark; a sensible scientific guess sometimes can.
  4. If a part is hard, write something short, mark it with a small star in the margin, move on, and come back at the end.
  5. Use the last few minutes to check units, ticks (did you tick the right number of boxes?) and graphs (points plotted in pencil, exactly).

Question styles you will meet

Checkpoint Science questions are context-based: each question tells a short story about a learner, an investigation or an object. Most questions mix a content topic with Thinking and Working Scientifically. For example, a question about dissolving may also ask you to name the independent variable or to spot an anomalous result.

Understanding the mark scheme

After the test, examiners mark your answers with a mark scheme. Knowing how it works helps you write answers that earn every mark. The mark scheme has four columns: Question, Answer, Marks, Further information. The important ideas are below.

Word or signWhat it meansWhat it means for you
/Separates different answers that are all correct for the same mark.There is often more than one right answer — choose the one you are sure of.
( )Words or units in brackets are not needed for the mark.But writing them does no harm, and it makes your answer clearer.
underlined wordThat exact word is needed.Learn key words exactly: "capillaries", not "small tubes", when the word is asked for.
acceptAnother answer that also gets the mark.
do not acceptA wrong answer that cancels a right one.Never write two answers "just in case": a wrong one next to a right one can lose the mark.
ignoreExtra words that are not relevant; they do not lose the mark but earn nothing.Do not waste time writing extra.
ecf (error carried forward)If you made a mistake in one part and use your wrong answer correctly in the next part, you can still get the next mark. This is sometimes called "follow through".Always try the next part, even if you think your first answer was wrong.
ora (or reverse argument)The opposite way of saying the same thing also gets the mark."Metal is a better conductor than wood" = "wood is a worse conductor than metal".
independent marking pointsUnless the mark scheme says otherwise, each mark is given separately.In a [2] answer you can get 1 mark even if the second point is wrong.
show your workingWrite the numbers you used, not only the final answer.If the final answer is wrong, clear working can still help an examiner see what you did.

Two more rules help you: spelling — a word spelled the way it sounds still gets the mark, unless the spelling turns it into a different science word (for example, "vain" for vein is fine, but writing "artery" when you mean "vein" is simply wrong). Grammar is not marked — only the science. Examiners can also use professional judgement for answers that match the marking points in different words.

Worked examples: how marks are given

Worked example 1 — a 1-mark naming question

Question: Omar boils some water in a kettle. Name the change of state when water turns into water vapour. [1]

Answer writtenMarkWhy
evaporation / boiling1Both are accepted: each names a liquid turning into a gas.
evapration1Spelled as it sounds — still clearly the right word.
condensation0Wrong: that is gas turning into liquid.
boiling or condensing0A wrong answer next to a right one contradicts it.

Common mistake: writing two answers to be safe. The examiner cannot choose for you.

Worked example 2 — a 2-mark explanation

Question: Huda puts an ice cube on a metal tray and another ice cube on a plastic tray. The ice on the metal tray melts first. Explain why. [2]

  1. Count the marks: [2] means two separate points.
  2. Point 1 (fact): metal is a good thermal conductor / plastic is a poor conductor (an insulator).
  3. Point 2 (reason): so heat moves from the warm room, through the metal, into the ice faster.
  4. Answer: "Metal is a better thermal conductor than plastic, so heat travels into the ice faster through the metal tray and it melts sooner."

Common mistake: "Metal is cold." This is not a scientific reason and earns 0. Metal only feels cold because it conducts heat away from your hand quickly.

Worked example 3 — a calculation with error carried forward

Question: A bottle of fizzy drink has a mass of 525 g. After the lid is opened and the gas escapes for an hour, its mass is 518 g. (a) Work out the change in mass. [1] (b) Explain what your answer tells you about gases. [1]

  1. (a) 525 − 518 = 7 g (write the unit).
  2. (b) The mass went down because gas left the bottle, so gas has mass.
  3. If a student wrote 525 − 518 = 17 g in part (a) (a mistake), they lose the mark in (a), but in (b) "the mass decreased because gas (with mass) escaped" still earns the mark — the mistake is not punished twice (ecf).

How results are reported

Your papers are sent to Cambridge and marked there. Each learner then receives a statement of achievement, and the school receives a diagnostic feedback report. Results are reported for the subject as a whole and for each strand (for example, how well you did in Biology compared with Physics). This shows your teachers and parents which areas are strong and which need more practice.

For parents

The diagnostic feedback report is very useful: it shows strengths and weaknesses by strand. Ask the school to share it, and use the matching section of this course (Biology, Chemistry, Physics, Earth and space) to practise the weaker strand.

Thinking and Working Scientifically (TWS)

TWS is not a separate topic — it is in almost every Checkpoint question. It has four parts. The page Thinking and Working Scientifically in this course teaches each one with many examples; here is the big picture.

PartWhat you doExample
Models and representationsUse models and diagrams to explain ideas.Drawing the particles in a solid and a liquid; a model of the Sun, Earth and Moon.
Purpose and planningAsk questions, predict, plan a fair test, think about risks."I predict sugar dissolves faster in hot water because the particles move faster."
Carrying out enquiryChoose equipment, measure accurately, repeat, record in tables, use keys and secondary sources.Use a thermometer to measure water temperature to the nearest 1 °C, three times.
Analysis, evaluation and conclusionsDescribe patterns, spot anomalous results, conclude, suggest improvements, draw graphs."As the temperature increases, the time to dissolve decreases. The result at 40 °C is anomalous."

Scientists use five main types of scientific enquiry. You must know what each one is and choose the right one for a question:

Type of enquiryWhat it isExample question
Fair testingChange one variable, measure another, keep everything else the same.Does the temperature of water change how fast salt dissolves?
Observing over timeWatch and record the same thing changing over minutes, days or months.How does the Moon's shape change over one month?
Pattern seekingCollect data from many things you cannot control, then look for a pattern.Do people who exercise more have a lower resting heart rate?
Identifying and classifyingSort things into groups using their features; use or make a key.Which of these rocks are igneous, sedimentary or metamorphic?
ResearchUse secondary sources (books, reliable websites, experts) to find answers you cannot test yourself.How did scientists find out that the heart pumps blood around the body?

Common mistakes in Checkpoint Science
  • Writing two answers "to be safe" — a wrong answer next to a right one loses the mark.
  • Giving only one point for a [2] question — two marks need two separate ideas.
  • Forgetting units: 7 is not an answer; 7 g is.
  • Using everyday words instead of science words: "the heart pushes blood" is fine, but "blood goes in the tubes" is too vague when the question wants "arteries" or "veins".
  • Ignoring the context: if the question is about Sara's experiment with sugar, your answer must be about sugar and water, not a general rule.
  • Leaving a blank. Always write something scientific.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • The command word tells you what to do: Name = a word or short phrase; Describe = say what happens or what the pattern is; Explain = say why (use "because"); Predict = say what you think will happen; Suggest = use your knowledge in a new situation — any sensible scientific idea can score.
  • Most questions mix a topic with investigation skills. Revise the variables words for every topic.
  • Tables and graphs appear in most questions: practise reading values and describing patterns ("as … increases, … decreases").
  • Diagrams must be drawn in pencil with a ruler: circuit symbols, force arrows, food web arrows.
  • Calculations are short (for example, a change in mass or temperature) — show the subtraction and write the unit.
