American Diploma · ACT · English: Organisation, Relevance and Style

Organisation: Sentence Placement, Paragraph Order, Introductions and Conclusions

Where a sentence belongs, how sentences and paragraphs should be ordered, and how to choose the best introduction, conclusion and transition sentence on the ACT.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Organisation questions belong to Production of Writing, the reporting category that is 38–43% of the ACT English score. They test whether ideas are in a logical order and whether the writing holds together: each sentence in the right place, each paragraph in the right place, and clear openings, closings and links.

These questions are usually not about grammar. All four choices are normally correct English; what changes is the order or the job of a sentence. They come in five forms:

FormTypical stem (in our own words)
Sentence placement"For the sake of logic and cohesion, Sentence 3 should be placed: where it is now / before Sentence 1 / after Sentence 1 / after Sentence 4."
Adding a sentence in the right place"The writer wants to add the following sentence to this paragraph/essay … The sentence would most logically be placed after Sentence __ / at the end of Paragraph __."
Paragraph order"For the sake of logic and cohesion, Paragraph 3 should be placed: where it is now / before Paragraph 1 / after Paragraph 4 …"
Introductions"Which choice most effectively introduces the essay / the main topic of the paragraph?"
Conclusions and transition sentences"Which choice most effectively concludes the paragraph / the essay?" "Which choice provides the most effective transition from the previous paragraph to this one?"

Signal: when a passage has a placement or order question, its sentences are numbered (1), (2), (3) or its paragraphs are labelled [Paragraph 1], [Paragraph 2]. When you see numbers, read that paragraph with order in mind — the problem is often obvious on first reading.

Sentence placement: the method

  1. Read the sentence to be moved and circle its pointing words: this, these, such, it, they, he, also, another, then, the + a noun, a time word.
  2. Find the partner of each pointing word. "These signals" needs signals described just before; "He advised us" needs a man already named.
  3. Look at both neighbours. The sentence must follow what it refers to AND lead into what comes next.
  4. Test your answer by reading the three sentences in a row. Every pronoun needs a clear partner and the time order must run forward.
  5. Check "where it is now" first. It is a real answer. Move a sentence only if you can name a problem in its current place.
Clue in the sentenceWhat it tells you✓ / ✗
this / these / such + nounthe idea must appear just before✓ A fire burned at night. This light guided ships. ✗ This light guided ships. A fire burned at night.
he, she, it, theythe person or thing must be named first✓ We called an engineer. He checked the roof. ✗ He checked the roof. We called an engineer.
also, another, too, a seconda first one must come before✓ The first problem was weight. Another was water. ✗ Another problem was water. The first was weight.
for example, for instancethe general claim comes first✓ Many birds migrate. For example, storks cross Sinai. ✗ Storks cross Sinai. Many birds migrate, for example.
first, then, later, by 1900follow the time order✓ He opened a shop in 1990. Ten years later, he opened a second. ✗ Ten years later, he opened a second shop. He opened his first in 1990.
a full introduction: "a farmer named Ali"this is the first mention — it must come early✓ A farmer named Ali owned the land. Ali grew cotton. ✗ Ali grew cotton. A farmer named Ali owned the land.
so, as a result, thereforethe cause must come just before✓ The soil was heavy, so we called an engineer. ✗ So we called an engineer. The soil was heavy.

Adding a new sentence works the same way. Read the new sentence, circle its pointing words and find the one place where every partner comes just before it and the next sentence still follows smoothly. If the question asks about the whole essay ("at the end of Paragraph 2"), first decide which paragraph shares its topic, then where in that paragraph.

Logical order inside a paragraph

Even without pointing words, sentences follow patterns. Know the common ones:

PatternOrderExample of the order
Time order (narrative, history, process)earlier → later; step 1 → step 2built → stood 1,000 years → earthquakes → ruin → fortress
General → specificclaim → example or detail"Camels suit the desert." → "They can go days without water."
Claim → evidence → explanationpoint → proof → why it matters"The scheme worked." → "Waste fell by half." → "Less rubbish reached the landfill."
Problem → solutionwhat went wrong → how it was fixed"Trains used different times." → "Companies adopted one time."
Cause → effectreason → result"The floods stopped." → "The fields lost their natural mud."
Known → newan idea already introduced → a new idea built on it"the lighthouse" (known) → "its fire" (new)
  • ✓ The market opens at dawn. By eight it is crowded. By noon, most stalls are empty.
  • ✗ The market opens at dawn. By noon, most stalls are empty. By eight it is crowded.
  • ✓ The bridge was badly damaged. Engineers closed it for repairs. It reopened in May.
  • ✗ Engineers closed the bridge for repairs. It reopened in May. The bridge was badly damaged.

Paragraph order

Paragraph-order questions are sentence placement on a larger scale, and they usually come at the end of the essay. Compare the first sentence of the paragraph in question with the last sentence of the paragraph that would come before it. Look for:

  • time order (1840s before 1883; first day before last day);
  • counting words ("the first problem" before "another problem");
  • an idea introduced before it is discussed ("this method" needs the method first);
  • problem before solution, cause before effect;
  • the introduction first and the conclusion last — a paragraph that sums up or looks to the future belongs at the end.

Answer paragraph-order questions only after you have read the whole essay. As with sentences, "where it is now" is often correct.

Introductions

An introduction (to the essay or to a paragraph) must name the specific topic that the following sentences develop. Before you look at the choices, say the topic in one short sentence.

Right choiceWrong choices usually …
Names the exact topic of what follows ("Few buildings have disappeared as completely as the lighthouse of Alexandria.")are too broad ("People have always liked tall buildings."), off-topic ("Alexandria is a large city."), or about one small detail from later in the paragraph.

Conclusions

A conclusion closes the paragraph or essay. For the essay, the right choice usually returns to the main idea, the title or an image from the opening, sometimes showing how things have changed. It never starts a new topic.

  • ✓ The broken chairs are gone, and the old tanks now water the garden. (returns to the opening scene)
  • ✗ Next year, we may keep chickens on the roof. (new topic)
  • ✗ Gardening is good for everyone. (too general — could end any essay)
  • ✗ Tomatoes need six hours of sun. (a small new detail)

Read the stem closely: "conclude the paragraph" means close THIS paragraph's point; "conclude the essay" means close the whole essay; "return to the opening" means repeat an idea or image from the first paragraph.

Transition sentences between paragraphs

A transition sentence is a bridge. It must touch both banks: the idea of the paragraph before and the idea of the paragraph after. The ACT's wrong choices usually touch only one bank or neither.

  • ✓ Weight was not our only problem: the plants would also need water every day. (back: weight / forward: water)
  • ✗ The engineer charged us only a small fee. (looks back only)
  • ✗ Hassan connected a hose to the tank. (jumps forward only — no link)
  • ✗ Hoses come in many colours. (neither)

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — sentence placement

Passage: (1) My uncle opened his first shop in Mansoura in 1990. (2) Within ten years, he had added a printing service and a second branch. (3) It sold only notebooks and pens. (4) Today he owns six shops across the Delta.

For the sake of logic and cohesion, Sentence 3 should be placed:

  • A) where it is now.
  • B) before Sentence 1.
  • C) after Sentence 1.
  • D) after Sentence 4.

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "It" would seem to refer to the second branch, and the time jumps back from "within ten years" to the beginning.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: "It" would have nothing to refer to — the shop has not been mentioned.
  • C) ✓ Correct: "It" = his first shop. The order becomes: first shop (1990) → what it sold → ten years later → today. Pointing word and time order both work.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: "It" would refer to six shops (plural), and the paragraph would end by going back in time.

Worked example 2 — adding a sentence in the right place

Passage: (1) Salt was once so valuable that traders crossed the Sahara to buy it. (2) Caravans of camels carried huge slabs from desert mines to markets in the south. (3) There, it was exchanged for gold, cloth and grain. (4) Today, salt is one of the cheapest items in any shop. The writer wants to add: "Some of these caravans included hundreds of animals."

The sentence would most logically be placed after:

  • A) Sentence 1.
  • B) Sentence 2.
  • C) Sentence 3.
  • D) Sentence 4.

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "These caravans" needs caravans already mentioned; Sentence 1 mentions traders, not caravans.
  • B) ✓ Correct: Sentence 2 introduces the caravans; the new sentence adds a detail about them before the story moves to the markets.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: After Sentence 3, "these caravans" is too far from its partner and the detail interrupts the trading at the market.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: After Sentence 4 the essay has moved to today — the detail would jump back in time.

Worked example 3 — introducing a paragraph

Passage: __________ Some farmers in Fayoum now grow tomatoes in plastic tunnels that keep out insects. Others use phone apps to decide exactly when to water. A few have even begun selling their vegetables online.

Which choice most effectively introduces the paragraph?

  • A) Fayoum is about a hundred kilometres from Cairo.
  • B) Farming in Fayoum is changing as growers adopt new methods.
  • C) Tomatoes are one of Egypt's most popular vegetables.
  • D) Many people enjoy shopping online.

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Off-topic location fact — nothing about methods.
  • B) ✓ Correct: Names the specific topic all three sentences develop: new farming methods.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Too narrow: tomatoes appear in only one sentence.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Off-topic and too broad: online shopping is only one detail of the third sentence.

Worked example 4 — transition between paragraphs

Passage: Paragraph A explains how sand dunes in the Western Desert move a few metres each year. Paragraph B begins: "__________ Engineers now plant rows of hardy shrubs to hold the sand in place."

Which choice provides the most effective transition into Paragraph B?

  • A) Sand is made of tiny pieces of rock.
  • B) The Western Desert is very large.
  • C) Some dunes are taller than buildings.
  • D) Because moving dunes can bury roads and farms, people have looked for ways to stop them.

Answer: D

  • A) ✗ Wrong: Off-topic fact about sand — no link to stopping dunes.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Too general; no link to the shrubs.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: A detail about dunes that only looks back.
  • D) ✓ Correct: Looks back (moving dunes) and forward (ways to stop them) — exactly a bridge.
Classic traps
  • Moving a sentence that is fine: "where it is now" is often correct — check it first.
  • Orphan pointing word: a placement that leaves "it," "these" or "he" with nothing before it.
  • Half a partner: "these signals" placed after only ONE signal; "both" after one thing.
  • Broken time order: a later event placed before an earlier one.
  • Too-broad introduction: about the general subject (tall buildings, gardening) instead of this essay's topic.
  • Too-narrow introduction: about one detail from a single later sentence.
  • One-way transition: only looks back or only looks forward.
  • New-topic conclusion: opens a new subject (chickens next year) instead of closing the essay.
Fast strategy for test day
  • When you see numbered sentences, read that paragraph slowly and note anything that sounds out of order.
  • Circle pointing words and connect each one to its partner.
  • Read the three sentences around your chosen spot in order — it must sound smooth.
  • Leave paragraph-order and whole-essay questions until you finish the essay.
  • For introductions and conclusions, say the essay's main idea in one sentence first, then match it.

Practice

Read the two essays and answer the 10 questions. Sentences and paragraphs are numbered for the order questions. Questions 9 and 10 ask about the second essay as a whole.

Remember
  • Pointing words (this, these, it, he, also, another, so) must come right after what they point to.
  • Order clues: time, first → another, problem → solution, cause → effect, general → specific.
  • Introductions name the specific topic; conclusions close it and often return to the opening.
  • A transition sentence looks back AND forward.
  • "Where it is now" is a real answer — check it first.
