Purity, Concentration and Chromatography

What purity means and how to calculate it, how concentration depends on the number of solute particles in a volume, and how to carry out and read paper chromatography.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 Science — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Know that purity describes how much of one particular chemical is in a sample. (8Cm.04)
  • Understand that the concentration of a solution depends on how many solute particles are in a volume of the solvent. (8Cp.01)
  • Describe how paper chromatography separates the substances in a sample and helps identify them. (8Cp.02)

2. What does "pure" mean in science?

In everyday language "pure" often means "natural" or "with nothing added" — a carton may say "100% pure orange juice". In science, a pure substance contains only one chemical. Orange juice contains water, sugars, acids and vitamins, so a scientist calls it a mixture.

Most real samples are not perfectly pure. They contain small amounts of other substances called impurities. Purity describes how much of the specific chemical you want is in the sample. A sample that is 99% pure is almost all the wanted chemical; one that is 60% pure contains a lot of other substances.

ExampleWhy purity matters
MedicinesImpurities could be harmful, so medicines must be extremely pure and are tested carefully.
Drinking waterTap water is not pure (it has dissolved minerals) but it must be free from harmful impurities such as microorganisms and toxic metals.
Gold jewellery24 carat gold is pure gold. 18 carat gold is 18/24 = 75% gold; other metals are added to make it harder.
Table saltSalt from the sea is purified to remove sand and other salts.

3. Calculating percentage purity

When you know the mass of the wanted chemical in a sample, you can work out its purity as a percentage:

Formula

percentage purity = (mass of the wanted chemical ÷ total mass of the sample) × 100

  1. Find the mass of the wanted chemical (sometimes you must subtract the mass of the impurities first).
  2. Divide by the total mass of the sample.
  3. Multiply by 100 and write the % sign.

You can also use particles: if 16 out of 20 particles are the wanted substance, purity = 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80%.

How do scientists check purity?

A pure substance melts and boils at fixed temperatures. For example, pure water boils at 100 °C (at normal air pressure) — salty water boils at a higher temperature. So if a sample melts or boils at a different temperature, or over a range of temperatures, it is probably impure. Chromatography (section 6) is another check: a pure substance gives only one spot.

4. Concentration of a solution

When a solute (such as sugar) dissolves in a solvent (such as water) it makes a solution. The solute particles spread out between the solvent particles. The concentration tells you how many solute particles there are in a certain volume of solvent.

  • More solute particles in the same volume → more concentrated.
  • The same number of solute particles in a bigger volume → more dilute (less concentrated).
  • To dilute a solution, add more solvent. The number of solute particles stays the same, but they are spread through more liquid.
  • To make a solution more concentrated, dissolve more solute in it, or evaporate some of the solvent.
  • For a coloured solution, a more concentrated solution usually looks darker (more coloured particles in each cm³).

Comparing concentrations with numbers. Always compare the mass of solute in the same volume. For example, 10 g of sugar in 50 cm³ of solution is the same concentration as 20 g in 100 cm³ (double both). 15 g in 100 cm³ is less concentrated than that.

5. How paper chromatography works

Many coloured substances — inks, food colourings, plant dyes — are really mixtures of several dyes. Paper chromatography separates them so you can see how many substances are present and identify them.

  1. Draw a start line in pencil about 2 cm from the bottom of the chromatography paper. (Pencil does not dissolve, so it will not move or add spots.)
  2. Put a small, concentrated spot of each sample on the line, spaced apart. Label each one in pencil under the line.
  3. Pour a little solvent (often water or ethanol) into a beaker. Hang the paper so the bottom dips into the solvent but the solvent level is below the start line.
  4. Cover the beaker with a lid so the solvent does not evaporate.
  5. The solvent soaks up the paper. As it passes the spots, it dissolves the substances and carries them upwards. Substances that are more soluble in the solvent travel further; less soluble ones travel a shorter distance.
  6. Take the paper out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark how far the solvent got (the solvent front) in pencil, then let the paper dry. The dried paper with its spots is the chromatogram.
Safety

Ethanol is flammable — keep it away from flames. Wear eye protection, and do not touch the spots or eat the food colourings used.

6. Reading a chromatogram

  • Each spot is one substance. The number of spots tells you the (minimum) number of substances in a sample. X has 2 spots, so it contains at least 2 substances.
  • A pure substance gives one spot. A, B and C each give one spot, so each could be a single substance.
  • Same height = same substance (on the same paper with the same solvent). X has spots level with A and B, so X contains A and B. Y has a spot level with C, so Y contains C.
  • A spot left on the start line is a substance that is insoluble in this solvent — it did not move. Y contains an insoluble substance as well as C.
  • If a spot in the unknown matches none of the known samples, the unknown contains a substance that was not tested.

Chromatography is used to identify food colourings, to check if a medicine is pure, to test athletes for banned substances, and by forensic scientists to match the ink on a document to a particular pen.

7. Worked examples

Worked example 1 — A 40 g sample of rock salt contains 2 g of sand. The rest is salt. Calculate the percentage purity of the salt. [2]

  1. Mass of salt = 40 − 2 = 38 g.
  2. Purity = (38 ÷ 40) × 100 = 95% (1 mark working, 1 mark answer).
  3. Common mistake: using the 2 g of sand: (2 ÷ 40) × 100 = 5% is the percentage of impurity, not the purity.

Worked example 2 — A particle diagram of a gas sample shows 25 particles: 20 are oxygen and 5 are nitrogen. (a) Is the oxygen pure? [1] (b) Calculate the percentage purity of the oxygen. [1]

  1. (a) No — it contains nitrogen particles (an impurity) as well as oxygen.
  2. (b) 20 ÷ 25 × 100 = 80%.

Worked example 3 — Solution A has 10 g of salt in 50 cm³. Solution B has 15 g of salt in 100 cm³. Which is more concentrated? Show how you decide. [2]

  1. Make the volumes the same: A = 10 g in 50 cm³ = 20 g in 100 cm³. B = 15 g in 100 cm³ (1).
  2. 20 g > 15 g in the same volume, so A is more concentrated (1).
  3. Common mistake: choosing B because 15 g is more than 10 g, without looking at the volume.

Worked example 4 — Explain, using particles, what happens to the concentration of a squash drink when you add more water. [2]

  1. The number of solute (squash) particles stays the same (1).
  2. They are spread through a larger volume of water, so there are fewer solute particles in each cm³ — the drink becomes more dilute (less concentrated) (1).

Worked example 5 — Use the chromatogram in section 6. (a) How many substances are in X? [1] (b) Which known dyes are in X? [1] (c) What does the spot on the start line for Y show? [1]

  1. (a) 2 (two spots).
  2. (b) A and B — X's spots are at the same heights as A's and B's spots.
  3. (c) Y contains a substance that is insoluble in the solvent, so it did not move.
  4. Exam tip: say "at the same height" or "travelled the same distance" — not just "the same".

Worked example 6 — Omar sets up chromatography. He draws the start line in felt-tip pen and pours the solvent in until it is 1 cm above the line. Describe two mistakes and explain why each is a problem. [4]

  1. Mistake 1: the line is in ink (1). The ink would dissolve and separate into its own spots, mixing with the results (1).
  2. Mistake 2: the solvent is above the start line (1). The samples would dissolve into the solvent in the beaker instead of moving up the paper (1).
  3. Exam tip: a "describe and explain" question needs what is wrong and why — 2 marks each.
Common mistakes
  • Calling a natural product like "pure honey" or "pure juice" a pure substance — in science these are mixtures.
  • Working out the percentage of impurity when the question asks for the purity.
  • Comparing concentrations without making the volumes the same.
  • Thinking that adding water removes solute particles — the number of solute particles stays the same.
  • Confusing dilute with dissolve. Dissolve = the solute mixes into the solvent; dilute = add more solvent.
  • Saying a spot on the start line "was not tested" — it means the substance is insoluble in that solvent.
  • Drawing the start line in ink, or letting the solvent cover the spots.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Draw particle diagrams of a pure substance and an impure sample (use different shapes or shading and a key).
  • Calculate a percentage purity — show the division and the × 100.
  • Circle the most concentrated / most dilute solution from particle diagrams or from masses and volumes.
  • Explain dilution using the words particles, same number, larger volume.
  • Label the chromatography apparatus and explain why the line is in pencil and the solvent is below it.
  • Read a chromatogram: how many substances, which known dyes match, which sample is pure, which contains an insoluble substance.

Practice

Remember
  • A pure substance contains only one chemical. Purity = how much of the wanted chemical is in a sample.
  • Percentage purity = mass of wanted chemical ÷ total mass × 100.
  • Concentration = how many solute particles are in a volume of solvent. Adding solvent dilutes; the number of solute particles stays the same.
  • Chromatography: pencil start line, small spots, solvent below the line, lid on. More soluble substances travel further.
  • One spot = one substance; same height = same substance; a spot on the start line = insoluble in that solvent.
