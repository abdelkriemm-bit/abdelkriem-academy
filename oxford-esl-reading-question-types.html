Reading: Every Question Type

Every question type in the 9280 Reading paper with an original mini text, a model answer, the reason it is right, a step-by-step method and the traps.

British & IGCSE › Oxford International GCSE English as a Second Language

The 9280 Reading paper uses three big families of questions: short open questions, multiple-choice questions (you write letters in boxes) and collation questions (you list several details). Inside these families there are about a dozen question types that come back in every series. This page shows each type with an original mini text, a model answer, the reason it is correct, a step-by-step method and the traps to avoid.

Golden rule for every type

Look at the marks in brackets before you answer. [1 mark] = one detail or one letter. [2 marks] = two details, two letters or two ideas. [4 marks] = four. The marks are your instructions.

1. Find a detail (short open question)

Skill: R1 identify and select. What it looks like: A direct question with a question word: when, where, who, how much, how many, what. Usually [1 mark].

Mini text: Paragraph 2: 'The library's new study zone opens on 3rd March. It is free for members, but non-members pay £2 a day. Laptops can be borrowed from the front desk, although they must stay inside the building.'

Question: How much do non-members pay to use the study zone? [1 mark]

Answer and why
  • Answer: £2 a day.
  • Why: The question word 'how much' asks for a price. 'Free' is for members, so it is the wrong group. The answer is short; no full sentence is needed.
  1. Underline the question word and the key noun (non-members, pay).
  2. Go to the paragraph named in the question.
  3. Scan for the key word or a synonym (non-members, pay).
  4. Read the whole sentence around it; check it answers the exact question.
  5. Write the shortest complete answer.
Traps
  • Mixing up groups: members vs non-members, adults vs children, weekdays vs weekends.
  • Copying a whole paragraph: the examiner needs the exact detail, and extra wrong information can cost the mark.
  • Giving a date when the question asks for a day, or a number without its unit (£2, 5 km, 3 hours).

2. Single-answer multiple choice (detail)

Skill: R1, R2. What it looks like: A question with three options A, B, C. You write ONE letter in the box. [1 mark].

Mini text: 'I was going to fly to Aswan on Thursday, but the flight was cancelled, so we took the overnight train on Friday instead. Dad says the train is more comfortable anyway!'

Question: How did the writer travel to Aswan? A by plane on Thursday · B by train on Thursday · C by train on Friday

Answer and why
  • Answer: C
  • Why: The plan was a Thursday flight, but it was cancelled. The real journey was 'the overnight train on Friday'. Option A uses words from the text (fly, Thursday) but describes the plan that did not happen.
  1. Read the question and answer it in your head first.
  2. Find the place in the text and read to the END of the sentence; plans often change after 'but'.
  3. Check each option: cross out the ones the text contradicts.
  4. Write one capital letter clearly in the box.
Traps
  • Options that describe a plan, a hope or someone else's choice, not what really happened.
  • Linking words that change the meaning: but, however, instead, although.
  • Numbers in words vs figures: 'a dozen' = 12, 'a fortnight' = two weeks, 'half an hour' = 30 minutes.

3. Main point of a paragraph

Skill: R2 understand ideas. What it looks like: 'What is the main point of paragraph 3?' with three options. [1 mark].

Mini text: Paragraph 3: 'Our town's new cycle lanes have changed how people travel. Over 400 students now cycle to school. The air near the main road is cleaner, and local shops say more customers stop to buy things on their way home.'

Question: What is the main point of paragraph 3? A To describe how students get to school · B To explain the effects of the new cycle lanes · C To complain about pollution on the main road

Answer and why
  • Answer: B
  • Why: Every sentence in the paragraph is about a result of the cycle lanes: more cyclists, cleaner air, more customers. A is only one detail (one sentence). C is wrong: the writer says the air is cleaner, not that he complains.
  1. Read the whole paragraph and ask: what is EVERY sentence about?
  2. Say the main point in five words in your head ('the good results of cycle lanes').
  3. Choose the option that covers the whole paragraph.
  4. Reject options that match only one sentence or that change the writer's attitude.
Traps
  • The 'one sentence' option: true, but too small.
  • The 'wrong attitude' option: complain / criticise / persuade when the writer only informs.
  • Choosing the option that contains the most words from the text.

4. Main purpose of the whole text / which statement best describes the text

Skill: R2 purpose. What it looks like: Usually the LAST question on each text: 'What is the main purpose of the email?' or 'Which statement best describes the whole article?' [1 mark].

Mini text: An email: Paragraph 1 thanks a friend's family for a lovely weekend at their farm. Paragraph 2 describes milking the cows and riding a horse for the first time. Paragraph 3 asks the friend to visit Cairo in the summer and stay with the writer's family.

Question: What is the main purpose of the email? A To describe life on a farm · B To thank the family and invite the friend to visit · C To ask for advice about horse riding

Answer and why
  • Answer: B
  • Why: The whole email does two things: it thanks (paragraph 1) and invites (paragraph 3). The farm description supports the thanks. A is only the middle paragraph. C is not in the text.
  1. Answer this question last for each text.
  2. Ask: why did the writer write this? Who is the reader? What does the writer want the reader to do or know?
  3. Use purpose verbs: inform, advise, persuade, describe, explain, invite, thank, complain, entertain, tell about an experience.
  4. Check that the option fits the beginning, middle AND end of the text.
Traps
  • 'Persuade' vs 'inform': an advert for activities informs parents about them, but a text that pushes you strongly to act ('Book now! Don't miss out!') persuades.
  • A balanced article (both sides) vs an article that explains only the advantages.
  • For literary non-fiction, the purpose is often to describe an experience or show how the writer felt, not to persuade readers to visit a place.

5. Which two (or four) statements are correct?

Skill: R2, R3, R4. What it looks like: A list of statements (often A-D, A-H or more). You write the number of letters that matches the marks. [2-5 marks].

Mini text: Paragraph 4: 'The summer camp runs for two weeks in July. Campers aged 12 to 15 sleep in wooden cabins by the lake. Phones are collected on arrival and returned at the end of each week. Meals are cooked by a professional chef, but campers wash their own plates.'

Question: Which two statements are correct? A The camp lasts one week. · B Campers stay near water. · C Campers can use their phones every evening. · D Campers have some cleaning jobs. Write the correct letters in the boxes. [2 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: B and D (in any order).
  • Why: B: 'cabins by the lake' = near water. D: 'wash their own plates' = a cleaning job. A is wrong (two weeks). C is wrong (phones are collected and returned only at the end of each week).
  1. Count the boxes: two marks = two letters, no more.
  2. Mark each statement T (true), F (false) or ? next to it, checking it against the text.
  3. Look for paraphrase: the correct statements rarely use the same words as the text ('by the lake' becomes 'near water').
  4. If you have more true statements than boxes, re-read: one of them must have a small wrong detail.
Traps
  • Statements that are 'nearly true' but change one detail (number, time, who, always/sometimes).
  • Extreme words: always, never, only, all, every. They are often the wrong statements.
  • Writing three letters for two boxes: you do not get extra credit, and it may cost marks.

6. List / collation questions

Skill: R3 collate and present. What it looks like: 'List three things...' or 'Give two reasons...' with numbered lines. One mark per correct, different point. [2-5 marks].

Mini text: Paragraph 2: 'The new sports centre has something for everyone. There is an indoor pool with a slide for younger children, a climbing wall, and a gym with modern equipment. Parents can relax in the café, which also sells healthy snacks.'

Question: List three facilities at the sports centre. [3 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: 1 an indoor pool (with a slide) 2 a climbing wall 3 a gym (any three of: pool, climbing wall, gym, café).
  • Why: Each line gives one different facility from the text. A short noun phrase is enough. 'Healthy snacks' is something the café sells, not a separate facility, so it is safer to choose clearer points.
  1. Underline what you are collecting (facilities, reasons, problems, things the writer says about...).
  2. Go through the paragraph and number each possible point in the margin.
  3. Choose the clearest ones; write ONE point per line.
  4. Use short phrases from the text; you do not need to change the words for list questions.
  5. Check: are all points different? Do they all answer the question?
Traps
  • Writing two points on one line and leaving another line empty: use every line.
  • Repeating the same idea in different words ('cheap', 'not expensive').
  • Including details from the wrong paragraph when the question limits you to one paragraph.

7. Good points and bad points (sorting into two lists)

Skill: R3, R4. What it looks like: In an opinion article you may be asked to list advantages and disadvantages, or the good and bad points the writer mentions. [several marks].

Mini text: 'Online classes saved me two hours of travel every day, and I could replay difficult lessons. On the other hand, I missed my friends, and staring at a screen for six hours gave me headaches.'

Question: List two good points and two bad points about online classes mentioned by the writer. [4 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: Good: 1 saves (two hours of) travel time 2 can replay difficult lessons. Bad: 1 missing friends / loneliness 2 headaches from the screen.
  • Why: Signal phrases split the text: 'On the other hand' moves from good points to bad points.
  1. Draw two columns on the question paper: + and -.
  2. Look for signal words: however, on the other hand, but, the downside is, another benefit is.
  3. Put each point in the right column, then copy the clearest ones onto the answer lines.
Traps
  • Putting a point in the wrong list because of a positive word: 'it's true that it wastes time' is a BAD point, even though it starts with 'true'.
  • Listing the writer's opinion about other people instead of a point about the topic.

8. Explain in your own words (word, phrase or sentence)

Skill: R2 vocabulary. What it looks like: 'In paragraph 3, the writer says, "...". Explain in your own words what this phrase means.' The marks show the number of ideas. [1-3 marks].

Mini text: 'After the storm, the fishermen's village was a hive of activity: people were repairing boats, mending nets and clearing the beach.'

Question: Explain in your own words what 'a hive of activity' means. [2 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: A place that is very busy, where a lot of people are working / doing things at the same time.
  • Why: Two ideas are explained: 'hive' (a busy place full of people, like bees in a hive) and 'activity' (people working/doing things). The example after the colon (repairing, mending, clearing) is the context clue.
  1. Count the marks: 2 marks = two ideas.
  2. Split the phrase into its key parts (hive / activity).
  3. Find a synonym or short explanation for each part. Use the sentences around it as clues.
  4. Write a simple sentence that includes all parts. Do NOT reuse the same words.
Traps
  • Repeating the words from the text ('it is a hive with activity').
  • Explaining only one part of a 2- or 3-mark phrase.
  • Translating word by word when the phrase is an idiom: think about the meaning in context. See the Vocabulary in context page for full practice.

9. Find phrases that show an idea

Skill: R3, R4. What it looks like: 'In paragraphs 2 and 3, find three phrases that show the writer was nervous.' You copy short phrases from the text. [2-4 marks].

Mini text: 'By the third day my legs felt like lead. I dragged myself up the last hill, stopping every few metres. That night I fell asleep before dinner, still wearing my boots.'

Question: List three phrases which show that the writer was exhausted. [3 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: 1 'my legs felt like lead' 2 'I dragged myself up the last hill' / 'stopping every few metres' 3 'fell asleep before dinner' / 'still wearing my boots'.
  • Why: Each phrase shows tiredness in a different way: heavy legs, slow movement, sleeping early in his clothes. Short exact quotations are best.
  1. Underline the idea you are looking for (exhausted).
  2. Find every phrase that SHOWS it, even if the word 'tired' never appears.
  3. Copy short phrases (3-8 words) exactly, one per line.
  4. Choose phrases from the paragraphs named in the question only.
Traps
  • Copying a whole long sentence that contains two points on one line: you may only get one mark.
  • Changing the words when the question says 'phrases': copy them exactly.
  • Choosing a phrase that shows a different feeling (e.g. happiness at the end).

10. Inference: feelings and attitudes

Skill: R4 infer and deduce. What it looks like: 'How does the writer feel when...?', 'What does the writer's reaction suggest?' or an MCQ about feelings. The answer is suggested, not stated. [1-2 marks].

Mini text: 'When the results appeared on the screen, I read my name three times. Then I ran down the corridor, laughing, and nearly knocked over the head teacher.'

Question: How does the writer feel when she sees her results? Give one piece of evidence. [2 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: She feels delighted / extremely happy (1). Evidence: she ran down the corridor laughing (1).
  • Why: The text never says 'happy', but reading her name three times (disbelief) and running and laughing show great joy.
  1. Look at actions, body language and descriptions: what someone does tells you what they feel.
  2. Name the feeling with a precise adjective: relieved, nervous, proud, disappointed, amazed, frightened.
  3. If asked, support it with a short quotation.
  4. Check the feeling fits the whole moment, not one word.
Traps
  • Choosing a weak adjective ('good', 'fine') when the text shows a strong feeling.
  • Mixed feelings: a writer can be both excited and nervous. Read carefully; an MCQ option like 'a mixture of emotions' may be correct.
  • Confusing the writer's feelings with another person's in the text.

11. 'Which ... is NOT mentioned?'

Skill: R1, R2. What it looks like: An MCQ with a negative: 'Which of these is not mentioned in the article?' [1 mark].

Mini text: 'At the festival, my aunt cooked stuffed vine leaves, my cousins hung lanterns in the street, and my grandfather told stories about the old days.'

Question: Which activity is not mentioned? A cooking food · B dancing · C telling stories

Answer and why
  • Answer: B
  • Why: Cooking (the aunt) and telling stories (the grandfather) are both in the text. Nobody dances.
  1. Circle the word NOT in the question.
  2. Tick every option you find in the text.
  3. The option without a tick is the answer.
Traps
  • Forgetting the 'not' and choosing the first option you find in the text.
  • Options that are close to something in the text: 'hanging lanterns' is mentioned, so 'decorating the street' would also count as mentioned.

12. Explain a title or a writer's comment

Skill: R2, R4. What it looks like: 'The article is called "...". Explain what the title means.' or 'Why does the writer say...?' [1-2 marks].

Mini text: Title: 'Small Steps, Big Changes'. The article describes students who each planted one tree a month; after five years their school had a small forest.

Question: Explain what the title means. [2 marks]

Answer and why
  • Answer: Small actions repeated over time (one tree a month) can lead to an important, large result (a whole forest).
  • Why: Two ideas: 'small steps' = little actions, and 'big changes' = an important result. The answer links each idea to the text.
  1. Split the title or comment into its parts.
  2. Explain each part in simple words.
  3. Link it to what happens in the text.
Traps
  • Repeating the title.
  • Explaining only one half.

Question types you will NOT usually meet in this paper

The 9280 Reading paper is built from short open questions, multiple-choice questions and collation questions. Some popular exercise types from other exams are not part of this format, so do not waste revision time on them:

TypeIs it in the 9280 Reading paper?What to practise instead
True / False / Not GivenNo. The paper asks 'Which statements are correct?' and you choose letters.Type 5: checking each statement against the text.
Sentence completion / gap-fillNot in the Reading paper. Notes completion appears in the Listening paper (Task 3).Types 1 and 6: short exact details.
Matching headings to paragraphsNot as a separate task. The same skill is tested by 'main point of paragraph X'.Type 3: main point questions.

Practice: name the type and answer

Read the mini text, then answer each question. Try to name the question type before you look at the answer.

Mini text (paragraph 1): 'Last spring our class spent a week volunteering at a farm outside Fayoum. We planted olive trees, fed the goats and painted the farmer's fence. The work was hard and the sun was fierce, yet nobody complained. On the last evening, the farmer's wife cooked us a feast, and I realised I had not checked my phone for five whole days.'

  1. Where was the farm? [1 mark]

    Show answer
    Outside Fayoum. (Type 1: find a detail.)

  2. List three jobs the students did. [3 marks]

    Show answer
    1 planted olive trees 2 fed the goats 3 painted the (farmer's) fence. (Type 6: list.)

  3. Explain in your own words 'the sun was fierce'. [2 marks]

    Show answer
    The sunshine was very strong/hot (fierce = extremely strong or powerful, sun = sunshine/heat). (Type 8.)

  4. Which two statements are correct? A The students stayed for a month. B The work was easy. C The students did not complain. D The writer did not use his phone that week. [2 marks]

    Show answer
    C and D. A is wrong (a week). B is wrong (the work was hard). (Type 5.)

  5. What does the last sentence suggest about the writer's experience? [1 mark]

    Show answer
    He was so busy/happy/involved that he forgot about his phone. He enjoyed the experience. (Type 10: inference.)

  6. What is the main point of this paragraph? A To describe the farmer's family B To describe the students' week of volunteering C To complain about hard work in the sun

    Show answer
    B. A and C are only small details, and the writer does not complain. (Type 3.)
