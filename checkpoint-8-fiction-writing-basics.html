Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Fiction Writing Basics: Everything for Paper 2 Writing

All the elements of a Paper 2 story — plot, character, setting, conflict, dialogue, voice, tense, openings and endings — plus sentence starters, the G.C.S.P.P. planning technique and a strong 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is Paper 2 writing?

Paper 2 is the Fiction paper. It lasts 1 hour 10 minutes and is worth 50 marks: Section A Reading (25 marks) is based on a fiction extract from a novel or short story, and Section B Writing (25 marks) asks you to write one story. The suggested time for Section B is about 35 minutes. The same format is used in the Stage 8 Progression Test you sit in school and in the Checkpoint exam later.

The story task is usually linked to the theme of the reading extract. If the extract is about a boy learning a difficult craft, the writing task may ask for a story about someone learning a new skill. If the extract is about a trip that goes wrong, you may be asked for a story about an unexpected event. Under the task you normally get bullet prompts — short points such as who the character is, what happens and how the story ends — and a planning space. Planning is not marked, but it is where full-mark stories are born.

What you getWhat it means for you
One story task on a themeNo choice of task — you must be ready to write about any everyday theme (a challenge, a journey, a surprise, a friendship, a dream, a lesson learnt).
A link to the extractYou may borrow the THEME, never copy the extract's characters, sentences or plot. Your story must be your own.
Bullet promptsEvery bullet must appear clearly in your story. Each one usually becomes one stage of your story mountain.
About 35 minutesEnough time to plan and write a story of at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words), if you plan fast and do not cross out whole pages.
25 marksMarked in five categories (below). Grammar, punctuation and structure together are 14 of the 25 marks.
Two rules that can destroy your mark
  • A very short answer is capped: 20 words or fewer scores 0, and a piece of about 21–60 words can only reach a small total. Always write a complete story.
  • Writing the wrong text type (for example a diary or an article instead of a story) caps your Creation of texts mark at the bottom band and pulls your structure mark down too. Paper 2 = a STORY.

2. How your story is marked (25 marks)

Your story is marked with five categories. The names below are the official Cambridge names, with your teacher's older names in brackets where they differ.

CategoryMarksWhat a top story shows (in simple words)
Creation of texts5The story clearly answers the task and every bullet. Ideas are developed, not listed. A clear narrative voice. The reader stays interested from the first line to the last.
Vocabulary and language3Precise, ambitious words chosen for effect (strong verbs, exact nouns). Figurative language that creates a mood. Vocabulary that suits the genre.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7A clear story mountain. Paragraphs that change with time, place, speaker or event. Smooth links between paragraphs. A strong opening and a satisfying ending. Pace controlled on purpose.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7A range of sentence types used for effect. Correct, consistent past tense. Expanded noun phrases. Accurate commas, apostrophes and speech marks, plus a wider range (dashes, colon, semicolon, ellipsis) used correctly.
Word structure (spelling)3Common words always correct. Plurals, prefixes, suffixes, double consonants and homophones correct. Ambitious words spelt correctly.
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.

Markers use best fit: they start at the lowest band and move up while your story meets the descriptions. One beautiful sentence does not lift a weak story — the whole piece must be controlled.

3. The G.C.S.P.P. technique — plan before you write

Before you write a single sentence, spend about 5 minutes breaking the task down with G.C.S.P.P.: Genre · Characters · Setting · Point of view · Plot. This makes every big decision before you start, so you do not change your mind halfway through (which is where inconsistencies and wasted time come from). Each letter also links to a marking category, so this planning directly earns marks.

G — Genre. Decide what kind of story it is. The genre sets the mood, the vocabulary and the reader's expectations. If the task does not name a genre, choose the one that fits the theme — most Checkpoint themes (a challenge, a dream, a new skill, a holiday surprise) suit realistic fiction or adventure best.

GenreWhat the reader expectsTypical vocabulary
Realistic fictionBelievable people, everyday places, real problems and real feelingsnervous, relieved, stubborn, crowded, ordinary, hesitated
AdventureA goal or journey, risk, movement, couragescrambled, plunged, treacherous, summit, determined
MysteryA question, clues, secrets, suspicion, a final revealclue, suspicious, vanished, witness, puzzled
Horror / suspenseDread, isolation, darkness, delay before the dangereerie, flickering, creaked, shadow, motionless
Science fictionFuture technology, other worlds, a 'what if…?' ideahologram, orbit, malfunction, colony, signal
FantasyMagic, invented creatures or worlds with clear rulesenchanted, ancient, spell, realm, glimmering

C — Characters. Decide who is in the story, how many people you need (2–3 well-built characters beat 6 vague ones), what each person's job in the plot is, and how they relate to each other — who is the main character (protagonist), who or what is against them (antagonist), who helps. Give your main character one clear trait and one want (what they want or fear). This is what makes your content developed instead of flat.

S — Setting. Decide where and when. If the task gives a setting, use it and stay consistent. If not, choose one that fits your genre and that you can describe with real detail — a place you know (your street, a market, a beach in Alexandria, a desert camp) is easier to make vivid than a place you have never seen. The setting creates atmosphere. A cheerful sunny beach in a frightening story only works if you do it on purpose.

P — Point of view. Choose first person (I, we) for a personal, emotional voice, or third person (he, she, they) for more freedom to describe things the main character cannot see. If the task tells you which to use, obey. If you are continuing a story, you must keep the same point of view as the original. Switching from 'I' to 'he' in the middle of a story is a serious, easy-to-avoid error.

P — Plot. Decide exactly what part of the story you are writing: a whole story (most common in Year 8), an opening, an ending, or a continuation of the extract. Then map your story mountain in five quick notes (see section 5). If it is a continuation, re-read the extract first and lock in the characters, setting, point of view, tone and the exact moment where it stopped.

Worked example — G.C.S.P.P. on an exam-style task
  • Task: 'You have read an extract about a girl who is trusted with her family's boat for the first time. Write a story about a character who has to take responsibility for something important.' Bullets: who the character is and what they are responsible for · a problem that happens · how the story ends.
  • G: realistic fiction — warm but tense. Vocabulary of nerves and pride.
  • C: Youssef (13, eager but unsure of himself — wants to prove he is grown up). Uncle Hassan (owner of a bicycle stall, strict but kind). A delivery rider in a hurry (creates the problem).
  • S: a bicycle-repair stall on a busy Cairo street, the day before Eid — noise, heat, oil, coloured lights.
  • P: first person (Youssef) — so the reader feels his panic and pride from the inside.
  • P: whole story. Opening: uncle leaves him in charge. Build-up: easy morning, growing confidence. Problem: rider with a broken chain and no time. Climax: Youssef repairs it on the third try. Resolution: uncle returns and gives him a key — trust earned. (This is the plan for the sample story in section 15.)
Continuation tasks

If the task says 'continue the story', the extract is your rule book. Same characters with the same personalities, same setting, same point of view, same tense and the same tone. Start exactly where the extract stopped. Markers check whether your writing feels like a natural next page of the original, not a new story that happens to share a name.

4. Responding to the theme and the bullet prompts

The bullet prompts are not a list to copy. They are a skeleton for your story. A top story covers every bullet, in a developed way, and keeps the theme visible from start to finish. Follow these steps:

  1. Underline the theme words in the task (for example 'responsibility', 'a dream', 'an unexpected event', 'learning a skill'). Your ending should show what the character has learnt or how they have changed in relation to that theme.
  2. Number the bullets. Next to each, write which paragraph(s) will answer it.
  3. Decide what the problem is. A story without a problem is a description, not a story. The theme tells you where the problem lives: 'learning a skill' → the skill is hard. 'a dream come true' → something stands in the way of the dream.
  4. Do not answer the bullets like questions ('The character is Youssef. He is responsible for the stall.'). Turn them into scenes, with action, dialogue and description.
  5. Make sure the bullet about the ending is answered clearly. Many students run out of time and stop in the middle of the climax — that loses Creation and Structure marks.
Typical bulletWhere it goesHow to make it strong
Describe the character / who they areOpening paragraph(s)Show the character through one action, one line of speech and one detail of appearance (STEAL), not a list of adjectives.
Describe the place / where it happensOpening or build-upThree senses, one strong simile or metaphor, verbs that move.
What happens / the unexpected event / the problemBuild-up → climaxSlow down at the key moment: short sentences, feelings, sounds, one line of dialogue.
How the character feelsThroughout, peaking at the climaxShow feelings through the body and actions (hands shook, stomach knotted), not just 'he was scared'.
How it ends / what they learnResolutionA clear outcome plus a small final image that shows the change or lesson — do not preach.

5. Plot: the story mountain

Almost every good exam story follows the story mountain (plot structure). In 35 minutes, aim for 5–7 paragraphs. Short paragraphs (even one line) are allowed for drama, but use them on purpose.

StageWhat happensApprox. length in a 400–550 word story
1. Exposition (opening)Introduce the main character, the setting and a hint of what they want or fear. Hook the reader in the first line.70–100 words
2. Rising action (build-up)Things start to happen. Small events lead towards the problem. Tension grows.80–110 words
3. ClimaxThe most exciting or important moment — the problem at its worst, the big decision, the turning point. Slow down here.110–150 words
4. Falling actionThe result of the climax. The problem begins to be solved.60–90 words
5. Resolution (ending)The final outcome and how the character has changed. Link back to the theme and, if you can, to the opening.60–90 words
Pace is a structure mark

Move quickly through ordinary time ('By ten o'clock, I had sold three bells and swept the pavement twice.') and slow down at the climax (seconds described in detail). This control of pace is exactly what markers look for in the top band of Structure of texts.

6. The elements of a story

Character and characterisation. Characterisation is how you show what a character is like. Direct characterisation tells the reader ('Youssef was nervous'). Indirect characterisation shows it, and is almost always stronger. Use STEAL: Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks. Two or three STEAL details per character are enough in an exam story. (Full pack: Describing a Character.)

Setting. The time and place of the story. Describe it through the senses, not only sight, and choose one mood. You can also reveal setting indirectly: through dialogue ('It's nearly midnight,' she whispered), through imagery (The tarmac shimmered in the afternoon heat) or through sound (Horns blared from the bridge). (Full pack: Describing a Setting.)

Conflict. The problem that drives the plot. Without conflict there is no story.

Type of conflictMeaningExample idea
Character vs characterTwo people want different thingsTwo friends both want the captain's armband.
Character vs natureWeather, sea, desert, animalsA sandstorm hits a school trip in the Western Desert.
Character vs selfAn inner struggle: fear, guilt, doubtA boy is terrified of performing the solo he has practised for months.
Character vs society / rulesA rule, tradition or group stands in the wayA girl wants to join the football team, but the club has never had a girls' squad.
Character vs technology / the unknownMachines, mysteries, the supernaturalThe smart lock on the new flat refuses to open.

Theme and main idea. The main idea is what the story is about (a boy runs his uncle's stall alone). The theme is the deeper message (responsibility is earned through action). In Paper 2 the theme is often given — your job is to make it visible through what happens, not to explain it in a lecture at the end.

Literary elements. Foreshadowing is an early hint of what will come ('He did not notice the loose bolt on the back wheel.'). Irony is when the opposite of what is expected happens (the swimming champion is afraid of the bath). Dramatic irony is when the reader knows something the character does not. Flashback is a short scene from the past that explains the present. One of these, used well, lifts Creation of texts.

Imagery and figures of speech. Imagery appeals to the five senses. A simile compares using 'like' or 'as' (the spanners gleamed like silver fish). A metaphor says one thing is another (the market was a river of voices). Personification gives human qualities to objects (the old door complained). Onomatopoeia imitates a sound (clang, hiss). Alliteration repeats the first sound (silent, silver streets). One fresh comparison is worth more than five tired ones ('as white as snow').

7. Dialogue — how to punctuate speech

Dialogue shows character, moves the plot and makes a story feel real. Keep it short — two to six short exchanges in a whole exam story is plenty. Every line of speech must do a job: reveal a feeling, raise the tension or move the action forward.

RuleCorrect example
Put the spoken words inside inverted commas (speech marks)."Can you fix it?" he asked.
Punctuation that belongs to the speech goes INSIDE the closing speech marks."I have no time," he panted.
Use a comma (not a full stop) before the closing speech marks when a reporting clause follows."It's broken," she said. (not "It's broken." she said.)
If the speech is a question or exclamation, use ? or ! instead of the comma — and keep the reporting verb lower case."Where did you learn that?" I gasped.
If the reporting clause comes first, put a comma before the speech marks and start the speech with a capital letter.My uncle said, "Never touch the gears."
If one sentence of speech is split by the reporting clause, use commas and a small letter to continue."If anyone asks," he said, "the answer is no."
If a new sentence starts after the reporting clause, use a full stop and a capital."It works!" he laughed. "You're a genius."
Start a new line (new paragraph) every time the speaker changes."Chains?" he asked. / (new line) "Just one," I said.
Better than 'said'

Choose reporting verbs that show HOW the words are spoken: whispered, muttered, snapped, panted, stammered, insisted, gasped, called, admitted, grumbled. But do not use a fancy verb every time — 'said' is invisible and fine in quick exchanges. Add an action instead: "Chains?" He raised one eyebrow.

8. Narrative voice, tense and show-don't-tell

Narrative voice (point of view). First person ('I') feels personal and lets you share thoughts directly. Third person ('he', 'she') lets you describe more widely. Never use second person ('you') as the narrator, because 'you' belongs only inside dialogue. Whichever you choose, keep it all the way through.

Tense consistency. Write the story in the past tense (he walked, she had forgotten, they were waiting). Use the present tense only inside dialogue, or for facts that are still true ('Every building on our street has a doorman'). The most common Year 8 mistake is drifting into the present tense at the exciting part: 'He ran to the door. He opens it and sees…' — that costs Grammar and punctuation marks.

TenseUse it forExample
Past simpleThe main eventsI knelt on the pavement.
Past continuousBackground action / an action interruptedThe rain was drumming on the window when the lights went out.
Past perfectSomething that happened earlier than the main events (flashback)I had watched my uncle do it a hundred times.
Present (in speech only)What characters say"I have twenty orders," he panted.

Show, don't tell. Telling names a feeling. Showing lets the reader see the evidence and work it out. Showing is one of the clearest signs of a top-band story.

Telling (weak)Showing (strong)
He was scared.His hands froze on the handlebars, and he could hear his own heartbeat.
She was angry.She slammed the book shut so hard that the pencil jumped off the desk.
It was hot.The tarmac shimmered, and the metal tools burned his fingers.
The house was old.Paint curled from the door in long strips, and every stair complained underfoot.
He was happy.He could not stop grinning. He took the stairs two at a time.
Do not show everything

Sometimes a short, direct sentence is exactly right ('Nobody answered.'). The skill is balance: show the important feelings and moments. Tell the unimportant links quickly.

Ending rules for the exam
  • Never end with 'and then I woke up — it was all a dream.' It cancels your whole story.
  • If the bullets ask how the story ends, answer it. A cliffhanger is fine only after the main problem has been dealt with.
  • Do not end with a moral in capital letters ('THE MORAL IS…'). Show the lesson through an action or image.

10. Sentence patterns, sentence starters and sentence types

A range of sentence types and starters, used for effect, is one of the main things the Grammar and punctuation category rewards. Do not start every sentence with 'I', 'He' or 'The'. Use these eight sentence-starter patterns — then make sure each one fits the moment.

11. Punctuation for effect

Punctuation is marked in the same category as grammar, but learn it separately from sentence types. Use only the marks you can use correctly:

  • Ellipsis (…) — incomplete information, suspense or a pause, or an interruption: 'Once. Twice…'
  • Single dash (—) — emphasis on what follows, or an interruption in the middle of speech: 'He looked up — straight at me.' / '"Wait, I didn't —"'
  • Pair of dashes — always extra information: 'He took a key — a small brass one — from his pocket.'
  • Brackets — extra information: 'my aunt (who hated hospitals) was waiting in the car'.
  • Pair of commas — extra information: 'Uncle Hassan, still in his cap, counted the money.'
  • Colon — introduces a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information: 'I remembered my uncle's voice: slowly, link by link.'
  • Semicolon — joins two independent clauses (two related sentences): 'Uncle Hassan always did the chains; I only watched.'
  • Exclamation and question marks — strong feeling and the character's inner questions: 'What if it slipped again?'

12. Paragraph length

Paragraph length. Long paragraphs slow the reader down for description. Short paragraphs (sometimes one sentence) speed up action and create suspense. Start a new paragraph for a new time, place, speaker, event or feeling (remember TiPToP: Time, Place, Topic, Person).

15. A strong sample story

Sample story — The Key (realistic fiction)450 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: You have read an extract about a girl who is trusted with her family's fishing boat for the first time. Write a story about a character who has to take responsibility for something important. In your story you should: describe who the character is and what they are responsible for, describe a problem that happens, and explain how the story ends.

"Don't touch the gears unless you're sure, Youssef," Uncle Hassan said, pulling his cap down over his eyes. "And if anyone asks for credit, the answer is no."

I nodded, although my stomach had already folded itself into a small, tight knot. It was the morning before Eid, the busiest day of the year, and my uncle had to take my aunt to the hospital in Mansoura. For the first time in my life, the bicycle stall on Talaat Street was mine.

At first, everything was easy. Arranged in neat rows along the wall, the spanners gleamed like silver fish. I pumped up two tyres, sold a bell to a boy who rang it all the way home, and swept the pavement until it shone. By ten o'clock, I was whistling. Being in charge, I decided, was simple.

Then the delivery rider arrived.

He skidded to a stop in a cloud of dust, his bicycle groaning like an old door. The chain hung from the back wheel like a broken necklace, and a bag of hot ful sandwiches swung dangerously from the handlebars. "I have twenty orders and fifteen minutes," he panted. "Can you fix it? Please."

My hands froze. Uncle Hassan always did the chains; I only watched. I glanced at the clock, then at the rider's sweating face, then at the toolbox, which suddenly seemed full of strangers.

Taking a deep breath, I knelt on the burning pavement. I remembered my uncle's voice: slowly, link by link. I loosened the wheel, lifted the chain onto the smallest cog and turned the pedal gently. It slipped. Once. Twice… A crowd of curious children gathered behind me, and somebody's grandmother offered advice that nobody had asked for. Sweat stung my eyes, but I refused to give up. On the third attempt, the chain clicked into place with a sound sweeter than any music.

"It works!" the rider laughed, spinning the pedal. He pressed a note into one oily palm and a warm sandwich into the other, and then he was gone, weaving between the cars like a fish through reeds.

That evening, Uncle Hassan came back looking tired but relieved; my aunt was fine. He inspected the stall, counted the money and noticed the black stripes across my fingers.

"Chains?" he asked, raising one eyebrow.

"Just one," I said, trying not to smile.

He said nothing. Instead, he took a second key from his pocket — a small brass key that I had seen him guard for years — and placed it in my hand. The stall was still his, of course. But that night, walking home beneath the coloured Eid lights, I felt as if I had grown a little taller.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: every bullet is answered as a scene — the character and his responsibility (paragraphs 1–2), the problem (paragraphs 5–6), the ending (final three paragraphs). The theme of responsibility is visible at the start (the warning) and the end (the key), without a lecture. A consistent first-person voice keeps the reader close to Youssef's nerves and pride.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise verbs (skidded, groaning, panted, knelt, weaving). Fresh similes chosen for the setting (spanners 'like silver fish', chain 'like a broken necklace', the rider 'like a fish through reeds'). The metaphor of the toolbox 'full of strangers' shows panic without saying 'I was scared'.
  • Structure of texts 7/7: a clear story mountain — dialogue opening, calm build-up, a one-line paragraph ('Then the delivery rider arrived.') as the turning point, a slowed-down climax with short sentences ('It slipped. Once. Twice…'), falling action and a circular ending that echoes the uncle's opening warning. Time connectives (At first, By ten o'clock, That evening) link the paragraphs smoothly.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: all sentence types — simple ('My hands froze.'), compound ('Sweat stung my eyes, but I refused to give up.'), complex ('I nodded, although my stomach had already folded itself into a small, tight knot.'), compound-complex ('A crowd of curious children gathered behind me, and somebody's grandmother offered advice that nobody had asked for.'), minor sentences for effect ('Once. Twice…'). Varied starters (-ed starter 'Arranged in neat rows…', -ing starter 'Taking a deep breath…'). Accurate speech punctuation with new lines for each speaker. Semicolons joining two related clauses. A colon for explanation. A pair of dashes for extra information. An ellipsis for suspense. Consistent past tense throughout, with past perfect ('I had seen him guard') for the earlier time.
  • Word structure (spelling) 3/3: ambitious words spelt correctly (dangerously, attempt, inspected, curious, relieved). Possessives correct (rider's, somebody's). No homophone errors.

Telling feelings ('he was sad')Vocabulary and Creation stay in the middle bandsShow through body, actions and speech
Copying the extract's characters or sentencesThe writing is not your ownBorrow the theme only
Final checklist (tick before you hand in)
  • My story answers the theme and every bullet prompt.
  • I planned with G.C.S.P.P. and kept the same point of view throughout.
  • I have a clear opening hook, a build-up, a climax, a falling action and a resolution.
  • I used at least four different sentence starters and all four sentence types.
  • My dialogue is short, useful and correctly punctuated, with a new line for each speaker.
  • I showed feelings instead of only telling them, and used at least one strong simile, metaphor or personification.
  • The whole story is in the past tense (present only inside speech).
  • I proofread spelling, capital letters, apostrophes and homophones.

  • اقرأ الموضوع وحدد الفكرة الرئيسية (theme) وكل نقطة من النقاط (bullets) — كل نقطة لازم تظهر في قصتك كمشهد، مش كإجابة سؤال.
  • خطط في 5 دقائق بطريقة G.C.S.P.P.: النوع، الشخصيات، المكان، الراوي (I أو he/she)، والحبكة على شكل جبل القصة.
  • اكتب القصة كلها بالماضي، وما تغيرش الراوي في النص.
  • استخدم أنواع جمل مختلفة وبدايات جمل متنوعة، وعلامات ترقيم صحيحة خصوصًا في الحوار.
  • ما تكتبش 'he was scared' — وريني الخوف من خلال الجسم والأفعال.

17. Practice

  1. Plan this task with G.C.S.P.P.: 'You have read an extract about a boy who builds a model boat with his grandfather. Write a story about a character who learns something important from an older person.' Bullets: who the two characters are · what they do together · what the younger character learns.

    Show answer
    G: realistic fiction, warm and reflective. C: Ziad (12, impatient, wants everything quickly) and his grandmother, a retired seamstress who is calm and precise. S: her small flat in Alexandria, the sewing machine by the balcony, the sound of the sea and trams. P: first person (Ziad). P: whole story — Ziad must make a costume for the school play by tomorrow. He rushes and ruins the fabric. Grandmother teaches him to measure twice and cut once. Climax: the final seam at midnight. Ending: on stage, he notices the tiny perfect stitches and finally understands patience.

  2. Plan this task with G.C.S.P.P.: 'Write a story with the title The Wrong Turn.' Bullets: where the characters are going · what goes wrong · how they solve the problem.

    Show answer
    G: adventure with suspense. C: two friends, Karim (confident, refuses to ask for directions) and Adam (careful, carries the map). A farmer who helps. S: bicycles on desert roads near Fayoum, late afternoon turning to dusk. P: third person. P: the shortcut to the lake. A sign has fallen. They get lost as the sun sets, phone signal gone. Climax: a tyre bursts as darkness comes. The farmer's lantern and donkey cart. Ending: they reach the lake at night, see the stars and Karim finally admits he was wrong.

  3. Correct the punctuation: "where are you going she asked. I'm going home I said."

    Show answer
    "Where are you going?" she asked. (new line) "I'm going home," I said. — capital letter to start the speech, question mark inside the speech marks, comma before the closing speech marks, new line for the new speaker.

  4. Rewrite to SHOW, not tell: 'Omar was very nervous before the race.'

    Show answer
    Example: Omar retied his laces for the third time, his fingers clumsy and cold. Each time the starter lifted the whistle, his stomach dropped like a stone.

  5. Find and fix the tense errors: 'I opened the gate and walked into the garden. Suddenly a dog jumps out and barks at me.'

    Show answer
    '…Suddenly a dog jumped out and barked at me.' — the story is in the past tense, so 'jumps' and 'barks' must become 'jumped' and 'barked'.

  6. Rewrite with three different sentence starters: 'He ran to the station. He was late. He saw the train leave.'

    Show answer
    Example: Racing down the hill, he reached the station with his bag bouncing on his back (-ing starter). Exhausted and already ten minutes late, he pushed through the gate (-ed starter). As he stepped onto the platform, the train slid away without him (subordinate clause starter).

  7. The task theme is 'a dream come true'. Write a circular opening and ending pair.

    Show answer
    Opening: Every night for three years, Nour fell asleep listening to the commentary of other people's football matches. Ending: That night, for the first time, she fell asleep listening to a commentary that said her own name.

  8. In the sample story, why does the author use the ellipsis in 'It slipped. Once. Twice…'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers (any one, explained): to create suspense — the reader waits to find out whether the chain will slip a third time. To show a pause, as if Youssef is holding his breath. To show the information is incomplete — the attempts go on and we do not yet know the result.

  9. In the sample story, why does the author use the pair of dashes in 'a second key from his pocket — a small brass key that I had seen him guard for years — and placed it'?

    Show answer
    A pair of dashes always adds extra information: here it tells the reader that the key is precious and has been guarded for years, which shows how much trust the uncle is giving Youssef.

  10. In the sample story, why does the author use the colon in 'I remembered my uncle's voice: slowly, link by link.'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to introduce an explanation of what the uncle's voice had said. To add extra information (the exact advice). To give emphasis to the advice that will help Youssef solve the problem.

  11. In the sample story, why does the author use the semicolon in 'Uncle Hassan always did the chains; I only watched.'?

    Show answer
    To join two independent clauses that are closely related: both clauses are about who usually repairs the chains, so linking them in one sentence makes the reader see at once why Youssef panics.

  12. Why does the author use the simile 'the spanners gleamed like silver fish'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to help the reader picture the shiny, neatly arranged tools (imagery). To show Youssef's pride and happy mood at the start, as the tools look beautiful to him. To create a calm, positive atmosphere before the problem arrives, which makes the later panic stronger.

  13. Why does the author use the metaphor 'the toolbox, which suddenly seemed full of strangers'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show Youssef's panic without saying 'I was scared' (show, don't tell). To show that tools he sees every day suddenly feel unfamiliar because he does not know how to use them. To build tension at the moment the problem begins.

  14. Why does the author write 'Then the delivery rider arrived.' as a one-line paragraph?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to mark the turning point where the problem begins. To create suspense and make the reader pause. To change the pace suddenly after the calm, easy morning. To make the sentence stand out and grab attention.
