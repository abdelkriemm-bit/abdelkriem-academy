Checkpoint 8 · Reading: understanding texts

Vocabulary Skills: Context Clues, Word Parts, Idioms and Spelling

Work out unfamiliar words from context and word parts, answer 'find a word' and word-effect questions, and master phrasal verbs, British idioms, connotation and the spelling rules marked in your writing.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Why vocabulary matters in Checkpoint 8

Vocabulary is tested twice in every Checkpoint paper. In Section A Reading (25 marks), you will meet questions that ask you to find a word or phrase with a given meaning, to work out what a difficult word means from the sentence around it, and to explain the effect of a single word the writer chose. In Section B Writing (25 marks), two of the five marking categories are about words: Vocabulary and language (3 marks) rewards ambitious words chosen for effect, and Word structure (spelling) (3 marks) rewards accurate spelling of common and ambitious words, plurals, prefixes and suffixes, homophones and double letters.

So this page does two jobs. It teaches you how to understand unfamiliar words in a reading text, and how to use and spell strong words in your own writing. Work through it in order: context clues, word parts, synonyms, phrasal verbs, idioms, connotation, then spelling. Each section ends with a short check, and there is a full practice set at the bottom.

1. Context clues: working out a word you don't know

A context clue is a hint in the words, phrases or sentences around a difficult word. Good writers almost always leave one. In the exam you are not expected to know every word; you are expected to use the clues. There are several kinds of clue, and the best students learn to spot which kind they are looking at.

Type of clueHow it worksOriginal example (the clue is in bold)
Definition / restatementThe writer explains the word straight away, often after a comma, a dash or the words 'which means' / 'that is'.The village was isolated — cut off from every road and town — for most of the winter.
SynonymA word with a similar meaning appears nearby.The soup was bland. In fact, it was the most tasteless meal of the week.
Antonym / contrastA word with the opposite meaning appears, often after but, unlike, whereas, however.Unlike her talkative cousin, Mariam was taciturn at dinner.
ExampleExamples show what group the word belongs to.Citrus fruits, such as lemons, limes and oranges, grow well in warm climates.
Cause and effectThe word causes something, or is caused by something.The path was treacherous, so the guide made everyone hold the rope.
Inference (general sense)No single clue: the mood of the whole passage tells you.Omar beamed. He hugged his mother and danced round the kitchen. He was elated.
PunctuationCommas, brackets or dashes put an explanation right next to the word.They found a fossil (the hardened remains of an ancient creature) in the cliff.
Word partsBreak the word into prefix + root + suffix.un + forget + table = not able to be forgotten.
Word classWhere the word sits tells you if it is a noun, verb or adjective, which narrows the meaning.He gave a curt reply. ('curt' sits before a noun, so it is an adjective describing the reply.)

Signal words tell you what kind of clue is coming, even before you see it:

The 4-step method for any 'What does this word mean?' question
  • Step 1: Read the whole sentence, plus the sentence before and after.
  • Step 2: Decide the word class (noun, verb, adjective, adverb). Your answer must be the same word class.
  • Step 3: Find the clue: definition, synonym, contrast, example, cause/effect or the general mood. Decide if the word is positive or negative.
  • Step 4: Put your meaning back into the sentence in place of the word. If the sentence still makes sense, your answer is right.

Worked mini-example. Text: "The old lighthouse keeper was frugal. He mended his own coat for twenty years, ate the same plain bread every day and never once bought a newspaper." Question: What does 'frugal' mean? Answer: careful not to spend money / using very little. Why: the next sentence gives examples (mending the coat, plain bread, no newspaper), and 'frugal' is an adjective describing him, so the answer must describe a person.

2. 'Find a word or phrase that means…' questions

This is one of the most common 1-mark questions in Checkpoint reading. You are given a meaning (for example, 'to ruin' or 'hidden') and must find the word in a named paragraph that has that meaning. It sounds easy, but students lose the mark every year for small reasons.

  1. Look only in the paragraph named. If the question says paragraph 2, the answer is in paragraph 2.
  2. Match the word class. If the meaning is a verb ('to ruin'), the answer is a verb (e.g. 'spoil'). If it is an adjective ('hidden'), the answer is an adjective (e.g. 'concealed').
  3. Copy the word exactly as it appears in the text, with the same spelling. Do not change 'spoilt' into 'spoil'.
  4. Word means one word; phrase allows a few words. If the question says 'one word', write one word only. Writing extra words can cost you the mark even if the right word is inside.
  5. Test it. Swap the given meaning into the sentence. If it fits perfectly, you have found it.
  6. Do not write a sentence or an explanation. Just the word (you may put it in inverted commas).

Worked mini-example. Paragraph 2: "Behind a curtain of ivy, the gardeners discovered a concealed doorway. Nobody knew who had built it, or when." Question: Give one word from paragraph 2 that means 'hidden'. Answer: concealed. Why: 'concealed' is an adjective, like 'hidden', and 'a hidden doorway' makes perfect sense. 'Behind' and 'curtain' are linked to the idea but are not the right word class.

Common traps
  • Choosing a word from the wrong paragraph.
  • Giving a word that is connected to the idea but has a different meaning (e.g. 'dark' for 'hidden').
  • Writing two or three words when the question says 'one word'.
  • Changing the form of the word (giving 'conceal' when the text says 'concealed').

3. Word roots, prefixes and suffixes

Most long English words are built from smaller parts. If you know the parts, you can work out words you have never seen, and you can spell them correctly. A root is the part that carries the main meaning (many come from Latin or Greek). A prefix goes at the front and changes the meaning. A suffix goes at the end and usually changes the word class (for example, a verb into a noun).

How to use word parts in a reading question. Take 'irreplaceable'. Prefix ir- = not, root replace, suffix -able = can be done. So: something that cannot be replaced. In an effect question you could add: the writer shows the object is precious and unique. Take 'inaudible': in- = not, aud = hear, -ible = can be: cannot be heard.

Prefixes and spelling

Adding a prefix never changes the spelling of the root: dis + appear = disappear (one s), mis + spell = misspell (two s), un + necessary = unnecessary (two n), im + mature = immature (two m), il + legal = illegal (two l). If you know the parts, you will never miss a double letter.

4. Synonyms, antonyms and shades of meaning

A synonym has the same or nearly the same meaning (big / large / enormous). An antonym has the opposite meaning (brave / cowardly). In the exam, synonyms appear in 'find a word that means' questions and in 'explain in your own words' questions, where you must replace the text's words with your own.

Synonyms are rarely exact. They often differ in strength (shades of meaning). Strong writers choose the exact strength they need. Arrange words from weakest to strongest:

'In your own words' questions

When a question says 'in your own words', you must not copy the key words from the text. Replace each key word with a synonym. Text: 'The crowd was jubilant.' Own words: 'The people were extremely happy and celebrating.' You may still quote afterwards if the question also asks for evidence.

5. Phrasal verbs

A phrasal verb is a verb + one or two small words (particles such as up, out, off, on, down, into) that together make a new meaning. 'Give' means to hand over, but 'give up' means to stop trying. You cannot always guess the meaning from the separate words, which is why they appear in reading questions.

  • Two parts: break down, set off, look after.
  • Three parts: come up with (an idea), look forward to, run out of, put up with.
  • Separable: the object can go in the middle: turn the light off / turn off the light. With a pronoun it MUST go in the middle: turn it off (not 'turn off it').
  • Inseparable: the object always goes after: look after the baby (not 'look the baby after').
  • Many meanings: 'pick up' = lift / collect someone / learn something without lessons / improve.
  • Formal vs informal: phrasal verbs are informal. In a formal letter or report, a single formal verb is often better.

Using phrasal verbs in your writing

In a diary, blog, informal email or a story's dialogue, phrasal verbs make you sound natural ('We set off at dawn'). In a formal letter, report or argument, swap them for the formal verb ('The council should postpone the work' rather than 'put off the work'). Showing that you can choose the right register helps your Vocabulary and language mark.

6. Idioms (common British idioms)

An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning is different from the meaning of the individual words. 'It's raining cats and dogs' has nothing to do with animals. Idioms can appear in reading texts (especially fiction, diaries and informal articles), where you may be asked what they mean or why the writer used them. Writers use idioms to sound informal, chatty and friendly, or to show how a character speaks.

Idioms in exam writing

One well-chosen idiom in an informal piece (blog, diary, friendly email, dialogue) adds a natural voice. Do not fill your writing with idioms, and avoid them in formal letters and reports. Never use an idiom you are not sure about; a wrong idiom looks worse than none.

7. Denotation and connotation

Denotation is a word's dictionary meaning. Connotation is the feeling, picture or judgement the word carries with it. 'Home' and 'house' have the same denotation (a building where people live), but 'home' connotes warmth, family and safety. This is the heart of every 'explain the effect of the word…' question: the examiner wants the connotation, not just the dictionary meaning.

How to answer an 'effect of a word' question (1-2 marks). Use three moves: (1) quote the word. (2) say what it suggests (its connotation). (3) say what the reader thinks or feels as a result.

Worked mini-example. Text: "The traffic crawled along the coast road." Question: Explain why the writer uses the word 'crawled'. Answer: 'Crawled' suggests the cars were moving extremely slowly, like an insect or a baby, so the reader can picture a long, frustrating traffic jam. Why it scores: it gives the meaning (very slow), the connotation (like something creeping) and the effect (frustration). A weak answer such as 'it describes the traffic' scores nothing because it does not explain anything.

8. Spelling: the rules that save marks

Spelling is marked in every piece of writing under Word structure (spelling) (3 marks). The examiner looks at high-frequency words, plurals, prefixes and suffixes, homophones, double consonants and how well you spell ambitious words. A student who uses ambitious words and spells them correctly reaches the top band; a student who only uses simple words, or who misspells common words, stays lower. The rules below cover most mistakes at this level.

RuleWhat to doExamples

  • I can break a long word into prefix + root + suffix and explain its meaning.
  • I know 15 phrasal verbs and their formal equivalents.

Practice questions

Read each short text, then answer. Check your answers and read the explanations carefully.

  1. Text: "The castle had been abandoned for centuries. Its towers were crumbling, ivy covered every window and no voice had been heard inside for three hundred years." Give ONE word from the text that means 'left empty and no longer used'.

    Show answer
    abandoned. It is the past participle in 'had been abandoned', and 'the castle had been left empty and no longer used' fits perfectly. 'Crumbling' means breaking into pieces, which is a different meaning.

  2. Text: "Unlike her timid younger brother, Salma was audacious; she climbed the highest rock and leapt into the sea without a second thought." What does 'audacious' mean? Name the type of clue.

    Show answer
    Bold, daring, willing to take risks. Clue types: contrast ('Unlike her timid… brother') and example (climbing the highest rock and leaping into the sea).

  3. What does 'illegible' mean? Use the word parts to explain.

    Show answer
    Not able to be read. il- = not · leg = read (Latin root, as in 'legend') · -ible = able to be. So: cannot be read (e.g. very messy handwriting).

  4. Add the prefix: ___responsible, ___patient, ___legal, ___visible.

    Show answer
    irresponsible, impatient, illegal, invisible. (ir- before r, im- before p/m/b, il- before l, in- before most other letters.)

  5. Change the word class: 'decide' → noun · 'mystery' → adjective · 'careful' → adverb · 'modern' → verb.

    Show answer
    decision · mysterious · carefully · modernise.

  6. Text: "Before the storm the sea was placid. By midnight, it was hurling waves against the harbour wall." What does 'placid' mean, and which word signals the clue?

    Show answer
    Calm, still, peaceful. The time phrases 'Before the storm' and 'By midnight' signal a contrast: the sea changed from calm to violent ('hurling waves'), so 'placid' must mean the opposite of violent.

  7. Replace the phrasal verb with a formal single word: "The school has put off the trip because of the weather."

    Show answer
    The school has postponed the trip because of the weather.

  8. What does the phrasal verb mean in each sentence? (a) The car broke down on the motorway. (b) She broke down when she heard the news.

    Show answer
    (a) stopped working. (b) started crying / lost control of her feelings. The same phrasal verb can have different meanings, so always check the context.

  9. Text (from a blog): "Honestly, the new science lab cost an arm and a leg, but it's worth every penny." What does the idiom mean, and why might the blogger use it?

    Show answer
    It means very expensive. Possible reasons the writer uses an idiom: to sound chatty and informal, which suits a blog. To build a friendly, relaxed relationship with the reader. To exaggerate for emphasis (how much it cost). To add humour and make the writing lively and memorable. To show the writer's own personal voice.

  10. Which idiom fits? "I was ____ when I heard I'd won the scholarship." (a) under the weather (b) over the moon (c) on the fence

    Show answer
    (b) over the moon = extremely happy.

  11. Text: "The visitors swarmed into the museum the moment the doors opened." Explain the effect of the word 'swarmed'. (2 marks)

    Show answer
    'Swarmed' suggests the visitors moved like a mass of insects, in a large, uncontrolled crowd (1). This makes the reader feel the museum was suddenly crowded, noisy and chaotic (1).

  12. Sort into positive and negative connotation: thrifty, stingy, curious, nosy, slender, scrawny, determined, pig-headed.

    Show answer
    Positive: thrifty, curious, slender, determined. Negative: stingy, nosy, scrawny, pig-headed. Each positive word has a negative partner with the same basic meaning.

  13. Text: "Grandma's kitchen was a cosy little room." Why does the writer choose 'cosy' rather than 'small'?

    Show answer
    'Small' only gives the size (denotation). 'Cosy' suggests warmth, comfort and safety, so the reader feels the kitchen is a welcoming, loving place.

  14. Correct the spelling: begining, beleive, untill, definately, seperate, neccessary, embarassed, tommorow.

    Show answer
    beginning, believe, until, definitely, separate, necessary, embarrassed, tomorrow.

  15. Add the suffix and spell correctly: hope + ing, hope + ful, stop + ed, happy + ness, travel + ed (British), panic + ed, beauty + ful, argue + ment.

    Show answer
    hoping, hopeful, stopped, happiness, travelled, panicked, beautiful, argument.

  16. Choose the correct word: (a) Their / There / They're bringing their own lunch. (b) The dog wagged its / it's tail. (c) I need more practice / practise. (d) Please practice / practise every day.

    Show answer
    (a) They're (they are). (b) its (belonging to it). (c) practice (noun). (d) practise (verb).

  17. Write the plurals: knife, potato, city, monkey, child, box, piano, crisis.

    Show answer
    knives, potatoes, cities, monkeys, children, boxes, pianos, crises.

  18. Text: "The explorer's progress through the jungle was arduous: every metre had to be cut with a blade, and the heat sapped their strength." Give ONE word from the text that means 'very difficult and tiring'. Then explain how the colon helps you.

    Show answer
    arduous. The colon introduces an explanation of the word: cutting every metre and the heat show why the journey was so difficult and tiring.

  19. Rewrite with stronger vocabulary: "The man went into the big room and said he was very scared."

    Show answer
    Sample answer: The man edged into the vast hall and whispered that he was terrified. (Each tired word is replaced with a precise, stronger one.)

  20. In your own words, explain what this sentence means: "The volunteers were indefatigable."

    Show answer
    The volunteers never seemed to get tired; they kept working with endless energy. (in- = not · 'fatigue' = tiredness · -able = able to be: cannot be made tired.)
