Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Science Fiction and Dystopian Stories

How to write a gripping science fiction or dystopian story for Paper 2: the features, a paragraph-by-paragraph plan, phrases, vocabulary and a full-mark 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is science fiction and dystopian writing?

Science fiction (sci-fi) is a story built on a "what if" question about science or technology. What if robots ran our schools? What if we could live on Mars? What if a machine could read our thoughts? The story takes something we know today and pushes it into the future to see what happens to people. The future can be exciting, frightening or both.

Dystopian fiction is a type of science fiction that always shows a bad future: a society that looks perfect or orderly on the surface but is really unfair, controlling or damaged. The word comes from "dys" (bad) + "utopia" (a perfect place). A dystopian story nearly always follows one ordinary young person who starts to notice that something is wrong — and decides to question it.

Science fictionDystopian fiction
The future is…good, bad or mixedalways bleak, unfair or controlled
The main conflict is…a mission, an alien contact, a discovery that goes wrong, a machine that malfunctionsa person against a system (a Council, a regime, a company, rules)
Typical settingspaceship, space station, colony on another planet, laboratory, future citysealed city, walled zone, ruined city after a disaster, a place full of cameras
The big idea (theme)Is this technology good for people?Freedom, truth, courage, being an individual

Purpose: to entertain the reader and make them think. Audience: the examiner is your reader — write for an intelligent teenager or adult who wants to be gripped from the first line.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Paper 2 is the Fiction paper. It lasts 1 hour 10 minutes and is worth 50 marks: Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks).
  • Section B is one story task. It is usually linked by theme to the fiction extract you read in Section A, and it gives you bullet prompts (for example: who the character is, where the story happens, what goes wrong, how it ends).
  • The suggested time for Section B is about 35 minutes, and planning space is given. Planning is not marked, but it is where good stories are won.
  • The task will not always say "write a science fiction story". It gives you a theme. YOU decide that this genre is the best way to answer it — and then you must stay inside that genre from the first line to the last.
  • The same format is used in the Stage 8 Progression Test you sit at school, so everything on this page works for both.

Themes that suit sci-fi or dystopian stories (these are the kinds of theme a task may give you): a discovery, a secret, breaking a rule, freedom, a journey to an unknown place, a mistake with big consequences, technology going wrong, a character who is different, a choice that changes everything, a world that is not what it seems.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Your story is marked in five categories. Here is what the highest band looks like for a sci-fi or dystopian story (in our own words, not the mark scheme's).

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in this genre
Creation of texts5The story answers the task's theme and every bullet prompt. The futuristic world is believable and has clear rules. The reader cares about the main character and is kept interested to the last line.
Vocabulary and language3Precise, ambitious words that fit the genre (synthetic, sterile, surveillance, malfunction) and create a mood — not random "big words".
Structure of texts (Text structure)7A clear arc: hook → world → inciting incident → rising tension → climax → resolution. Paragraphs change for time, place, speaker or mood. The ending links back to the opening.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7A deliberate mix of simple, compound and complex sentences (short ones for shock, long ones for description). Speech punctuated correctly. A range of punctuation used accurately (colon, semicolon, dashes, ellipsis).
Word structure (spelling)3Ambitious words spelt correctly (atmosphere, surveillance, artificial, government, necessary). No slips with homophones (their/there, its/it's).
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. A very short answer (about 21-60 words) can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example, a diary or an article instead of a story), Creation of texts is capped at 1 and Structure of texts is capped too.
  • If the story drifts away from the task's theme, you lose Creation marks even if the writing is beautiful — keep the bullet prompts in front of you.

2. Features of science fiction and dystopian stories

Use this table as your toolkit. You do not need every feature — choose the 5-6 that fit your idea — but each one you use well pushes up your Creation and Vocabulary marks.

FeatureWhat it meansExample sentence
Futuristic technologyInventions that do not exist yet (or not like this): drones, androids, holo-screens, implantsA monitoring drone drifted past the window, its single red eye blinking every three seconds.
A world with clear rulesShow HOW the society works in one or two details, not a lectureEvery citizen received one hour of daylight-simulation a day. Mine started at 6:14.
An oppressive system (dystopian)A Council, Leader, company or law that controls peopleThe Council’s face smiled from every screen: OBEDIENCE IS SAFETY.
Loss of freedom or privacyCameras, curfews, rations, numbers instead of namesIn the Academy, nobody used names. I was Student 4471.
A bleak or sterile settingRuined, grey, polluted — or clean in a cold, lifeless wayThe corridor smelt of nothing at all, not even dust.
A questioning protagonistAn ordinary young person who notices something is wrongI had never counted the birds before. Now I could not stop.
An inciting discoveryA clue, message or object that breaks the lieInside the vent was something green.
Human versus technologyA machine that malfunctions, rebels or replaces peopleThe navigation system repeated the same word: ERROR. ERROR. ERROR.
Scientific detail (light touch)One or two believable terms make it convincingThe oxygen gauge dropped to 14 per cent.
Theme / messageThe story quietly says something about today’s worldFreedom, truth, the environment, courage

Language and layout features every story needs (these earn the Structure and Grammar marks):

FeatureHow to use itExample
A titleShort and intriguing — one image or idea from the storyThe Painted Sky / Student 4471 / The Last Signal
ParagraphsNew paragraph for a new time, place, speaker, or a change of moodA one-line paragraph for a shock: The lock clicked.
DialogueNew line for each speaker. Speech marks + punctuation inside. Reveals character and conflict“They’ve lied to us,” I said.
Show, don’t tellShow emotion through actions and the body (STEAL: Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks)My hands shook as I typed the code. (not: I was scared.)
Sensory imagerySight, sound, smell, touch, taste — especially in the settingSynthetic bread that tasted faintly of metal.
Figurative languageSimile, metaphor, personification — one strong image, not threeThe towers hunched over us, their windows like blank eyes.
Sentence varietyShort sentences for shock/tension. Long complex ones for descriptionNothing grew in the vents. Nothing could grow there unless…
Varied punctuationColon (list or explanation), semicolon (two linked sentences), dashes, ellipsisOne wrong number would trigger the alarm; one alarm would mean the Correction Wing.
First or third person, past tenseChoose one and keep itFirst person (I) feels personal and tense.
The golden rule of world-building

Do NOT start with a paragraph explaining the history of your future world. Drop the reader into a moment, then reveal the world bit by bit through small details: a rule on a wall, a strange food, a drone, a number instead of a name. Three or four well-chosen details are enough to make the examiner believe in your world.

3. How to structure your story

First, plan with G.C.S.P.P. (about 5 minutes):

  1. G — Genre: sci-fi or dystopian? Decide the "what if" in one line (What if the sky in our city was a screen?).
  2. C — Characters: 2-3 only. A protagonist with a name and one clear feeling, a helper (friend, grandparent), and an opposing force (the Council, a guard, a machine).
  3. S — Setting: one main place you can describe with the senses (a domed city, a space station, the 40th floor of a tower). If the task gives a setting, use it.
  4. P — Point of view: first person (I) is the easiest way to create tension. Use past tense. If you continue an extract, keep ITS point of view.
  5. P — Plot: whole story, opening, or continuation? Write 5-7 paragraph notes using the plan below.

Then follow this paragraph-by-paragraph plan. It is based on the 9-step sci-fi/dystopian arc from my writing book, squeezed into what you can write well in about 25 minutes of writing time.

ParagraphStageWhat to writeLength
1Hook — protagonist through dialogue or actionOpen with a line of speech or a strange detail that hints at the conflict. Show the main character’s personality through what they say or do.2-4 sentences
2The world (setting)Build the world with 3-4 sensory details and ONE rule of the society. Let the character react to it, so the setting shows their feelings.4-6 sentences
3Inciting incidentThe discovery, message, malfunction or warning that changes everything. Use a short sentence or one-line paragraph for impact.2-5 sentences
4Goal and motivation (+ the opposing force)What does the character decide to do, and why? Show the system or antagonist and the risk of acting (dialogue works well here).4-6 sentences
5Rising tensionOne or two obstacles: an alarm, a locked door, a drone, a friend who refuses to help. Shorter sentences. Time pressure.4-6 sentences
6ClimaxThe moment of choice or confrontation. Keep dialogue short and powerful. The character must ACT (not be rescued).3-6 sentences
7ResolutionWhat changed — in the world and inside the character. End with a short, memorable line that echoes the opening or hints at the future.2-4 sentences
Science fiction (not dystopian)? Swap the enemy

The same plan works for a non-dystopian sci-fi story. Just replace the oppressive system in paragraph 4 with the problem your story needs: an alien signal, a mission that goes wrong, a robot that malfunctions, an experiment with unexpected results, a meteor heading for the colony.

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). By about minute 17 you should have reached the inciting incident.
30–35Proofread: spelling of your ambitious words, full stops (no comma splices), speech punctuation, capital letters, tense consistency.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Adapt these — change the names, places and objects to fit your story. Use one or two per stage. Do not stack them.

Openings (hooks)

  • “Have you ever noticed that the [cameras / birds / clocks] never change?” [Name] whispered.
  • I never imagined that one ordinary [shift / lesson / mission] could decide the future of [the whole city / humanity].
  • Crash! The sound of shattering glass echoed through the empty [spacecraft / laboratory], and I knew we were not alone.
  • In [City name], nobody asked questions. That was the first rule, and the most important.
  • The countdown had already started when I opened my eyes.

Describing the futuristic setting

  • The air tasted of recycled oxygen, with a faint metallic bite.
  • Beyond the viewing window, the stars shimmered in an endless black sea.
  • A low hum pulsed through the corridors, steady as a mechanical heartbeat.
  • The towers hunched over the cracked streets, their dead neon signs flickering.
  • Everything in the Academy was white, spotless and silent — like the inside of a fridge.

Introducing characters (STEAL)

  • Captain [Name] never let fear show on her face, even when the odds were stacked against us.
  • Grandad’s oil-stained fingers knew every bolt in the station.
  • His eyes flicked towards the camera before he answered — once, quickly, like a warning.
  • She spoke in the flat, careful voice that everyone used when the drones were listening.

Building tension

  • “Did you hear that signal?” I asked, my voice cutting through the static.
  • The more I tried to warn them, the more they ignored me.
  • The countdown blinked red, each second flashing brighter, like a heartbeat racing towards disaster.
  • Boots clanged on the metal stairs. Close. Too close.
  • One wrong move would trigger the alarm; one alarm would mean the end.

Climax

  • This was it: now or never.
  • For a heartbeat, the whole station seemed to hold its breath.
  • I could have run. I could have pretended. Instead, I [stepped forward / pressed the switch / told the truth].
  • “You can’t change anything,” the guard sneered. I lifted my chin. “Watch me.”

Endings

  • Tomorrow, the Council would have to explain. Tonight, all I needed to do was breathe.
  • It wasn’t the end. It was the beginning of something new.
  • Sometimes the smallest choice — a switch flipped, a word spoken — can change a whole world.
  • Above us, for the first time in a hundred years, the real sky was waiting.
  • The screen went dark. For once, nobody was watching.
Word / phraseMeaning / use
dystopian (adj.)describing an imagined society that is unfair or frightening — The dystopian city ran on rules nobody questioned.
futuristic (adj.)looking like it belongs to the future — futuristic glass towers
synthetic (adj.)made by a chemical process, not natural — synthetic bread that tasted of metal
sterile (adj.)completely clean but cold and lifeless — the sterile white corridor
oppressive (adj.)cruelly controlling, heavy and hard to bear — the oppressive silence of the curfew
surveillance (n.)watching people closely, often secretly — surveillance cameras on every corner
regime (n.)a strict government — The regime rewrote history every year.
malfunction (v./n.)to stop working properly — The navigation system malfunctioned.
obsolete (adj.)no longer used because something newer exists — Human pilots were obsolete.
simulate (v.)to copy something real artificially — The dome simulated a blue sky.
barren (adj.)empty, unable to support life — a barren plain of cracked earth
colony (n.)a settlement in a new place — The Mars colony had stopped answering.
rebellion (n.)organised resistance against those in power — The rebellion began with one question.
atmosphere (n.)the gases around a planet, also the mood of a place — The atmosphere was thin and unbreathable.
humanity (n.)the human race as a whole — One switch stood between humanity and silence.

5. Strong sample answer

Dystopian story — The Painted Sky433 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: In the extract you have read, a young character begins to question the rules of the place where she lives. Write a story about a young person who discovers that something in their world is not what it seems. In your story you should: introduce the main character and the place where they live, describe what they discover and how they feel about it, and explain what they decide to do and what happens as a result.

“Have you ever noticed that the birds never change?” Grandad asked, tapping the glass with one oil-stained finger.

I looked up. Above Haven-9, the sky was its usual perfect blue, and the same flock of seven swallows was sweeping from east to west, exactly as it had every morning of my thirteen years. I had never counted them before. Now I could not stop.

Our city lived under a dome. The Council told us that the world outside had been poisoned a century ago; the air out there would burn our lungs in minutes. So we breathed filtered air, ate synthetic bread that tasted faintly of metal, and obeyed the rules printed on every wall: STAY INSIDE. STAY CALM. STAY GRATEFUL.

Grandad and I repaired the air filters on Level Four. It was dull, dusty work — until the Tuesday I opened Vent 112.

Inside, wedged between two rusted blades, was something green.

It was a seedling: two trembling leaves on a stem as thin as a thread. My heart thudded. Nothing grew in the vents. Nothing could grow there unless the air pushing through them — the air from outside — was clean.

“Put it back,” Grandad whispered, his face suddenly grey. “If the monitors see that, Yusuf…”

“They’ve lied to us,” I said. “All of us. Our whole lives.”

He did not answer, and his silence told me everything.

That night, while the monitoring drones hummed along the corridors, I crept to the maintenance hatch at the edge of the dome. My hands shook as I typed Grandad’s access code. One wrong number would trigger the alarm; one alarm would mean the Correction Wing, where people went in asking questions and came out asking none.

The lock clicked.

The hatch groaned open, and the wind arrived — not the stale, recycled breeze of the vents, but something wild and cold and alive. It smelt of wet earth and rain. Above me, there was no painted blue. There were stars: thousands of them, scattered carelessly across a sky that nobody had designed.

Behind me, red lights began to flash. Boots clanged on the metal stairs.

I could have run back. I could have pretended. Instead, I lifted my wristband, switched on its camera and turned it towards the sky, sending the picture to every screen in Haven-9.

When the guards finally reached the hatch, they did not grab me. They simply stood there, staring upwards, their helmets tilted back, as if they too were seeing the stars for the first time.

Tomorrow, the Council would have to explain. Tonight, all I needed to do was breathe.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — every bullet is answered: character and place (paragraphs 1-3), the discovery and his feelings (5-9), his decision and its result (10-16). The "what if" (the sky is a screen) is clear and the world has rules (STAY INSIDE. STAY CALM. STAY GRATEFUL.). The reader is kept guessing until the hatch opens.
  • Creation — viewpoint: first person keeps us inside Yusuf’s fear and courage. Grandad’s silence ("his silence told me everything") shows the relationship without explaining it.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — genre words chosen for effect: synthetic, filtered, monitoring drones, maintenance hatch, access code, Correction Wing. Contrasting word fields: the dome is stale, recycled, painted. Outside is wild, cold, alive, wet earth and rain.
  • Vocabulary — imagery: simile (a stem as thin as a thread), personification (the hatch groaned open, the wind arrived), and the contrast "a sky that nobody had designed".
  • Structure 7/7 — clear arc: hook through dialogue (1) → world and rules (3) → inciting incident as a one-line paragraph ("Inside… was something green.") → motivation and opposing force (7-10) → rising tension with risk (10-11) → climax/choice (14) → resolution (15-16).
  • Structure — cohesion: the opening question about the birds is answered by the real stars at the end. "Tomorrow… Tonight…" gives a strong, balanced final line. Short paragraphs ("The lock clicked.") control pace.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — sentence variety: short sentences for shock ("Now I could not stop."), complex sentences ("When the guards finally reached the hatch, they did not grab me."), a triple of short sentences on the wall.
  • Grammar & punctuation — range used accurately: colon for explanation/list ("It was a seedling: two trembling leaves…"), semicolon joining two linked sentences ("One wrong number would trigger the alarm; one alarm would mean…"), a pair of dashes for extra information ("— the air from outside —"), a single dash for emphasis ("dull, dusty work — until…"), ellipsis for an unfinished warning ("Yusuf…"), and correctly punctuated speech on new lines.
  • Spelling 3/3 — ambitious words spelt correctly: synthetic, maintenance, monitoring, carelessly, designed, thirteen, trembling. Apostrophes correct (They’ve, Grandad’s).

6. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
A whole paragraph of history: "In 2150 the war destroyed the world and the government built…"Slow opening. Telling not showing. Wastes words (Structure, Creation)Start in a moment. Reveal the history in one line later.
Too much technology, no peopleThe reader does not care. Creation stays in the middle bandOne main character with a feeling and a goal. Technology is the background.
Laser battles and explosionsAction without tension. Often confusing and unsuitableTension comes from risk, secrets and choices — a locked door, a camera, a countdown.
The character is rescued at the endA weak climax: the protagonist must actMake your character make the final choice.
"And then I woke up — it was all a dream."Cancels the whole story. Examiners mark this downEnd with a real consequence or a hint at the future.
Switching tense or person (I walk… I walked… he walked)Grammar and Structure marks dropPast tense, one point of view, all the way.
Comma splices: "The alarm rang, we ran."Grammar & punctuationFull stop, semicolon or a connective: The alarm rang, so we ran.
Using a semicolon before a list or with half a sentenceWrong punctuation is worse than noneA semicolon only joins two complete, related sentences. Use a colon for lists.
Checklist before you hand in
  • My story answers the theme and EVERY bullet prompt.
  • I opened with dialogue, action or a strange detail — not "One day…" or a history lesson.
  • My future world has one clear rule and 3-4 sensory details.
  • There is one clear inciting incident and a climax where my character acts.
  • I used short sentences for tension and at least two long complex sentences for description.
  • I used a colon, a semicolon and a dash correctly, and my speech is on new lines with correct punctuation.
  • My ending links back to the opening or hints at the future (no "it was a dream").
  • I checked the spelling of my ambitious words and stayed in past tense, one point of view.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: The extract you read describes a character who is made to follow strict rules. Write a story about a young person who breaks a rule for a good reason. You should describe the setting, the rule and why it exists, and what happens when the rule is broken. Write a short G.C.S.P.P. plan.

    Show answer
    G: dystopian. C: Malak (13, top student, nervous), her younger brother Ali (ill), Inspector Rahm (antagonist). S: the Grey Zone, a city where every citizen gets exactly one litre of clean water a day. Scanners at every tap. P: first person, past tense. Plot — P1 hook: "You’ve already had your litre," the tap said in its polite voice. P2 world: grey dust, ration cards, the rule (water is rationed so the Council can sell the rest to the Upper City). P3 incident: Ali has a fever and needs water. P4 decision: Malak uses her dead grandmother’s card. P5 tension: the scanner hesitates. Inspector Rahm appears. P6 climax: she tells the truth in front of the queue. Others hand over their cards. P7 resolution: Ali recovers. The queue becomes the start of something — "For the first time, nobody in the queue was looking at the floor."

  2. Practice task 2: Write a science fiction story called "The Signal". Plan it in 7 paragraph notes.

    Show answer
    1 Hook: "Captain, it’s repeating our names," Omar said. 2 Setting: research station orbiting Saturn. Recycled air, the hum of the reactor, six crew. 3 Incident: the signal comes from the empty storage module. 4 Goal: Omar (youngest crew member) volunteers to investigate. The captain refuses — then agrees. 5 Tension: lights fail, oxygen gauge drops, the door seals behind him. 6 Climax: he finds an old probe launched from Earth fifty years ago, its message recorded by children — including his grandfather’s name. 7 Resolution: the crew send a reply home. "Some signals take fifty years to arrive. Ours would be ready." (Non-dystopian sci-fi: the conflict is a mystery, not a regime.)

  3. Improve this opening so it earns higher marks: "In the year 3000 the world was very bad. There was a bad government and people were sad. My name is Sam and I lived there."

    Show answer
    Example: "Smile," the camera said. Sam smiled. Everyone in Sector 9 smiled when the cameras asked; the ones who didn’t were never seen at school again. — Why it is better: it starts in a moment, shows the bad government through one rule instead of telling us, uses a short-sentence rhythm and creates a question in the reader’s mind.

  4. Look at the sample story. Why does the writer use the semicolon in "One wrong number would trigger the alarm; one alarm would mean the Correction Wing"? Give every possible answer.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to join two independent clauses (two complete sentences) that are closely related. To show that the second idea follows directly from the first (number → alarm → punishment), like a chain. To keep the two ideas in one breath so the danger builds quickly and the tension rises.

  5. Why does the writer use the ellipsis in “If the monitors see that, Yusuf…” and the one-line paragraph "The lock clicked."?

    Show answer
    Ellipsis: to show incomplete information — Grandad does not finish because the consequence is too frightening to say. To create suspense and make the reader imagine what would happen. To show a pause that reveals his fear. One-line paragraph: to make the moment stand out and grab attention. To slow the pace and build suspense. To mark a turning point — there is no going back now.

  6. Which sentence uses the semicolon correctly? (a) The drone had three things; a camera, a speaker and a light. (b) The drone stopped above us; its red eye blinked twice. (c) Because the drone stopped; we froze.

    Show answer
    (b). A semicolon joins two complete, closely related sentences. (a) needs a colon before a list. (c) has a subordinate clause, not a full sentence, before the semicolon — it needs a comma.
