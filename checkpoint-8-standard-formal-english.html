Checkpoint 8 · Grammar in Use

Standard and Formal English

The difference between Standard and non-standard English and between formal and informal register, the features of each, formal vocabulary, and how Checkpoint asks you to identify them, with practice and answers.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. Two different ideas: standard and formal

Students often mix up these two ideas. They are not the same thing.

  • Standard English is about correctness: English that follows the accepted rules of grammar and vocabulary. It is used in exams, books, news and formal writing.
  • Formal English is about register (how formal or informal a text is): the level of politeness, distance and seriousness chosen for the reader and purpose.

A text can be informal but still Standard: I'm so excited about the trip! Can't wait to see you. (chatty, but no grammar errors). A sentence like We was gonna go is non-standard (wrong grammar for written English). In an exam, your writing must always be Standard English, and its register must match the task.

Key words
  • Standard English: English that follows the accepted rules of grammar, used in exams and formal writing.
  • Non-standard English: English that breaks those rules, common in everyday speech, dialects and text messages.
  • Register: how formal or informal a text is.
  • Contraction: two words shortened into one with an apostrophe (don't, it's, we'll).
  • Colloquial / slang: very informal, everyday spoken words (loads of, mates, cool).

Why a writer uses non-standard English on purpose: in fiction, in dialogue, to make a character sound realistic, to show the character's age, background, region or personality, to create a casual, informal voice and sometimes for humour. In your own writing, use Standard English everywhere except inside a character's speech.

3. Formal and informal language

FeatureFormalInformal
ContractionsAvoided: do not, it isUsed: don't, it's
VocabularyPrecise, often longer words: obtain, assist, purchaseEveryday words, slang, phrasal verbs: get, help out, buy
PersonOften third person, impersonal It is believed that...First and second person: I, you, we
VoicePassive is common: The decision was made...Mostly active
SentencesLonger, complex, carefully linkedShorter · fragments · questions · exclamations
PunctuationFew exclamation marksExclamation marks, dashes, brackets for chatty asides
TonePolite, serious, objectiveFriendly, chatty, personal, humorous
Typical textsReports, formal letters, news, information texts, speeches to officialsBlogs, diaries, emails and letters to friends, some magazine articles

Formality is a scale. A letter to a head teacher is formal, a school magazine article is semi-formal (friendly but still careful), and a message to a friend is informal. Choose the level that suits your reader and purpose.

5. Common mistakes

ProblemExampleBetter
Contractions in a formal letterI'm writing because the park isn't safe.I am writing because the park is not safe.
Slang in a reportLoads of kids reckon the canteen food is rubbish.Many students believe the quality of the canteen food is poor.
Mixing registersDear Sir, hope you're good! I would like to complain...Dear Sir, I am writing to complain...
Too stiff for a friendDear Ali, I wish to inform you that I commenced my holiday.Hi Ali, Guess what? My holiday has finally started!
Non-standard grammarWe was told the trip is cancelled.We were told that the trip had been cancelled.

6. How Checkpoint asks about register

Question style: Identify two features that make this text formal. Name the feature and quote an example for each: 1. No contractions, e.g. 'it is not possible'. 2. The passive voice, e.g. 'visitors are requested to...'.

Worked example. Visitors are requested to remain on the marked paths at all times. Failure to do so may result in damage to rare plant species. Two formal features: (1) passive voice, Visitors are requested. (2) Formal, precise vocabulary, Failure to do so may result in. Also (3) no contractions and no direct address to you.

You may also be asked why a character speaks in non-standard English, or why a writer uses an informal, chatty tone (for example, in a blog: to build a friendly relationship with the reader and make the text feel personal).

In your writing, match the register to the task: formal for letters to officials, reports, news reports and speeches to adults, informal (but still Standard English) for diaries, blogs and emails to friends. Keep the register consistent from the first line to the last.

7. Practice

  1. Rewrite in Standard English: Me and him seen the accident but we didn't say nothing.

    Show answer
    He and I saw the accident, but we didn't say anything.

  2. In a story, a character says: "Ain't nobody gonna stop me now!" Why has the writer used non-standard English? (1 mark)

    Show answer
    To make the character's speech sound realistic and show their confident, casual personality (and perhaps their background).

  3. Identify two features that make this text formal: Residents are advised that the swimming pool will be closed for maintenance. We apologise for any inconvenience caused.

    Show answer
    Any two: passive voice (Residents are advised, will be closed). Formal vocabulary (maintenance, inconvenience). No contractions (will be, not 'll). Polite, impersonal tone (We apologise for any inconvenience caused).

  4. Rewrite in a formal register: Hey, we're gonna need loads of help with the fair, so grab your mates and come along!

    Show answer
    We will require a large number of volunteers for the fair. Students are invited to attend with their friends.

  5. Is this sentence formal or informal, and is it Standard English? Honestly, the new café is amazing – you've got to try the mango cake!

    Show answer
    Informal (contraction, Honestly, exclamation mark, direct address to you), but it is Standard English: the grammar is correct.

  6. Identify two features that make this blog extract informal: So, here's the thing. I tried to bake bread last week (big mistake!) and it came out like a rock.

    Show answer
    Any two: contractions (here's), chatty opener (So, here's the thing), brackets for a humorous aside (big mistake!), exclamation mark, first person and a casual simile (like a rock).

  7. Replace the informal words with formal ones: We need to find out why loads of students don't get enough sleep.

    Show answer
    We need to discover / investigate why numerous / many students do not get sufficient sleep.

  8. Correct the non-standard forms: You should of seen it. Them birds was huge.

    Show answer
    You should have seen it. Those birds were huge.
Remember

Standard = correct grammar (always in your writing, except in dialogue). Formal = no contractions, precise words, passive, impersonal. Match the register to the reader and keep it consistent.
