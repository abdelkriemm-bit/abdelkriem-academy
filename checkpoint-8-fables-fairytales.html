Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Fables and Fairy Tales

How to write a fable or fairy tale for Paper 2: the features, the rule of three, a six-stage plan, phrases, vocabulary and a full-mark 350+ word sample with a clear moral.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What are fables and fairy tales?

Fables and fairy tales are short, timeless stories that use magical or symbolic elements — talking animals, enchanted objects, spells, wise strangers — to teach a clear lesson. The characters are simple and symbolic (the hero, the villain, the trickster, the helper) rather than deeply complex, and the story turns on a clear struggle between good and bad behaviour that ends in a reward, a punishment or a change.

FableFairy tale
LengthVery short — often one sceneLonger — several scenes or trials
Main charactersAnimals (or objects) that talk and act like people: the fox, the tortoise, the crowPeople (a poor boy, a princess, two brothers) plus magical beings: a fairy, a talking bird, a witch, a giant
MagicLittle or none — talking animals are enoughMagic is central: spells, wishes, enchanted objects, transformations
The lessonAlways stated at the end as a moralUsually shown through the ending (good is rewarded, evil is punished). A moral may be stated
Opening“A hungry fox once…”“Once upon a time…”
Example ideaA boastful hare loses a race to a patient tortoise.A kind brother helps a magical stork and is rewarded. A greedy brother is not.

Purpose: to entertain and to teach a lesson about how to behave. Audience: traditionally children and families — but in the exam, the examiner, who wants to see that you control the genre’s features and language. Point of view: almost always third person (he/she/they), past tense, told by a storyteller’s voice.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Any story you write goes in Paper 2 (Fiction), Section B Writing — 25 marks. The paper lasts 1 hour 10 minutes. About 35 minutes is suggested for the writing, and planning space is given.
  • Fables and fairy tales have not been set as a named genre in the recent Year 8 / Checkpoint papers we have reviewed. The task gives a theme linked to the reading extract, usually with bullet prompts.
  • Some themes fit this genre very well: a test of character, a lesson learned the hard way, greed or kindness, a wish, a reward, a promise. If the extract itself is a traditional tale, or the theme is about a lesson, a fable or fairy tale is a clever choice.
  • If the task asks for a realistic story (a real place, real people), do NOT write a fairy tale. Wrong text type caps your Creation mark at 1.

Themes that suit fables and fairy tales: honesty, kindness, greed, pride, patience, jealousy, bravery, keeping a promise, being tested, appearances can be deceiving, sharing, cleverness against strength.

Fable / fairy tale or myth / legend?

If your idea has gods, a heroic quest or explains how something in nature began (why the sea is salty), use the Myths and Legends pack. If it is a short story with a clear lesson about behaviour, use this pack.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in a fable or fairy tale
Creation of texts5Every bullet prompt is answered. The magical world has clear rules. The characters are simple but vivid. The lesson grows naturally from the events. The storyteller’s voice is confident from first line to moral.
Vocabulary and language3Genre words chosen for effect (enchanted, cunning, humble, benevolent, envious) and traditional imagery (as hard as a millstone, feathers like polished silver).
Structure of texts (Text structure)7Traditional opening → characters → problem → three trials → climax → resolution and moral. Repetition gives rhythm. Paragraphs are clear. The ending answers the opening.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7Varied sentences (short for the turning point, long and balanced for storytelling). Speech punctuated correctly. Colon, semicolon, dashes and ellipsis used accurately.
Word structure (spelling)3Genre words spelt correctly (mischievous, benevolent, cunning, generous, jealous, transformation).
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. About 21-60 words can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • Wrong text type (a fairy tale when the task needs realism, or a diary instead of a story) caps Creation at 1 and Structure too.
  • Retelling a famous tale (Cinderella, The Tortoise and the Hare) is risky: it shows no original idea. Borrow the FEATURES, not the plot.

2. Features of fables and fairy tales

FeatureWhat it meansExample sentence
Traditional openingSignals the genre in the first lineOnce upon a time, in a village beside a slow brown river…
Timeless settingForest, village, castle, river, desert — no phones, no cars, no datesBeyond the seven hills lay a kingdom where no stranger had set foot for a hundred years.
Magical or supernatural elementsTalking animals, enchanted objects, spells, wishes, disguised helpersThe old coat began to glow.
Simple, symbolic charactersEach character stands for ONE quality: kindness, greed, pride, cunningKaram counted his sacks every evening, twice.
Good against badA clear struggle between right and wrong behaviourthe generous brother vs the greedy brother
The rule of threeThree trials, three wishes, three visitors, three brothers — with a pattern of repetitionOn the first morning… On the second morning… On the third morning…
PersonificationAnimals or objects speak and act like humans“Will you share your bread?” asked the stork.
A test or trialA character is tested, often without knowing itThe stork was the Keeper of the River in disguise.
Transformation or consequenceCharacters are rewarded, punished or changedNadim receives the spring. Karam receives only thistles.
A clear moralThe lesson, stated at the end or made obvious by the endingMoral: Kindness that expects a reward is not kindness at all.

Language features: a storyteller’s voice (third person, past tense). Traditional similes (as tall as a doorway, as quiet as snow). Balanced sentences that compare the good and bad characters (“The neighbours bought their bread from Karam, but they brought their troubles to Nadim.”). Repetition with small changes. Short, simple dialogue.

Keep the characters simple — but not boring

Fable characters do not need long backstories. Give each one a single quality and ONE vivid detail that proves it: Karam locks his door even when he is inside (greed and mistrust). Nadim’s laugh makes the dogs wag their tails (warmth). One perfect detail is worth a paragraph of description.

3. How to structure a fable or fairy tale

Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (about 5 minutes):

  1. G — Genre: fable (animals, short, stated moral) or fairy tale (people + magic, trials). Write the moral FIRST, in one line — the whole story will prove it.
  2. C — Characters: a hero (one good quality), an opposite (one bad quality) and a magical helper or tester.
  3. S — Setting: one timeless place: a village, a forest, a riverbank, a palace kitchen.
  4. P — Point of view: third person, past tense, storyteller voice.
  5. P — Plot: six stages, with three trials in the middle.
StageWhat to writeLength
1. Opening hook and settingA traditional opening phrase and a timeless, magical or simple setting.1 paragraph
2. Introduce the charactersPresent the hero and the opposite character, one quality and one vivid detail each. A balanced sentence can compare them.1 paragraph
3. The problemA challenge, temptation or unfairness: a drought, a curse, a contest, a stranger asking for help. The magical element enters.1-2 paragraphs
4. Rising action: the three trialsThe character is tested three times. Repeat the pattern (On the first… second… third…) and make each trial harder.2-4 short paragraphs
5. ClimaxThe hardest test, a defining choice or the moment the magic is revealed: the spell breaks, the disguise falls, the reward appears.1-2 paragraphs
6. Resolution and moralBalance is restored: good is rewarded, bad is punished or changed. State the moral clearly, or end with a line that makes it obvious.1-2 paragraphs + moral
MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph. Write the moral first, then plan the six stages and the three trials.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). Keep paragraphs short and rhythmical. By about minute 15 you should be in the trials.
30–35Proofread: speech punctuation, tense, capital letters for magical names (the Keeper of the River), spelling.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Openings

  • Once upon a time, when [magic still lingered in the land / the animals could still speak]…
  • Long ago, in a [village / kingdom] beside [place], there lived [characters].
  • In a faraway land, where [magical detail], there was once a [humble / proud] [role].
  • A [hungry / cunning / boastful] [animal] once [action].

Describing the setting

  • Beyond the [mountains / sea] lay a [kingdom / village] where no stranger had set foot for [a hundred years].
  • A [tiny cottage / crumbling tower] stood at the edge of [place], its [detail] like [magical comparison].
  • The [forest / river / palace] glowed with [light], as if it were keeping a secret.

Introducing the characters

  • The [clever / cunning] [animal], known to all the [group], always found a way to [trick / outwit] [others].
  • A humble [role] lived with [family], never suspecting what [wonders / dangers] the [place] held.
  • [Name] was as [adjective] as [traditional comparison], but [his/her] heart was [opposite].

The problem and the trials

  • One day, [problem], and no one knew why.
  • Tempted by [greed / pride / curiosity], [character] ignored the warning and [action].
  • On the first [day / night], … On the second [day / night], … On the third [day / night], …
  • Three times [character] tried to [goal], and three times [rival] [outwitted / stopped] [him/her].

Climax

  • At once, the [object] began to [glow / tremble / sing], and [transformation].
  • With one final [word / cry], the spell was broken, and [consequence].
  • At last, [character] realised that [his/her] [greed / pride / boasting] had led to [downfall].

Resolution and moral

  • From that day on, [character] [new behaviour].
  • And so, the people of [place] remembered: [moral as timeless wisdom].
  • The [greedy / proud / foolish] [character] learned that [lesson].
  • Moral: [one sentence that sums up the lesson].
Word / phraseMeaning / use
enchanted (adj.)filled with magic — The enchanted forest sparkled under the moonlight.
bewitched (adj.)placed under a spell — The bewitched prince could only speak at night.
cunning (adj.)clever in a tricky or deceitful way — The cunning fox flattered the crow.
mischievous (adj.)playfully naughty — The mischievous monkey hid the merchant’s slippers.
benevolent (adj.)kind and generous — A benevolent stranger granted her three wishes.
humble (adj.)modest, not proud, of low rank — a humble fisherman
virtuous (adj.)morally good — The virtuous girl told the truth, whatever it cost.
envious (adj.)wanting what someone else has — envy burned inside him like a fever
boastful (adj.)talking too proudly about yourself — The boastful hare had never lost a race.
deceive (v.)to make someone believe something untrue — The fox deceived the crow.
outwit (v.)to defeat someone by being cleverer — The tortoise outwitted the hare.
disguise (n./v.)clothes or a shape that hide who you really are — The fairy came in the disguise of a beggar.
transformation (n.)a complete change, often magical — the frog’s transformation into a prince
moral (n.)the lesson a story teaches — The moral is that patience wins.
inevitable (adj.)certain to happen — Once his lie was discovered, his downfall was inevitable.

5. Strong sample answer

Fairy tale with a moral — The Silver Stork542 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: In the extract you have read, a character is tested without knowing it. Write a story in which a character’s true nature is tested. In your story you should: introduce the character or characters and the place where they live, describe the test and how they respond, and show what happens to them as a result.

Once upon a time, in a village beside a slow brown river, there lived two brothers who shared a single field and nothing else.

Karam, the elder, was as tall as a doorway and as hard as a millstone. He counted his sacks of grain every evening, twice, and he locked his door even when he was inside. Nadim, the younger, was small and thin, with patched sleeves and a laugh that made the village dogs wag their tails. The neighbours bought their bread from Karam, but they brought their troubles to Nadim.

One summer, the rain forgot the village. The river shrank to a muddy thread, the wheat bowed its yellow head, and the people grew quiet and hungry.

On the first morning of the drought, a stork with feathers like polished silver limped into the field. One wing hung crooked at its side.

“Kind farmers,” it said in a voice as thin as reed music, “I have walked for nine days. Will you share your bread?”

Karam snorted. “Birds that cannot fly are not my problem.” He turned his back and went on counting.

Nadim, however, broke his last loaf in two and placed the larger half in front of the stork.

On the second morning, the stork returned. “Will you share your water?” it asked. Karam slammed his gate. Nadim tipped the last of his jar into his cupped hands and let the bird drink every drop.

On the third morning, an icy wind came down from the hills, and the stork stood shivering in the dust. “Will you share your warmth?” Karam pretended not to hear. Nadim took off his only coat — patched, faded and full of holes — and wrapped it gently around the broken wing.

At once, the old coat began to glow. The crooked wing straightened, the silver feathers blazed like moonlight on water, and the stork rose high above the two brothers.

“I am the Keeper of the River,” it said, “and I have tested every village from the mountains to the sea. In this one, only one man passed.” It dropped a single white seed into Nadim’s palm. “Plant this where you stand.”

By evening, a spring of clear, cold water was bubbling from the ground where the seed had fallen. Nadim did not build a wall around it; instead, he called the whole village to drink.

Karam watched from his window, and envy burned inside him like a fever. If one bird could make his foolish brother rich, he thought, then another could make him richer. So he set out to find an injured bird of his own.

At last he found a crow tangled in a fisherman’s net. He cut it free, fed it bread, gave it water and wrapped it in his finest blanket — and all the while he muttered, “Now, where is my reward? Where is my spring?”

The crow ate every crumb, cawed once and flew away.

Karam waited in that spot for seven days. Nothing grew there except thistles…

In the end, it was Nadim who carried water to his brother’s door each morning, and slowly — very slowly — Karam learned to open it.

Moral: Kindness that expects a reward is not kindness at all; it is only trade.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — all three bullets answered: the characters and their village (paragraphs 1-3). The test and how each brother responds (4-9). The results for both brothers (10-17). The idea is original (not a retold famous tale), and the moral grows naturally from the events, so the reader feels it before it is stated.
  • Creation — genre control: traditional opening (“Once upon a time”), timeless setting, a talking animal, a disguised tester (the Keeper of the River), a magical reward (the seed and the spring) and a stated moral. The storyteller’s voice never slips into modern language.
  • Simple, symbolic characters: Karam = greed (he counts his sacks twice and locks his door even when he is inside). Nadim = kindness (patched sleeves, the laugh that makes dogs wag their tails). The balanced sentence “The neighbours bought their bread from Karam, but they brought their troubles to Nadim” sums up both.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — genre imagery: traditional similes (as tall as a doorway, as hard as a millstone, as thin as reed music, like polished silver, like moonlight on water, like a fever). Personification (the rain forgot the village, the wheat bowed its yellow head). Precise verbs (limped, snorted, slammed, blazed, muttered).
  • Structure 7/7 — six clear stages: opening (1) → characters (2) → problem: the drought (3) → three trials with the rule of three (On the first / second / third morning, 4-9) → climax: the transformation (10-11) → resolution for both brothers (12-17) and moral (18). The second half mirrors the first: Karam tries to copy the three gifts (bread, water, blanket) but for the wrong reason.
  • Structure — pace: a one-line paragraph (“The crow ate every crumb, cawed once and flew away.”) gives the failure its impact. The final paragraph softens the ending, so even the greedy brother can change — a mature touch.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — varied sentences: short (“Karam snorted.”), compound and complex (“If one bird could make his foolish brother rich, he thought, then another could make him richer.”), a long list-like sentence for the drought. Punctuation as taught: a pair of dashes for extra information (“— patched, faded and full of holes —”, “— very slowly —”). A single dash for emphasis (“— and all the while he muttered”). A semicolon joining two related sentences (“Nadim did not build a wall around it; instead, he called the whole village to drink.” and in the moral). A colon to introduce the moral as an explanation. An ellipsis for a pause that lets the reader feel Karam’s disappointment (“except thistles…”). Speech punctuated correctly.
  • Spelling 3/3 — correct spelling of ambitious words: millstone, drought, shivering, envy, thistles, muttered, injured, fisherman’s. Apostrophes correct (Nadim’s, fisherman’s).

6. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
Retelling a famous taleNo original idea. Creation stays lowInvent your own characters and test. Keep only the features.
Too many charactersThe simple, symbolic shape is lostTwo or three: hero, opposite, magical helper/tester.
Modern details (phones, cars, “OK”, “cool”)Breaks the timeless setting and the storyteller’s voiceKeep the world and the language old and simple.
Magic that solves everything with no testNo tension. The character does nothingMake the reward or punishment depend on the character’s CHOICES.
A moral that does not match the storyCreation and Structure marks dropWrite the moral first and build every trial to prove it.
Only two trials, or three identical trialsWeak rhythm. No build-upThree trials, each a little harder or more costly than the last.
Mixing up a semicolon and a colon before the moralGrammar & punctuation“Moral:” uses a colon. A semicolon only joins two complete, related sentences.
Checklist before you hand in
  • My story answers the theme and EVERY bullet prompt.
  • I started with a traditional opening and a timeless setting.
  • Each character stands for one clear quality, proved by one vivid detail.
  • There is magic or a talking animal, and a test or trial.
  • I used the rule of three with repetition.
  • Good behaviour is rewarded and bad behaviour has a consequence.
  • My moral is clear and matches the story.
  • I used a colon, a semicolon and a dash correctly. Speech is punctuated correctly.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: In the extract you have read, a character makes a promise. Write a story about a promise that is kept or broken. You should: introduce the characters and the place, describe the promise and the challenge of keeping it, and show what happens as a result. Plan it as a fable or fairy tale.

    Show answer
    Moral first: A promise is only worth what you are willing to give to keep it. G: fairy tale. C: Salim, a young ferryman (honest, poor). A merchant (proud). The River Spirit in the shape of an old woman. S: a wide river with one small ferry. P: third person, past. Plot — 1 opening: Long ago, on a river too wide to swim… 2 characters: Salim promised his dying father to carry anyone across for free. 3 problem: a rich merchant offers gold if Salim refuses passengers and carries only his goods. 4 three trials: an old woman, a mother with a baby, a lost child arrive — each time Salim chooses the promise over the gold. 5 climax: a storm. The merchant’s overloaded boat is swept onto a sandbank. The old woman reveals herself as the River Spirit and calms the water for Salim’s ferry. 6 resolution: the river never floods his ferry again. Moral stated.

  2. Practice task 2: Write a short fable (animals only) that teaches: “Patience is stronger than force.” Give the characters, the three trials and the ending in note form.

    Show answer
    Characters: a strong, impatient bull and a small, patient ant. Setting: a dry field by a well whose bucket is stuck. Trials: (1) the bull charges the well and cracks his horn. (2) he pulls the rope and snaps it. (3) he kicks the wall and the bucket falls further. Meanwhile, the ant carries one grain of sand at a time to fill a gap and free the bucket from the stones. Ending: the bucket rises. The bull drinks humbly. Moral: Patience is stronger than force.

  3. Turn this telling sentence into a fable-style description with ONE vivid detail: “The king was very greedy.”

    Show answer
    Example: The king had a hundred locked chests, and every night he unlocked each one to count his gold — then locked it again and lay awake in case someone had heard him counting. (One habit proves the greed, and the dash adds emphasis.)

  4. Which ending is stronger, and why? (a) “And then they all lived happily ever after. The end.” (b) “From that day on, the fox never flattered anyone again — for he had learned that sweet words can cost a hungry stomach its dinner.”

    Show answer
    (b). It shows a change in the character, links to the events (the trick and its result) and states the lesson in a memorable way. (a) is a cliché that tells us nothing about what was learned.

  5. Correct the punctuation: “Moral; greed always ends in loss.”

    Show answer
    Moral: Greed always ends in loss. — A colon introduces the explanation (the moral). A semicolon can only join two complete, related sentences.
