Central Ideas and Details

Find the main idea of any text, avoid too-narrow and too-broad traps, answer detail questions exactly, and read older prose and poetry with confidence.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Central Ideas and Details questions check whether you understood what a text is mainly about and what it actually says. You read one short passage (25–150 words) from literature, history/social studies, the humanities or science, and answer one four-option question. Literature passages include prose fiction, drama and poems, and some are written in older English.

The two main forms (in our own words):

  • Main idea: "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?" (For literature: "…the main idea of the poem" or "…what happens in the text".)
  • Detail: "According to the text, what is true about…?", "According to the text, why did the researchers…?", "Based on the text, what is [the character's] main concern?"

This skill belongs to the Information and Ideas domain, together with Command of Evidence (textual and quantitative) and Inferences. Information and Ideas is about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (12–14 questions across both modules).

The big idea

The main idea is the one point that the WHOLE text supports. A detail answer must be stated in the text (perhaps in different words). In both cases, the right answer never goes beyond what is written.

The method for main-idea questions

  1. Find the topic: who or what is the text about? (sea otters; Mr. Ashby; the river)
  2. Find the point: what does the text say about that topic overall? Look for the turn (but, yet, however), the conclusion (thus, so, suggests that, researchers conclude) or, in fiction, the change or realisation.
  3. Write a one-line prediction: topic + point. "Sea otters protect kelp forests by eating urchins."
  4. Test each choice with the Goldilocks check: not too narrow (only one detail), not too broad (bigger than the text), not distorted (mixes up cause and effect, or who did what), not extreme.
  5. Ask: do the other sentences support it? If most sentences in the text are examples or evidence for the choice, it is the main idea.

The method for detail questions

  1. Find the key word in the question ("why… native shrubs?") and locate it in the text.
  2. Read that sentence and one either side. The answer is almost always right there.
  3. Match meaning, not words. The right choice usually paraphrases the text. Wrong choices often copy words from the wrong sentence.
  4. Do not infer. If you have to say "probably" or "maybe", it is not a detail answer.

Reading older literary language

Some SAT literature passages come from older novels, plays and poems, often from the 1800s. The sentences can be long, the word order unusual, and some words old-fashioned. The ideas, however, are usually simple: a character who is proud, mean, kind or foolish; a speaker who is sad, grateful or thoughtful. Use these tools.

  • Find the main subject and verb. Long sentences are often one simple statement plus many extra phrases. Example: "Mr. Ashby, who had never in his life been contradicted by anyone of consequence, believed himself wise." → Mr. Ashby believed he was wise.
  • Use semicolons and "yet" as signposts. A semicolon joins two complete, related ideas; "yet" or "but" after a list of details often carries the point.
  • Watch for irony. Older narrators often praise a character in a way that actually mocks them ("his generosity extended to everything except his purse" = he is mean with money).
  • Read poems by sentence, not by line. Follow the punctuation. A sentence may run over three or four lines.
  • Ask four questions about a poem: Who is speaking? To whom or what (an "O river…" address)? About what situation? Is there a shift (But, Yet, Now)? The main idea usually sits in the shift or the last lines.
  • Restore normal word order. Poets reorder words for rhythm: "Deep in the wood a voice I heard" = I heard a voice deep in the wood.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Main idea (science)

Sea otters are often described simply as playful animals, but ecologists regard them as essential to the health of kelp forests along the Pacific coast. Otters eat sea urchins, which feed on kelp. Where otters disappeared because of hunting, urchin numbers rose sharply and whole kelp forests were stripped bare. When otters returned to some areas, the urchins declined and the kelp grew back, along with the many fish that shelter in it.

Question: Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

  • A) Sea otters are among the most playful animals in the ocean.
  • B) Hunting has caused many marine species to disappear from the Pacific coast.
  • C) By controlling sea urchin numbers, sea otters help keep kelp forests healthy.
  • D) Many fish depend on kelp forests for shelter.

Answer: C

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Opposite focus. The text mentions this popular view only to move past it ("but ecologists regard them as essential…").
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Too broad. Only otters are said to have disappeared because of hunting; the text is about one food chain.
  • C — ✓ Correct. Topic = sea otters; point = they protect kelp by eating urchins. Every other sentence (urchins eat kelp; kelp lost without otters; kelp returns with otters) supports it. Correct.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Too narrow. A true detail from the last sentence, not the main idea.

Worked example 2 — Main idea (prose in a nineteenth-century style)

The following text is written in the style of a nineteenth-century novel. Mr. Ashby was a gentleman of considerable fortune and of still more considerable opinions, which he distributed among his neighbours with a liberality he never extended to his purse. He would lecture the vicar upon charity, the doctor upon physic, and the farmers upon the weather; yet when the church roof fell in, and a subscription was raised for its repair, his name was found, after much searching, at the very bottom of the list, beside the smallest sum of any.

Question: Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

  • A) Mr. Ashby freely gives advice to others but is unwilling to give money.
  • B) Mr. Ashby is the most respected and generous man in the parish.
  • C) The church roof fell in because Mr. Ashby refused to pay for repairs.
  • D) The vicar, the doctor and the farmers resent Mr. Ashby's lectures.

Vocabulary help: "physic" = medicine; "subscription" = a collection of money from many people; "liberality" = generosity.

Answer: A

  • A — ✓ Correct. Decode: "opinions… distributed with a liberality he never extended to his purse" = generous with opinions, not with money. The list of lectures + "yet" + smallest donation prove it. Correct.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Reversed. The narrator is mocking him; the smallest donation shows he is not generous.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Distorted cause. The roof fell in first; his small donation came afterward, when money was collected.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Unsupported. The text never tells us how the vicar, doctor or farmers feel.

Worked example 3 — Main idea (poem in an older style)

The following text is from a poem written in an older style. O river, thou that hast no rest, / That runn'st forever to the sea, / Thou bear'st the mountain on thy breast / In grains too small for eyes to see. / The hills that stood when time was young / Shall pass, like whispers, down thy stream; / And all the ages raised and sung / Thou carriest softly, as a dream.

Question: Which choice best states the main idea of the poem?

  • A) The speaker wishes to follow the river on its journey to the sea.
  • B) The river is a danger to the people who live in the mountains.
  • C) The speaker admires the river's beauty on a quiet evening.
  • D) Through its constant motion, the river slowly carries away even the oldest and greatest things.

Four questions: Speaker? Someone watching the river. Addressed to? The river ("O river"). Situation? The river flowing. Point? It slowly wears away everything, even mountains.

Answer: D

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Unsupported. The speaker talks TO the river but never wishes to travel with it.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Off-topic. No people are mentioned, and the river works "softly", not dangerously.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Unsupported detail. No evening is described; the poem is about time and change, not a scene.
  • D — ✓ Correct. Decode: "thou hast no rest" = you never stop; "bear'st the mountain… in grains" = you carry the mountain away as tiny bits; the ancient hills "shall pass… down thy stream." Correct.

Worked example 4 — Detail question (social science)

In 2019, a group of volunteers began recording the birds that visited a small public garden in their city each morning. Over three years, they recorded 64 species. Most were common city birds, but 11 were migrants that stopped in the garden only during spring and autumn, apparently to rest and feed on journeys between Europe and Africa. The volunteers now hope to persuade the city to plant more native shrubs, which provide the insects and berries that migrating birds need.

Question: According to the text, why do the volunteers want the city to plant more native shrubs?

  • A) To attract more common city birds to the garden
  • B) Because the shrubs provide food that migrating birds need
  • C) Because native shrubs require less water than other plants
  • D) To make the garden more attractive to visitors

Answer: B

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Wrong group. The shrubs are linked to migrating birds, not common city birds.
  • B — ✓ Correct. The last sentence: shrubs "provide the insects and berries that migrating birds need". Insects and berries = food. A paraphrase of the text. Correct.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Not stated. Water is never mentioned; this might be true in real life, but it is not in the text.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Not stated. Visitors are never mentioned.
Classic traps
  • Too narrow: a true detail presented as the main idea. Ask: does the WHOLE text support it?
  • Too broad: a general statement about a bigger topic than the text discusses.
  • Distorted: real words from the passage put together in the wrong relationship (wrong cause, wrong person, wrong order).
  • Opposite of the irony: in older prose, taking the narrator's "praise" literally when it is really mockery.
  • Outside knowledge: a choice that is true in real life but not stated in the text.
  • Literal reading of a poem: choosing the surface image (a sunset, a tree) instead of what the image means (the end of life, strength).
Fast strategy for test day
  • Before the choices, say the topic + point in one line.
  • Use the turn word or the last sentence; the main point is often there.
  • For detail questions, find the key word in the text and read one sentence before and after.
  • For older texts, find the main subject and verb, and translate one difficult phrase at a time.
  • Eliminate too narrow, too broad and extreme choices first. If two remain, pick the one that every sentence supports. Never leave a question blank: wrong answers cost nothing.

Remember
  • Main idea = topic + point that the WHOLE text supports.
  • Reject too narrow, too broad, distorted and extreme choices.
  • Detail answers are stated in the text, usually in different words; never infer.
  • Older prose: find the main subject and verb, and watch for irony.
  • Poems: read by sentence; ask who speaks, to whom, about what, and where the shift is.
