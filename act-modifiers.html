American Diploma · ACT · English: Sentence Structure

Misplaced and Dangling Modifiers

The opening phrase must describe the noun right after the comma — dangling and misplaced modifiers, only/almost placement, how to spot the fix in the choices, and a 10-question ACT-style practice.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Modifier questions belong to the Conventions of Standard English reporting category of the ACT English test (38–43% of the English score, together with punctuation and usage). The English test has 50 questions (40 scored) in 35 minutes over five essays: three of about 340 words with 10 questions each and two of about 185 words with 5 questions each.

A modifier item is easy to recognise: the underlined portion is often long — sometimes a whole sentence — and the four choices (the first usually NO CHANGE) contain the same words in a different order, or switch between active and passive ("the farmers cut the dates" / "the dates are cut by the farmers"). When the choices shuffle word order, the ACT is testing one thing: is every describing word or phrase sitting next to the thing it describes?

الفكرة الأساسية

الكلمة أو الجملة اللي بتوصف حاجة لازم تكون جنبها على طول. لو الجملة بتبدأ بوصف وبعده فاصلة (زي Walking… أو Built in…)، أول اسم بعد الفاصلة لازم يكون هو اللي بيعمل الوصف ده. لو لأ، يبقى dangling modifier = غلط.

Rule 1: The opening phrase describes the noun right after the comma

A modifier is a word or phrase that describes something. When a sentence opens with a describing phrase and a comma, English grammar attaches that phrase to the first noun after the comma (the subject). If that noun is not the one doing or being the action, the modifier is dangling — and the sentence says something silly.

  • ✗ Walking along the Corniche, the sea breeze felt wonderful. (the breeze was not walking)
  • ✓ Walking along the Corniche, we felt the wonderful sea breeze.

The ACT uses many kinds of opening modifier. Learn to spot them all:

Opening modifier✗ Dangling✓ Correct
-ing phraseStudying late every night, the exam seemed easy.Studying late every night, Omar found the exam easy.
-ed / past participle phraseBuilt in 1902, tourists still visit the old bridge.Built in 1902, the old bridge is still visited by tourists.
Having + past participleHaving finished the final, the trophy was lifted by the captain.Having finished the final, the captain lifted the trophy.
To + verb (purpose)To win the science fair, a strong hypothesis is needed.To win the science fair, a student needs a strong hypothesis.
After / Before / While + -ingAfter reading the report, the plan was changed.After reading the report, the manager changed the plan.
Noun phrase (appositive)A talented singer, the audience loved Hany's voice.A talented singer, Hany was loved by the audience.
Adjective phraseFamous for its dates, tourists visit Siwa in October.Famous for its dates, Siwa attracts tourists in October.
As a… / Like… / Unlike…Unlike her brother, chemistry bores Salma.Unlike her brother, Salma finds chemistry boring.

The test question to ask: "Who or what is doing this?" Then check that this exact noun comes right after the comma.

Three special traps in Rule 1

1. The possessive trap. The right name appears, but as a possessive (Mona's, the city's), so the real subject is the thing owned.

  • ✗ Trained as a pharmacist, Mona's first job was in Tanta. (the job was not trained)
  • ✓ Trained as a pharmacist, Mona found her first job in Tanta.

2. The passive trap. Changing to the passive voice can move the wrong noun to the front. Sometimes the passive is the fix, sometimes it is the problem — always check which noun follows the comma.

  • ✗ Climbing the tall palms, the dates are cut by the farmers.
  • ✓ Climbing the tall palms, the farmers cut the dates.
  • ✓ Packed in wooden boxes, the dates are sent to Cairo. (here the passive is correct: the dates were packed)

3. The "it / there" trap. "It" and "there" cannot do an action, so they can never follow an opening -ing phrase that needs a person.

  • ✗ Entering the classroom, there was a strange smell.
  • ✓ Entering the classroom, the students noticed a strange smell.

Two ways to fix a dangling modifier

  1. Put the doer right after the comma. Keep the opening phrase and change the main clause: "Walking along the Corniche, we felt the breeze." This is the fix the ACT uses most.
  2. Give the opening phrase its own subject. Turn it into a full dependent clause: "While we were walking along the Corniche, the sea breeze felt wonderful." Now the phrase is not a modifier any more, so any subject can follow.

Both fixes are correct. On the ACT, the underline usually covers the words after the comma, so the first fix is the one you will see in the choices. If the underline covers the opening phrase, look for the second fix.

Rule 2: Misplaced words and phrases

A modifier can also be misplaced in the middle or at the end of a sentence. Phrases and relative clauses attach to the nearest noun.

  • ✗ The waiter brought a cake to the table covered in chocolate. (the table is covered?)
  • ✓ The waiter brought a cake covered in chocolate to the table.
  • ✗ The first version of the arm could barely lift a block that we built. (we built the block?)
  • ✓ The first version of the arm that we built could barely lift a block.
  • ✗ I saw a mosque driving through Old Cairo with a beautiful minaret.
  • ✓ Driving through Old Cairo, I saw a mosque with a beautiful minaret.

Limiting words. Words such as only, almost, nearly, just, even, hardly, barely, merely, exactly describe the word that comes right after them. Moving them changes the meaning — and the ACT wants the logical meaning.

SentenceWhat it really says
✗ She almost read the whole book.She nearly started reading, but did not (illogical).
✓ She read almost the whole book.She read most of it.
✗ The library is nearly open every day.It is almost open, but not quite (illogical).
✓ The library is open nearly every day.It opens on most days.
✗ He only drinks water at lunch.At lunch he only drinks it (does not do anything else with it).
✓ He drinks only water at lunch.Water is his only drink at lunch.

Rule of thumb: put the limiting word directly in front of the number, amount or word it limits — "only three students", "almost every round", "nearly two hours".

كلمات زي only و almost و nearly و just بتوصف الكلمة اللي بعدها على طول. قول: I won almost every game (كسبت تقريبًا كل الماتشات) مش I almost won every game (كنت قربت أكسب بس مكسبتش). حطها قبل الرقم أو الكمية مباشرة.

How to spot the fix in the choices

  1. Notice the shuffle. If the four choices use the same words in a different order, this is a modifier question.
  2. Name the doer. Read the opening phrase and decide who or what is doing it (who was climbing? what was built?).
  3. Eliminate every choice where a different noun — or a possessive, "it" or "there" — follows the comma.
  4. Check the rest. Among the survivors, make sure the sentence is complete and not a comma splice, and that phrases in the middle and end also touch the right noun.
  5. Read it aloud in your head. The correct version should make sense literally, not just "probably".

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — dangling -ed phrase

Passage: __Opened in 1869, ships from all over the world have used the Suez Canal__ to travel between Europe and Asia.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) Opened in 1869, the Suez Canal has been used by ships from all over the world
  • C) Opened in 1869, the ships of the world have used the Suez Canal
  • D) Ships from all over the world, opened in 1869, have used the Suez Canal

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "ships" follows the comma, so the sentence says the ships were opened in 1869.
  • B) ✓ Correct: The canal was opened, and "the Suez Canal" comes right after the comma. The passive voice is the fix here.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Still dangling — the ships, not the canal, follow the modifier.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Misplaced — "opened in 1869" now sits next to "the world" / "ships".

Worked example 2 — the possessive trap

Passage: __Trained as a pharmacist in Alexandria, Mona's first job was__ in a small hospital in Tanta.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) Trained as a pharmacist in Alexandria, the first job Mona had was
  • C) Trained as a pharmacist in Alexandria, Mona found her first job
  • D) Mona's first job, trained as a pharmacist in Alexandria, was

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: The subject is "job", not Mona — a job cannot be trained.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: "the first job" follows the comma — still dangling.
  • C) ✓ Correct: Mona was trained, and "Mona" is the subject right after the comma.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: The phrase now describes "job" from the middle of the sentence — misplaced.

Worked example 3 — a limiting word

Passage: The new public library is __nearly open__ every day of the year; it closes only on two national holidays.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) open nearly
  • C) nearly opened
  • D) open, nearly,

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "nearly open" means the library is almost open but not quite — illogical with the next clause.
  • B) ✓ Correct: "nearly" now limits "every day": the library opens on almost all days, which matches "closes only on two holidays".
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Changes the meaning and the tense; still illogical.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: Commas make "nearly" an interruption and the meaning unclear.

Worked example 4 — a misplaced phrase at the end

Passage: At the party, the waiter brought __a cake to the table covered in chocolate__.

Choose the best version of the underlined portion.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) a cake to the table, covered in chocolate
  • C) covered in chocolate a cake to the table
  • D) a cake covered in chocolate to the table

Answer: D

  • A) ✗ Wrong: "covered in chocolate" touches "table", so the table is covered in chocolate.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: A comma does not move the phrase; it still seems to describe the table (or the waiter).
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Awkward and unclear word order.
  • D) ✓ Correct: The phrase sits right after "cake", the thing it describes.
Classic traps
  • The possessive dangler: "Born in Tanta, Ali's paintings…" — Ali is mentioned, but the subject is "paintings".
  • The passive switch: "Climbing the palms, the dates are cut by the farmers" — the passive puts the wrong noun first.
  • It / there after an -ing opener: "Entering the room, there was…" is always wrong.
  • The one-word answer: a choice that only changes the verb tense or a comma never fixes a dangling modifier.
  • The misplaced only/almost: "I almost won every round" vs "I won almost every round" — pick the logical meaning.
  • The far-away which-clause: "…a block that we built" when we built the arm, not the block.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Same words, different order in the choices → modifier question.
  • Opening phrase + comma → ask "Who is doing this?" and find that noun right after the comma.
  • Cross out choices with a possessive, "it", or "there" right after the comma.
  • only / almost / nearly / just → put it right before the word or amount it limits.
  • A long correct choice beats a short dangling one — the ACT does not reward brevity when the meaning is wrong.

لو الاختيارات الأربعة نفس الكلام بس مترتب بشكل مختلف، يبقى السؤال عن الـ modifier. اسأل نفسك: مين اللي بيعمل الوصف اللي في أول الجملة؟ ودوّر على الاختيار اللي الاسم ده جاي فيه بعد الفاصلة على طول. استبعد أي اختيار فيه 's أو it أو there بعد الفاصلة.

Practice

Two short ACT-style essays, 5 questions each. Every item tests modifier placement — sometimes NO CHANGE is correct. Explanations appear after you answer.

Remember
  • An opening phrase + comma describes the first noun after the comma — make it the right doer.
  • Watch the possessive trap (Mona's job), the passive trap and it / there after an opener.
  • Fix by putting the doer after the comma, or by giving the opening phrase its own subject (While we were walking…).
  • Phrases and which/that clauses go right next to the noun they describe.
  • only / almost / nearly / just go right before the word or amount they limit.
