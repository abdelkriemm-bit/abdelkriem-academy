Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Describing a Character

How to create a believable, memorable character with the STEAL method — speech, thoughts, effect on others, actions and looks — with building-block sentences and a 350+ word sample story.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is character description?

A character is a person (or animal or creature) in your story. Characterisation is how the writer shows what that character is like: their personality, feelings, habits and how they change. In Paper 2 (Fiction), your story in Section B Writing (25 marks, about 35 minutes) almost always needs a strong main character — many tasks even have a bullet such as 'describe the character' or 'show how the character feels'. Markers reward characters who feel real and developed, not names attached to events.

Character description also matters in Section A Reading: questions often ask what we learn about a character, how a writer shows their feelings, or what a phrase suggests about them. Learning to write characterisation makes you better at reading it.

Direct characterisation (telling)Indirect characterisation (showing)
The writer simply states the trait: 'Uncle Sayed was grumpy.'The writer gives evidence and lets the reader decide: '"No football in front of the cars!" he barked, and the pigeons took off in fright.'
Quick, clear, but flatVivid, memorable, and it involves the reader
Use it rarely — for minor characters or a deliberate short lineUse it for your main characters and the important moments

2. Features: the STEAL method

Indirect characterisation uses STEAL: Speech, Thoughts, Effect on others, Actions, Looks. You rarely need all five in one paragraph. A quick sketch needs two or three. A main character in a whole story can use all five, spread across the story.

STEAL elementWhat to showExample (original)
S — SpeechWhat they say and HOW they say it (tone, volume, favourite phrases)"No football in front of the cars!" he would bark.
T — ThoughtsWhat they think, fear or hope — inside their headI was certain he hated us.
E — Effect on othersHow other people react: fear, respect, laughter, silenceYounger children crossed to the other side of the street rather than walk past him.
A — ActionsWhat they do, habits, gestures, choices under pressureHe stopped the ball with his foot and passed it back without standing up.
L — LooksFace, body, clothes, posture, expressions, body languageShoulders like a wardrobe and a grey moustache that drooped over his mouth.
Other featureExample (original)
One dominant impression (a clear main trait)Every detail of Uncle Sayed first shows 'frightening'… then the twist shows 'gentle'.
A telling detail (one small thing that reveals a lot)his scarred knuckles. A watch he checks every minute. A phone with a cracked screen she refuses to replace
Simile or metaphor for the personhands like two sleeping animals. His voice was a rusty gate
Character change (development)Narrator moves from fear to respect. The doorman moves from barking to passing the ball back.
A want or a fearNour wants to play for a real team. Omar fears being laughed at.
Body language for feelingsfidgeting fingers, a forced smile, eyes fixed on the floor, shoulders dropping with relief
Names that fit the storyRealistic Egyptian names for a realistic story: Ziad, Uncle Sayed, Nour, Hamza

3. How to build a character — step by step

  1. Decide the role. Main character (protagonist)? Opponent (antagonist)? Helper? Every character must have a job in the plot.
  2. Choose one main trait and one want/fear. E.g. stubborn + wants to win the tournament. Shy + fears speaking in public.
  3. Pick a telling detail. One object, habit or feature that reveals the trait (a notebook he never lets anyone see).
  4. Introduce with action or speech, not a list. Open with the character doing or saying something typical.
  5. Spread STEAL through the story. Looks early, speech and actions in the build-up, thoughts and feelings at the climax, effect on others throughout.
  6. Show change. At the climax, the character does something that reveals a hidden side or forces them to change.
  7. Close with a lasting image. The final detail shows how the character (or the narrator's view of them) has changed.
ParagraphWhat to writeCharacterisation job
1. OpeningIntroduce the character in a typical momentLooks + one action. First impression
2. Build-upTheir everyday behaviour. Others' reactionsSpeech + effect on others. Confirm the first impression
3. Rising actionAn event pulls the character into the problemActions + narrator's thoughts
4. ClimaxThe character acts under pressureA surprising action reveals the hidden side
5. Falling actionDialogue about what just happenedSpeech reveals the past or the reason
6. ResolutionLife after — the relationship has changedA final image that shows the change

5. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Looks and body language

  • …with eyes that missed nothing
  • a smile that did not reach his eyes
  • shoulders hunched as if against a cold wind
  • her fingers drummed on the table
  • he carried himself like…

Speech and tone

  • …he barked / muttered / drawled / stammered
  • her voice dropped to a whisper
  • …in a voice like gravel
  • he always ended with the same words: …

Thoughts and feelings

  • Part of me wanted to…, but…
  • I could not shake the feeling that…
  • A question kept circling in my head: …
  • For the first time, I wondered whether…

Effect on others

  • People seemed to stand straighter when…
  • The room went quiet.
  • Even the … kept their distance.
  • Nobody could help smiling when…

Character change

  • From that day on, I saw … differently.
  • It was the first time I had seen him…
  • Perhaps I had never really looked at…

Timing in the exam. Section B Writing has about 35 minutes. Use them like this:

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph. Jot two or three STEAL details for your main character.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). Keep checking: am I showing the character through STEAL, not listing adjectives?
30–35Proofread: speech punctuation, capital letters, apostrophes, spelling of your ambitious words, tense slips and missing words.

6. A strong sample story

Sample story — The Man on the Bench (realistic fiction, character-led)451 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: You have read an extract about a boy who misjudges a new neighbour. Write a story about a character who turns out to be very different from how they first seem. In your story you should: describe the character when the narrator first knows them, describe an event that changes the narrator's opinion, and explain how the story ends.

Every building on our street has a doorman, but none of them is like Uncle Sayed.

He sat on the same wooden bench beside the entrance from sunrise until sunset, as if he had been built there along with the walls. He was a huge man with shoulders like a wardrobe and a grey moustache that drooped over his mouth, hiding whether he was smiling or not. He never was. His hands were enormous, cracked and scarred across the knuckles, and he kept them folded on his stomach like two sleeping animals.

"No football in front of the cars!" he would bark whenever our ball rolled near the gate. His voice was so deep that the pigeons on the balcony above him took off in fright. Younger children crossed to the other side of the street rather than walk past him, and even the delivery men handed him their parcels quickly and hurried away without a word.

I was certain he hated us. My friend Ziad was certain he hated everybody.

Then, one Friday in June, everything changed.

It was the final of our street tournament: our building against the boys from number fourteen. The score was two-all, and there were seconds left. Our goalkeeper, Ziad, twisted his ankle and limped off, white-faced. Nobody else would stand in goal. Nobody, that is, except the person who had just risen from his bench.

Uncle Sayed walked slowly onto the street, rolled up the sleeves of his galabeya and stood between the two plastic chairs that were our goalposts. The other team laughed. Then their striker kicked the ball as hard as he could.

What happened next lasted less than a second. The old man dived — really dived — and caught the ball cleanly in one enormous hand, before landing on the dusty tarmac with a grunt. For a moment, the whole street was silent. Then everyone was cheering.

"Where did you learn that?" I gasped.

He brushed the dust from his knees, and for the first time I saw what the moustache had been hiding: a crooked, shy smile. "Port Said," he said. "Nineteen eighty-two. Third division. My hands still remember, even if my knees do not."

The next evening, he did not shout when the ball rolled towards the gate. Instead, he stopped it with his foot and passed it back, perfectly, without even standing up. Soon there were three of us sitting beside him on the bench every afternoon, listening to stories of muddy pitches and roaring crowds.

The bench is still there, and so is Uncle Sayed. The younger children still cross the street when they see him. I just smile, because I know what those scarred hands can do.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: the three bullets are answered in order — the first impression (paragraphs 1–4), the event that changes the narrator's opinion (paragraphs 5–10), the ending (paragraphs 11–12). The theme — people are not always what they seem — is shown through events, never explained. The first-person narrator's changing view keeps the reader involved.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: all five STEAL elements are used — Looks (shoulders like a wardrobe, drooping moustache, scarred knuckles), Speech (the barked warning, then the quiet memory of Port Said), Thoughts (I was certain he hated us), Effect on others (pigeons, children and delivery men all avoid him), Actions (he dives, then passes the ball back). Precise verbs (bark, drooped, limped, dived, grunt) and fresh similes (hands 'like two sleeping animals') create a clear dominant impression that is then overturned.
  • Structure of texts 7/7: a short, confident opening line. A build-up that confirms the first impression. A one-line paragraph as the turning point ('Then, one Friday in June, everything changed.'). A slowed-down climax ('What happened next lasted less than a second.'). Falling action in dialogue that reveals the backstory. And a circular ending that returns to the bench and the children crossing the street — but with the narrator now changed.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: a full range of sentence types — simple for impact ('He never was.'), compound ('The bench is still there, and so is Uncle Sayed.'), complex ('His voice was so deep that the pigeons… took off in fright.'), compound-complex ('He brushed the dust from his knees, and for the first time I saw what the moustache had been hiding…'). Varied starters (subordinate clause, adverbials 'Then', 'Instead', 'Soon'). Punctuation: a pair of dashes for extra information ('dived — really dived —'), colons for explanation ('the final of our street tournament:' / 'what the moustache had been hiding:'), a pair of commas for extra information ('Our goalkeeper, Ziad, twisted…'), accurate speech punctuation with a new line for each speaker. The story is in the past tense. The present tense appears only for facts still true at the time of telling (first and last paragraphs) and inside speech.
  • Word structure (spelling) 3/3: ambitious and tricky words spelt correctly (moustache, enormous, tournament, division, perfectly, crooked). Plurals (parcels, pitches, knuckles) and capital letters for names and places (Port Said, June) correct.

Why does the author use…? (all the effects you could write about this sample)

  1. Why does the author use the simile 'shoulders like a wardrobe'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show how huge and broad Uncle Sayed is (looks). To make him seem solid, heavy and hard to move — like furniture. To make him seem frightening to the children at first. To create a humorous, memorable picture of him.

  2. Why does the author use the simile 'like two sleeping animals' for his hands?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show his hands are large and still. To suggest hidden power — animals that could wake up at any moment (foreshadowing the dive). To make the reader feel slightly nervous of him. To create a strong visual image.

  3. Why does the author use the pair of dashes in 'The old man dived — really dived — and caught the ball'?

    Show answer
    A pair of dashes always adds extra information: here it adds the narrator's amazed comment that it was a real, professional dive, which shows how surprised he is and makes the moment more dramatic.

  4. Why does the author use the colon in 'for the first time I saw what the moustache had been hiding: a crooked, shy smile'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to introduce extra information (what was hidden). To give emphasis to the surprise reveal of the smile. To introduce an explanation that answers the earlier mystery of whether he was smiling or not.

  5. Why does the author write the very short sentence 'He never was.'?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: it creates emphasis and humour after the long description. It shows the narrator's strong first opinion of Uncle Sayed. Its shortness matches the man's hard, unsmiling character. It is later contradicted, so it sets up the twist.

  6. What does 'the delivery men handed him their parcels quickly and hurried away without a word' suggest about Uncle Sayed?

    Show answer
    It suggests that even adults find him frightening or unfriendly (effect on others), which makes his later kindness more surprising.

7. Common mistakes and checklist

MistakeBetter
A 'police report' description: 'He was tall. He had black hair. He wore a blue shirt.'Choose details that reveal personality: 'His blue shirt was ironed so sharply it could cut paper.'
Only telling: 'She was kind and generous.'Show it: 'She slid half of her sandwich across the desk without looking up.'
Too many characters with no jobTwo or three characters, each with a clear role
The character never changes or reveals anything newPlan one moment where the character surprises the reader
Description dumped in one big paragraph, then forgottenSpread STEAL details through the whole story
Dialogue that sounds the same for every characterGive each character a different voice: short and gruff, nervous and rambling…
Feelings named, not shown: 'He was nervous.'Body language: 'He cracked his knuckles one by one.'
Character checklist
  • My main character has one clear trait and one want or fear.
  • I used at least three STEAL elements, spread through the story.
  • I showed more than I told.
  • I included one telling detail that reveals personality.
  • Other characters react to my main character (effect on others).
  • There is a moment where the character acts under pressure or reveals a hidden side.
  • Each speaking character has their own voice, and speech is correctly punctuated.
  • My ending shows how the character, or the narrator's view of them, has changed.

8. Practice

  1. Practice task: 'You have read an extract about a girl and her strict piano teacher. Write a story about a character who learns something from someone they did not like at first.' Bullets: describe both characters · what happens between them · how the story ends. Write a short plan.

    Show answer
    G.C.S.P.P.: realistic fiction. Narrator Adam (13, lazy at maths, jokes to hide worry) and Mr Rashad, a new maths tutor (brusque, never smiles, checks his watch). A hot summer tutoring centre in Heliopolis. First person. Whole story. STEAL for Mr Rashad: Looks — ironed shirt, red pen always in hand. Speech — 'Again.' Effect — students fall silent. Actions — he stays late to explain. Plot: (1) first lesson — Adam hates him. (2) weekly red crosses. (3) Adam fails a test and wants to quit. (4) climax — Mr Rashad stays two hours to explain, revealing he failed maths at school too. (5) Adam passes. (6) ending — Adam finds a note in red pen: 'Again — but this time for fun.'

  2. Turn this telling sentence into showing with two STEAL elements: 'Karim was very confident.'

    Show answer
    Example: Karim strolled to the front of the hall with his hands in his pockets (Actions) and winked at the judges (Actions). "Ready when you are," he said, loud enough for the back row to hear (Speech).

  3. Write a sentence using 'Effect on others' to show that a new student is mysterious.

    Show answer
    Example: Whenever the new boy walked into the canteen, conversations dipped for a moment, as if everyone were trying to guess his secret.

  4. Give a telling detail for each character: (a) a nervous person, (b) a careful, organised person, (c) a kind grandparent.

    Show answer
    Examples: (a) a pen lid chewed flat. (b) pencils lined up by size before every lesson. (c) a pocket that always contains sweets for the neighbours' children.

  5. Why does a writer use indirect characterisation instead of simply telling the reader the trait? Give three effects.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: it makes the reader work out the character for themselves, so they are more involved. It makes the character more vivid and believable. It allows surprises and twists, because first impressions can be wrong. It makes the writing more interesting and less like a list.

  6. Write two lines of dialogue that show two different personalities (one impatient, one calm).

    Show answer
    Example: "Hurry up! We'll miss the bus!" Ziad shouted, already halfway down the stairs. (new line) "The bus will wait," his grandfather said, slowly tying his shoelaces. "It always does."
