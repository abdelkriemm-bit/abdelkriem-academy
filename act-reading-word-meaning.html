American Diploma · ACT · Reading: Craft and Structure

Words and Phrases in Context

How to find the meaning of a word or phrase as it is used in an ACT Reading passage, including less common meanings, figurative language and connotation, with worked examples and a full practice passage.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Words-in-context questions belong to the Craft and Structure category, which makes up 26–33% of the scored ACT Reading questions (about 7 to 9 of the 27 scored questions). They ask what a word or a phrase means as it is used in one particular sentence. You do not need a big dictionary in your head: the passage always gives you the clues. What the ACT tests is whether you can read the sentence carefully enough to ignore the meaning you already know when the passage uses a different one.

These questions come in three forms. All three appear in literary narratives and in informational passages (humanities, social science, natural science).

FormTypical stem (in our own words)What you must decide
Word meaning"As it is used in paragraph 3, the word x most nearly means:"The meaning in THIS sentence, often a less common meaning
Figurative phrase"The phrase x (paragraph 2) most nearly means that…" / "…suggests that…"What an image or comparison really says in plain words
Connotation / word choice"The author's use of the word x suggests that…" / "The phrase x indicates that the critics believe…"The feeling or judgment the word carries: positive, negative, neutral
الفكرة في سطر

السؤال مش بيسألك الكلمة معناها إيه في القاموس. بيسألك معناها إيه في الجملة دي بالذات. وغالبًا المعنى المشهور اللي إنت عارفه هو الفخ.

The method, step by step

  1. Go back to the passage. Read the whole sentence that contains the word, plus the sentence before and the sentence after. Never answer from the word alone.
  2. Cover the choices and blank out the word. Read the sentence with a blank in its place: "He could ______ through ten years of a hero's life in one sentence."
  3. Predict your own simple word from the clues. It does not need to be elegant: "go fast" is a perfect prediction.
  4. Plug each choice into the blank. The right answer keeps the exact meaning of the sentence and fits the grammar. Read the full sentence each time.
  5. Be suspicious of the most familiar meaning. If a choice gives the first meaning you learned for the word, check it twice. The ACT likes to test a second or third meaning.
  6. For phrases, translate the picture into plain English. Ask: what is being compared to what, and what do they have in common?
  7. For connotation, ask: positive, negative or neutral? Then ask how strong. "Dismiss" is negative and firm; "question" is negative but gentle.

Where are the clues? Context clues come in a few regular shapes. Learn to spot them and you will rarely need to guess.

Clue typeSignalExample
Definition / restatementa colon, a pair of commas or dashes, "in other words", "that is""The story did not belong to him alone. It was shared property." (the second sentence explains the first)
Contrastbut, yet, rather than, not … but, unlike"A recording is fixed; a told story stays alive." → fixed = unchanging
Examplesuch as, for example, like, a list"trapped in a well, surrounded by enemies" → "the worst possible moment" = great danger
Cause and effectso, because, so … that"so attached to a hero that they refused to let him suffer" → attached = devoted
Tone of the paragraphpositive or negative words nearby"Critics dismiss…" → the next phrase will be negative

Right reasoning vs wrong reasoning

Here are ✓/✗ pairs that show the difference between a student who reads the sentence and a student who reads only the word.

  • ✓ "The bridge carried the weight of a thousand cars." → carried = supported. ✗ carried = took from one place to another (the bridge does not move).
  • ✓ "Her argument does not hold water." → it is weak, it cannot be defended. ✗ it is about water or containers (the phrase is figurative).
  • ✓ "The scientist entertained the idea for a week before rejecting it." → considered. ✗ amused (nobody was laughing).
  • ✓ "The new rules were sound." → sensible, well-reasoned. ✗ noise (a noun meaning in an adjective position).
  • ✓ "He weathered the crisis." → survived, came through. ✗ was damaged by rain and sun (that meaning fits old wood, not a person in a crisis).
  • ✓ "The museum was thin on modern work." → had little of. ✗ narrow or slim in shape.
  • ✓ "The villagers were frugal" (said with praise) → careful with money. ✗ "stingy" (same idea, but a negative connotation the author did not intend).

Figurative language: say it in plain words

ACT passages, especially literary narratives and humanities essays, are full of images. A figurative-language question asks you to translate the image into a plain statement. The right answer is never the literal picture.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
metaphordescribes one thing as if it were another: "The classroom was a beehive." → busy and noisy
similea comparison with like or as: "He waited like a cat at a door." → patiently, ready to move
personificationgives human actions to a thing: "The room leaned toward him." → the people listened closely
hyperboledeliberate exaggeration: "I have told you a million times." → many times, with frustration
idioma fixed phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words: "to break the ice" → to end an awkward silence
understatementsaying less than is meant: "The storm was a bit inconvenient" (it destroyed the roof) → often ironic or dry

Three questions turn any image into an answer: (1) What is the literal picture? (2) What is it really about in this passage? (3) What do the two share? "The radio slowly drained the rooms" → picture: liquid running out; really about: coffeehouses; shared idea: the people (audiences) left little by little.

Connotation: the feeling inside the word

Two words can share a dictionary meaning but carry different feelings. Authors choose words for their connotation, and the ACT asks what that choice suggests about the author's or a character's view.

determinedpersistentstubborn
confidentself-assuredarrogant
thriftyeconomicalstingy
curiousinterestednosy
traditionaloldoutdated
revivalreturnnostalgia (when critics use it)

في أسئلة الـ connotation اسأل نفسك: الكلمة دي إيجابية ولا سلبية ولا محايدة؟ وبعدين: قوية ولا خفيفة؟ مثلًا stubborn سلبية، و determined إيجابية، مع إن المعنى قريب. الاختيار الصح لازم يطابق الإحساس اللي في الكلمة مش بس معناها.

Worked examples

Excerpt 1 (natural science): For years, researchers assumed that the lake's fish population would recover on its own once the factory upstream closed. The numbers told a different story. Even a decade later, the population remained a fraction of its former size, and the scientists began to suspect that the problem lay not in the water but in the mud beneath it, where years of waste had settled.

Worked example 1 — word meaning (the common-meaning trap)

Question: As it is used in the excerpt, the word settled most nearly means:

  • A) sunk and collected.
  • B) agreed on.
  • C) paid.
  • D) become calm.

Answer: A. Put a blank in its place: years of waste had ______ in the mud beneath the water. Your prediction: "sunk down and stayed". A matches.

  • B is wrong — A real meaning ("the dispute was settled"), but waste cannot agree on anything.
  • C is wrong — "Settle a bill" means pay it: a money meaning with no place in this sentence.
  • D is wrong — "The baby settled" means became calm. It describes behaviour, not where waste goes.

Excerpt 2 (literary narrative): My grandfather never told us that the business was in trouble. He simply stopped buying new stock, and the shelves of the shop grew thinner every month, like a tree in autumn. We children noticed the empty spaces long before we understood them.

Worked example 2 — figurative phrase

Question: The comparison "like a tree in autumn" mainly suggests that the shop:

  • A) was decorated with leaves and branches.
  • B) was slowly losing its goods.
  • C) was busiest at the end of the year.
  • D) would soon be full again in spring.

Answer: B. Picture: a tree losing its leaves one by one. Really about: shelves. Shared idea: gradual emptying. B says it in plain words.

  • A is wrong — Too literal: there are no real leaves in the shop.
  • C is wrong — Off-topic: autumn is used for the image of falling leaves, not for the date or for business.
  • D is wrong — Goes beyond the text: the image is only about losing goods; nothing suggests the shop will recover.

Excerpt 3 (social science): Supporters of the new traffic plan call it bold. Its critics prefer the word reckless. Both groups agree on the facts: the plan closes three main roads to cars in a single month, without the slow trial period that most cities use.

Worked example 3 — connotation

Question: The critics' choice of the word reckless suggests that they believe the plan is:

  • A) admirably courageous.
  • B) too expensive for the city.
  • C) dangerously careless.
  • D) carefully tested.

Answer: C. "Bold" and "reckless" describe the same fast decision, but "reckless" carries a negative feeling: taking risks without thinking. C keeps both the meaning (risky) and the feeling (negative).

  • A is wrong — That is the connotation of "bold", the supporters' word: the opposite feeling.
  • B is wrong — Off-topic: money is never mentioned, and "reckless" is about risk, not cost.
  • D is wrong — Reversed: the plan skips the "slow trial period".

Excerpt 4 (literary narrative): The new teacher's handwriting was plain: no curls, no flourishes, every letter standing straight like a soldier. After two years of decorated worksheets that none of us could read, we found it a relief.

Worked example 4 — a word whose meaning depends on the narrator

Question: As it is used in the excerpt, the word plain most nearly means:

  • A) simple and undecorated.
  • B) unattractive.
  • C) flat and open, like land.
  • D) obvious to everyone.

Answer: A. The colon explains the word: "no curls, no flourishes". Plain here = simple. The students found it "a relief", so the word is not negative.

  • B is wrong — Wrong connotation: "a plain face" can mean unattractive, but the students like the handwriting.
  • C is wrong — A geography meaning (a plain = flat land), used as a noun, not for handwriting.
  • D is wrong — "It was plain that he was angry" = obvious. A real meaning, but the colon defines it as "no curls".
Classic traps
  • The common-meaning trap. The first meaning you learned (race = a competition, attached = fastened) is offered as a choice and is usually wrong.
  • The literal-picture trap. For figurative phrases, a choice that describes the image literally (moving seats, real leaves) is wrong.
  • The wrong-feeling trap. The meaning is close but the connotation is reversed: "courageous" for "reckless", "stingy" for "thrifty".
  • The nearby-word trap. A choice uses a word from the same paragraph so it looks familiar, but it does not fit the blank.
  • The "sounds smart" trap. A difficult choice is not more likely to be right. A plain choice that fits the sentence wins.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Words-in-context questions are quick points: answer them first in each passage set.
  • Read the full sentence plus one sentence on each side.
  • Blank out the word and predict a simple replacement BEFORE you look at the choices.
  • Plug in every choice and read the whole sentence aloud in your head.
  • For an image, name the literal picture, the real topic and what they share.
  • For connotation, label it positive, negative or neutral, then strong or mild.

Practice

Now try a full humanities passage (about 700 words) with 9 questions, as on the real test. Most of the questions test words and phrases in context. It is untimed, but aim for about 10 minutes. For every question, predict your own word or plain-English meaning before you look at the choices.

Remember
  • Craft and Structure questions = 26–33% of the scored ACT Reading questions; words in context are among the fastest of them.
  • The answer is the meaning in THIS sentence, and it is often not the most common meaning.
  • Blank out the word, predict a simple replacement, then plug in every choice.
  • Figurative phrases: translate the picture into plain words; the literal picture is a trap.
  • Connotation: match the feeling (positive, negative, neutral) as well as the meaning.
