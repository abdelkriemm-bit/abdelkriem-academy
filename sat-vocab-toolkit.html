High-Value Vocabulary for SAT Reading and Writing

130 academic words grouped by meaning — doubt, support, weakening, research verbs, tone, change, size and cause — each with a definition, Arabic meaning and example, plus word-part tips and a 12-question quiz.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

Words in Context questions reward students who know the academic words that SAT-style passages use again and again: words about evidence (corroborate, undermine), about certainty (tentative, speculative), about attitude (sardonic, ambivalent) and about change (supplant, wane). This toolkit gives you 130 high-value words, grouped by meaning, each with a simple definition, the Arabic meaning, and a short original example sentence.

Why vocabulary matters on the Digital SAT

Words in Context questions belong to the Craft and Structure domain (about 28% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, 13–15 questions), and they usually come first in each module. But vocabulary helps everywhere: in purpose and structure questions you must know what "qualify a claim" or "challenge a view" means; in inference questions a single word like "tentative" or "negligible" decides the answer; in tone questions you must tell "wry" from "scornful".

The words below are not rare "dictionary" words. They are the working words of academic English — the words researchers, historians and critics use to describe evidence, arguments and attitudes. That is why they keep appearing in SAT-style passages and choices.

How to learn these words

  • Learn by group, not alphabetically. Words with similar meanings (refute, rebut, debunk) are learned faster together, and the test often puts near-synonyms in the same question.
  • About 20 words a day, then revise them the next day, after three days and after one week.
  • Learn the charge (+, − or neutral) and the strength of each word. "Critical" and "scornful" are both negative, but "scornful" is much stronger.
  • Say the example sentence aloud and then make your own sentence about school, family or Egypt. A word you have used is a word you keep.
  • Notice the partner words (collocations): evidence corroborates a claim; researchers posit a mechanism; a policy curbs a problem.

1. Doubt and uncertainty

SAT science passages are full of careful claims. These words tell you how sure the writer is.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
skeptical (adj.)doubting that something is true (متشكّك) — e.g. Many astronomers were skeptical of the report until other telescopes confirmed it.
dubious (adj.)probably not true or not honest; also: feeling doubt (مشكوك فيه) — e.g. The website's statistics were dubious, since it gave no source for any of them.
tentative (adj.)not certain or final; careful (مبدئي / غير مؤكَّد) — e.g. The team reached a tentative conclusion and planned three more experiments.
ambiguous (adj.)having more than one possible meaning; unclear (غامض / يحتمل أكثر من معنى) — e.g. The ending of the story is ambiguous: we never learn whether the brothers make peace.
speculative (adj.)based on guesses, not on firm evidence (قائم على التخمين) — e.g. Without fossils, any picture of the animal's color remains speculative.
provisional (adj.)for now only; may be changed later (مؤقّت) — e.g. The museum gave the painting a provisional date until tests could be done.
inconclusive (adj.)not leading to a firm result or decision (غير حاسم) — e.g. The first trial was inconclusive because too few patients took part.
qualify (v.)to limit a statement by adding conditions (يُقيِّد / يحدّ من) — e.g. She qualified her claim, saying it applied only to small coastal towns.
reservation (n.)a doubt or feeling of not fully agreeing (تحفُّظ) — e.g. The engineer supported the plan but expressed reservations about its cost.
equivocal (adj.)unclear, often on purpose; open to two interpretations (مُبهَم / مُراوِغ) — e.g. The minister gave an equivocal answer that neither accepted nor rejected the offer.
contentious (adj.)causing strong disagreement (مثير للجدل) — e.g. The location of the new airport became a contentious issue among residents.
unfounded (adj.)not based on facts or evidence (لا أساس له) — e.g. Fears that the bridge would collapse proved unfounded.

2. Words that support or confirm

Used when evidence makes a claim stronger. All are positive toward the claim; "corroborate" and "substantiate" are the most common in research contexts.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
corroborate (v.)to confirm with new or independent evidence (يؤكِّد بدليل إضافي) — e.g. Diaries written by two travellers corroborate the merchant's account of the storm.
substantiate (v.)to prove a claim with evidence (يُثبِت بالدليل) — e.g. The journalist could not substantiate the rumor, so she did not publish it.
bolster (v.)to make stronger or more convincing (يُقوّي / يدعم) — e.g. Data from a second region bolstered the case for the new vaccine schedule.
validate (v.)to show that something is correct or acceptable (يُثبِت صحّة) — e.g. Field tests validated the computer model's predictions of flood height.
affirm (v.)to state firmly that something is true; to support (يؤكّد) — e.g. The court affirmed the earlier decision.
vindicate (v.)to prove that someone who was doubted was right (يُثبت أنه كان على حق) — e.g. Later discoveries vindicated the geologist, whose theory had been mocked.
buttress (v.)to support an argument, like a wall supports a building (يَسنِد / يدعم) — e.g. She buttresses her argument with letters from the period.
reinforce (v.)to strengthen an existing idea or feeling (يُعزِّز) — e.g. The final scene reinforces the novel's theme of loyalty.
attest (to) (v.)to show or prove that something exists or is true (يشهد على) — e.g. Thousands of preserved receipts attest to the city's busy trade.
uphold (v.)to support and keep a rule, decision or principle (يؤيّد / يحافظ على) — e.g. The committee upheld the ban on fishing in the bay.
endorse (v.)to publicly approve or support (يؤيّد علنًا) — e.g. Several doctors endorsed the campaign to reduce sugar in school meals.

3. Words that weaken or reject

The opposite group. Notice the strength: "challenge" and "call into question" only raise doubt; "refute", "debunk" and "overturn" show that a claim is wrong.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
undermine (v.)to weaken gradually or make less effective (يُضعِف / يُقوِّض) — e.g. Repeated errors in the data undermine the report's conclusion.
refute (v.)to prove that a claim is wrong (يُفنِّد) — e.g. The new fossil refutes the idea that the species never lived in Africa.
rebut (v.)to argue against a claim, giving reasons (يردّ على / يدحض) — e.g. In her reply, the author rebuts each of the reviewer's three objections.
discredit (v.)to make people stop trusting someone or something (يُشكِّك في مصداقية) — e.g. The forged letter was discovered, discrediting the whole collection.
dispute (v.)to say that something is not true or correct (يعترض على) — e.g. Few historians dispute the date of the battle, but many dispute its causes.
challenge (v.)to question whether something is true or right (يتحدّى / يُشكِّك في) — e.g. The study challenges the common belief that sharks avoid shallow water.
contradict (v.)to say or show the opposite (يُناقِض) — e.g. The witness's second statement contradicted her first.
debunk (v.)to show that a popular belief is false (يكشف زيف) — e.g. The documentary debunks the myth that goldfish remember only three seconds.
negate (v.)to cancel the effect of something (يُلغي / يُبطِل) — e.g. Skipping sleep can negate the benefits of extra study time.
dismiss (v.)to refuse to consider seriously (يرفض / يستبعد) — e.g. At first, critics dismissed the young director's film as a student project.
overturn (v.)to reverse a decision or replace an accepted idea (يقلب / يُبطِل) — e.g. The discovery overturned a theory that had been accepted for fifty years.
call into question (phr.)to cause doubt about (يضع موضع شك) — e.g. The broken thermometer calls into question all of that week's readings.
Raise doubt (weaker)Weaken (middle)Prove wrong (strongest)
challenge, question, call into question, disputeundermine, discredit, contradictrefute, debunk, overturn, negate

4. Research and science verbs

These verbs describe what researchers do. They are very common in passages about studies and in the answer choices of purpose and inference questions.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
posit (v.)to suggest an idea as a basis for argument (يفترض) — e.g. The researchers posit that the birds use the sun to navigate.
hypothesize (v.)to propose an explanation that can be tested (يضع فرضية) — e.g. She hypothesized that the fish change color to signal danger.
postulate (v.)to assume something is true as the starting point of a theory (يفترض كمُسلَّمة) — e.g. The model postulates that the planet's core is partly liquid.
infer (v.)to reach a conclusion from evidence (يستنتج) — e.g. From the worn steps, archaeologists inferred that the temple had many visitors.
deduce (v.)to reach a conclusion by logical reasoning (يستنبط) — e.g. By comparing the shadows, the student deduced the time the photo was taken.
extrapolate (v.)to estimate beyond known data by extending a trend (يستقرئ / يمدّ النتائج) — e.g. Extrapolating from ten years of records, they predicted the lake's level in 2040.
quantify (v.)to measure or express as a number (يقيس كمّيًا) — e.g. It is hard to quantify how much a good teacher changes a student's life.
replicate (v.)to repeat an experiment and get the same result (يُكرِّر (تجربة)) — e.g. Two other labs replicated the results within a year.
isolate (v.)to separate one factor so it can be studied alone (يعزل) — e.g. To isolate the effect of light, the team kept temperature and water constant.
elucidate (v.)to make clear; to explain (يوضّح) — e.g. The diagram elucidates how the ancient water wheel lifted water.
characterize (v.)to describe the main qualities of; to be typical of (يصف / يُميِّز) — e.g. The author characterizes the city as noisy but generous.
attribute (to) (v.)to say that something is caused by or belongs to (يَنسِب إلى) — e.g. Scientists attribute the drop in bird numbers to the loss of wetlands.
infer vs deduce vs extrapolate

Infer: conclude from evidence (the general word). Deduce: conclude by strict logic. Extrapolate: extend a known trend beyond the data you have. If a passage predicts the future from past figures, the precise word is "extrapolate".

5. Tone and attitude: positive and neutral

Tone questions and Words in Context questions often ask how a writer or character feels. First decide the charge, then the exact shade.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
admiring (adj.)showing respect and approval (مُعجَب) — e.g. The biography takes an admiring tone toward the doctor's work in rural clinics.
reverent (adj.)showing deep respect, almost religious (مُوقِّر / مُبجِّل) — e.g. Visitors spoke in reverent whispers inside the old library.
nostalgic (adj.)with warm, slightly sad longing for the past (حنين إلى الماضي) — e.g. The narrator is nostalgic about summers in her grandmother's village.
earnest (adj.)serious and sincere (جادّ / صادق) — e.g. His earnest apology convinced the team to give him another chance.
optimistic (adj.)expecting good results (مُتفائل) — e.g. Despite the delays, the engineers remain optimistic about the launch.
laudatory (adj.)expressing praise (مادِح) — e.g. The prize committee's statement was entirely laudatory.
objective (adj.)based on facts, not personal feelings (موضوعي) — e.g. A good news report presents events in an objective way.
detached (adj.)not emotionally involved (محايد / غير متأثّر عاطفيًا) — e.g. The scientist describes the disaster in a detached, technical voice.
measured (adj.)careful, calm and controlled (مُتَّزِن) — e.g. She gave a measured response instead of reacting angrily.
ambivalent (adj.)having mixed feelings (متردِّد / مشاعره متضاربة) — e.g. The poet seems ambivalent about the new city: it excites and frightens her.
impartial (adj.)not favouring either side (غير مُنحاز) — e.g. The judge must remain impartial throughout the trial.
pragmatic (adj.)practical; focused on what works (عملي / واقعي) — e.g. Rather than an ideal plan, the mayor chose a pragmatic one the city could afford.

6. Tone and attitude: negative and critical

Word / phraseMeaning / use
sardonic (adj.)mocking in a bitter, cold way (ساخر بمرارة) — e.g. "What a surprise," he said in a sardonic voice, when the train was late again.
scornful (adj.)showing that you think something is worthless (مُحتقِر) — e.g. The critic was scornful of the film's predictable plot.
disdainful (adj.)showing that you feel superior to someone (مُزدرٍ) — e.g. The duke gave the young painter a disdainful glance.
wry (adj.)lightly and cleverly humorous, often about something unpleasant (ساخر بخفّة) — e.g. She made a wry joke about her twelfth failed experiment.
cynical (adj.)believing people act only for selfish reasons (متشائم من نوايا الناس) — e.g. Years of broken promises had made the villagers cynical about officials.
indignant (adj.)angry because something seems unfair (ساخط) — e.g. Readers were indignant when the prize went to a copied story.
dismissive (adj.)treating something as not worth considering (مُستخِفّ) — e.g. The manager was dismissive of the workers' safety concerns.
condescending (adj.)speaking to others as if they were less intelligent (مُتعالٍ) — e.g. The guide's condescending explanations annoyed the expert visitors.
incredulous (adj.)unable or unwilling to believe something (غير مُصدِّق) — e.g. The fans were incredulous when the champion lost in the first round.
derisive (adj.)openly mocking; laughing at someone (مُستهزِئ) — e.g. The audience's derisive laughter shocked the speaker.
exasperated (adj.)very annoyed after a long time of trouble (نافد الصبر) — e.g. The exasperated teacher explained the rule for the fourth time.
LightMediumStrong
wry (gentle humor), skeptical (doubt)critical, dismissive, cynicalsardonic, scornful, disdainful, derisive
Tone traps
  • Ambivalent ≠ indifferent. Ambivalent = mixed feelings (cares a lot, both ways); indifferent = does not care.
  • Detached / objective describe a calm, factual voice — they are not negative.
  • Wry is gentle; sardonic is bitter. Choose by the strength of the words in the passage.
  • Nostalgic is warm, not sad and not angry.

7. Words for change

Word / phraseMeaning / use
evolve (v.)to develop gradually (يتطوَّر) — e.g. Her painting style evolved from realistic portraits to bold abstract shapes.
transform (v.)to change completely (يُحوِّل جذريًا) — e.g. The railway transformed the small fishing village into a busy port.
shift (n./v.)a change in position or direction (تحوُّل / يتحوّل) — e.g. The survey shows a shift in public opinion toward electric cars.
accelerate (v.)to happen faster (يتسارع) — e.g. Warmer water accelerates the melting of the ice shelf.
diminish (v.)to become smaller or less (يتناقص) — e.g. The river's flow diminishes sharply in late summer.
erode (v.)to wear away slowly; to weaken over time (يتآكل) — e.g. Years of scandal eroded public trust in the company.
fluctuate (v.)to rise and fall irregularly (يتذبذب) — e.g. Tomato prices fluctuate from week to week.
stagnate (v.)to stop developing or growing (يركد) — e.g. Without new investment, the town's economy stagnated.
revive (v.)to bring back to life or use (يُحيي) — e.g. A group of young weavers is reviving a nearly forgotten carpet pattern.
supplant (v.)to take the place of something older (يَحِلّ محلّ) — e.g. Digital cameras quickly supplanted film cameras.
wane (v.)to become gradually weaker or smaller (يضعف / يخفت) — e.g. Interest in the game waned after the first season.
proliferate (v.)to increase quickly in number (ينتشر بسرعة / يتكاثر) — e.g. Food-delivery apps have proliferated in large cities.

8. Importance, size and frequency

Word / phraseMeaning / use
pivotal (adj.)of central importance; causing a turning point (مِحوَري) — e.g. The discovery of the letters was pivotal to solving the mystery.
negligible (adj.)so small it does not matter (ضئيل جدًا) — e.g. The new paint added a negligible amount to the building's cost.
marginal (adj.)small and not very important; at the edge (هامشي) — e.g. The update made only a marginal difference to battery life.
substantial (adj.)large in amount or importance (كبير / جوهري) — e.g. The scholarship covers a substantial part of the fees.
paramount (adj.)more important than anything else (ذو أهمية قصوى) — e.g. On the ship, the safety of passengers is paramount.
profound (adj.)very great or deep (عميق) — e.g. The war had a profound effect on the writer's later work.
modest (adj.)not large; limited (متواضع / بسيط) — e.g. The trial produced a modest improvement in reading scores.
prevalent (adj.)common in a particular place or time (شائع / سائد) — e.g. The disease was once prevalent in the delta's villages.
scarce (adj.)available only in small amounts (نادر / شحيح) — e.g. In the desert, firewood is scarce and valuable.
pervasive (adj.)spreading through every part (مُتغلغِل / منتشر في كل جانب) — e.g. A pervasive smell of smoke filled the old house.
ubiquitous (adj.)found everywhere (موجود في كل مكان) — e.g. Mobile phones are now ubiquitous, even in remote areas.
peripheral (adj.)at the edge; not central (جانبي / ثانوي) — e.g. The essay spends too long on peripheral details.

9. Cause, effect and connection

Learn these in pairs: foster / facilitate (help) vs impede / hinder (block); mitigate (make less bad) vs exacerbate (make worse).

Word / phraseMeaning / use
precipitate (v.)to make something (usually bad) happen sooner (يُعجِّل بحدوث) — e.g. A sudden rise in bread prices precipitated the protests.
foster (v.)to encourage the development of (يُعزِّز / يرعى) — e.g. Group projects can foster cooperation between students.
impede (v.)to slow or block progress (يُعيق) — e.g. Heavy snow impeded the rescue teams.
hinder (v.)to make something difficult (يُعرقِل) — e.g. A lack of clean water hindered the clinic's work.
mitigate (v.)to make something bad less severe (يُخفِّف) — e.g. Trees along the streets mitigate the summer heat.
exacerbate (v.)to make a problem worse (يُفاقِم) — e.g. Cutting the forests exacerbated the flooding.
facilitate (v.)to make easier (يُسهِّل) — e.g. The new bridge facilitates trade between the two towns.
trigger (v.)to cause something to start suddenly (يُطلِق / يتسبّب في) — e.g. A small earthquake triggered a landslide.
stem from (phr. v.)to come from; to be caused by (ينبع من) — e.g. Her interest in plants stems from childhood visits to her uncle's farm.
render (v.)to cause to be in a certain state (يجعل) — e.g. The flood rendered the road unusable for a week.
curb (v.)to control or limit (يحدّ من) — e.g. The city introduced new fees to curb traffic in the center.
induce (v.)to cause or bring about; to persuade (يُحدِث / يحثّ) — e.g. The medicine can induce sleepiness.

10. Describing ideas, arguments and evidence

Word / phraseMeaning / use
novel (adj.)new and original (جديد ومُبتكَر) — e.g. The students proposed a novel way to collect rainwater from rooftops.
conventional (adj.)usual, traditional (تقليدي) — e.g. Unlike conventional bricks, these are made from recycled plastic.
orthodox (adj.)generally accepted; traditional (مُتعارَف عليه) — e.g. Her method went against orthodox teaching at the time.
plausible (adj.)reasonable and likely to be true (معقول / قابل للتصديق) — e.g. Strong winds are a plausible explanation for the damage.
coherent (adj.)logical and well connected (مُترابِط) — e.g. The essay builds a coherent argument from start to finish.
comprehensive (adj.)including everything; complete (شامل) — e.g. The report gives a comprehensive survey of the region's rivers.
nuanced (adj.)showing small but important differences; not simple (دقيق / متعدّد الجوانب) — e.g. The book offers a nuanced view of the king, neither hero nor villain.
simplistic (adj.)too simple; ignoring important facts (مُبسَّط بشكل مُخِلّ) — e.g. Blaming one person for the crisis is simplistic.
rigorous (adj.)very careful and thorough (صارم / دقيق) — e.g. The drug passed rigorous safety tests.
arbitrary (adj.)based on chance or personal choice, not reason (عشوائي / تعسُّفي) — e.g. The rule seemed arbitrary because no one could explain it.
compelling (adj.)very convincing; demanding attention (مُقنِع) — e.g. The lawyer presented compelling evidence.
anecdotal (adj.)based on personal stories, not systematic study (قائم على قصص فردية) — e.g. The claim rests on anecdotal evidence from a few travellers.

11. Character and behaviour (literature passages)

Word / phraseMeaning / use
meticulous (adj.)giving great attention to every detail (دقيق جدًا) — e.g. The meticulous editor checked every date in the book.
diligent (adj.)hard-working and careful (مُجتهِد) — e.g. A diligent student reviews her mistakes every week.
tenacious (adj.)refusing to give up (مُثابِر) — e.g. The tenacious reporter followed the story for three years.
aloof (adj.)distant and unfriendly; not involved (مُتحفِّظ / مُنعزِل) — e.g. The new neighbor seemed aloof, but he was only shy.
reticent (adj.)not willing to talk about one's feelings or thoughts (كتوم / قليل الكلام) — e.g. The champion was reticent about her plans.
impetuous (adj.)acting quickly without thinking (مُتهوِّر) — e.g. In an impetuous moment, he sold the family's boat.
prudent (adj.)wise and careful about the future (حكيم / حَذِر) — e.g. It is prudent to save some money every month.
resilient (adj.)able to recover quickly from difficulty (مَرِن / سريع التعافي) — e.g. Desert plants are remarkably resilient.
complacent (adj.)too satisfied, so not careful enough (مُتهاوِن) — e.g. After two easy wins, the team became complacent.
magnanimous (adj.)generous and forgiving, especially to an opponent (كريم / متسامح) — e.g. The winner gave a magnanimous speech praising her rival.
obstinate (adj.)refusing to change one's opinion, unreasonably (عنيد) — e.g. The obstinate captain ignored every warning about the storm.
gregarious (adj.)enjoying the company of others (اجتماعي) — e.g. Gregarious by nature, she made friends on her first day.

Fast strategy for test day
  • Predict your own simple word first, then look for its academic version among the choices.
  • Check charge and strength using the groups on this page (raise doubt → weaken → prove wrong).
  • Unknown word? Use its parts (in-, dis-, re-, -cred-) to guess its charge and eliminate.
  • Never choose a hard word only because it looks advanced — it must fit the clue exactly.

Practice

Twelve Words in Context questions. Every correct answer — and many of the wrong choices — come from the toolkit above.

Remember
  • Learn words in meaning groups, with their charge (+/−) and strength.
  • Key opposites: corroborate / undermine, mitigate / exacerbate, foster / impede, ubiquitous / scarce.
  • Strength scale for weakening a claim: challenge → undermine → refute / debunk / overturn.
  • Ambivalent = mixed feelings; indifferent = no interest. Wry = gentle; sardonic = bitter.
  • Unknown word? Use word parts (in-, dis-, re-, -cred-, -spec-) to guess its meaning and eliminate.
