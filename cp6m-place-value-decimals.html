Place Value and Decimals to Thousandths

Learn what every digit in a decimal is worth, how to multiply and divide by 10, 100 and 1000, and how to split and regroup decimals to thousandths — with worked examples and a Checkpoint-style quiz.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Understand and explain what each digit in a decimal is worth: tenths, hundredths and thousandths (6Np.01)
  • Use place value to multiply and divide whole numbers and decimals by 10, 100 and 1000 (6Np.02)
  • Understand and explain the value of each digit when you build up (compose), split (decompose) and regroup numbers, including decimals to thousandths (6Np.03)

Place value is the idea that a digit's position decides its value. The digit 5 is worth 500 in 527, but only 5 hundredths (0.05) in 3.152. Everything in decimals — rounding, ordering, adding, multiplying — depends on this page, so learn it well.

1. The place value chart

Each column is worth 10 times the column on its right, and one tenth of the column on its left. This pattern carries on past the decimal point. Here is the number 3527.416 in a place value chart:

  • 10 thousandths = 1 hundredth (10 × 0.001 = 0.01)
  • 10 hundredths = 1 tenth (10 × 0.01 = 0.1)
  • 10 tenths = 1 one (10 × 0.1 = 1)
  • So 1 = 10 tenths = 100 hundredths = 1000 thousandths.

Reading decimals aloud. Say the digits after the point one at a time: 3.416 is "three point four one six", not "three point four hundred and sixteen". You can also say it as a fraction: 3 and 416 thousandths.

Zero as a placeholder. 0.5, 0.05 and 0.005 are very different numbers. The zeros push the 5 into the hundredths or thousandths place. Zeros at the end of a decimal do not change its value: 0.5 = 0.50 = 0.500 (5 tenths = 50 hundredths = 500 thousandths).

2. Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1000

When you multiply or divide by 10, 100 or 1000, the digits move and the decimal point stays where it is. The digits keep their order; only their places change.

OperationDigits moveExample
× 101 place to the left4.65 × 10 = 46.5
× 1002 places to the left4.65 × 100 = 465
× 10003 places to the left4.65 × 1000 = 4650
÷ 101 place to the right38 ÷ 10 = 3.8
÷ 1002 places to the right38 ÷ 100 = 0.38
÷ 10003 places to the right38 ÷ 1000 = 0.038

Method (numbered steps):

  1. Count the zeros: 10 → 1 place, 100 → 2 places, 1000 → 3 places.
  2. Multiplying makes the number bigger, so move the digits to the left. Dividing makes it smaller, so move them to the right.
  3. Fill any empty places between the digits and the point with zeros (4.65 × 1000 = 4650; 38 ÷ 1000 = 0.038).
  4. Do not write zeros at the end of a decimal, or in front of a whole number: write 46.5, not 46.50, and 465, not 0465.
  5. Check with a sense of size: × 1000 should give a much bigger number; ÷ 1000 a much smaller one.

Missing-number questions. If 0.62 × ☐ = 620, look at how far the digits moved: the 6 moved from tenths to hundreds, which is 3 places, so ☐ = 1000. If ☐ ÷ 100 = 0.37, undo it: 0.37 × 100 = 37.

Where you meet this: changing units. 1 m = 100 cm, so 2.35 m = 2.35 × 100 = 235 cm. 1 kg = 1000 g, so 750 g = 750 ÷ 1000 = 0.75 kg.

3. Composing, decomposing and regrouping

Decomposing means writing a number as the sum of the values of its digits:

  • 6.284 = 6 + 0.2 + 0.08 + 0.004
  • 40.507 = 40 + 0.5 + 0.007 (no ones and no hundredths, so there is nothing to write for them)

Composing is the reverse. Put each part in its place. Be careful when the parts are given in a mixed order: 9 + 0.03 + 0.5 = 9.53 (the 0.5 is tenths, the 0.03 is hundredths). Using a place value chart stops you writing 9.035.

Regrouping means exchanging one unit for ten of the next smaller unit (or ten for one bigger). The number stays the same — only the way we split it changes. Here are some ways to make 2.586:

Way of writing itCheck

Counting how many tenths, hundredths or thousandths. 2.37 = 23.7 tenths = 237 hundredths = 2370 thousandths. To find how many hundredths, multiply by 100; how many thousandths, multiply by 1000.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Write the value of the digit 8 in 14.386. [1 mark]

  1. Name the places after the point: 3 is tenths, 8 is hundredths, 6 is thousandths.
  2. 8 hundredths = 8/100 = 0.08.

Answer: 0.08 (or 8 hundredths). Common mistake: writing "8" or "hundreds". Hundreds are on the left of the point; hundredths are on the right.

Worked example 2 — Complete: (a) 0.47 × 100 = ☐ (b) 0.47 × ☐ = 470 (c) 3.9 ÷ 100 = ☐ [3 marks]

  1. (a) × 100: digits move 2 places left. 4 tenths become 4 tens, 7 hundredths become 7 ones → 47.
  2. (b) 0.47 → 470: the 4 moved from tenths to hundreds = 3 places → 1000.
  3. (c) ÷ 100: digits move 2 places right. 3 ones become 3 hundredths → 0.039.

Common mistake: in (c), writing 0.39 (moving only one place) or 0.0039 (moving three). Count the zeros in 100: two.

Worked example 3 — Write 7.062 as the sum of the values of its digits. [1 mark]

7 is ones, 0 is tenths, 6 is hundredths, 2 is thousandths. Answer: 7 + 0.06 + 0.002. The zero tenths add nothing, so you do not need to write + 0. Common mistake: writing 7 + 0.6 + 0.2 — the 6 is hundredths, not tenths.

Worked example 4 — Complete these: (a) 5.43 = 4 + ☐ (b) 5.43 = 5 + 0.3 + ☐ [2 marks]

  1. (a) Take 4 away from 5.43: 5.43 − 4 = 1.43. (One of the ones has been regrouped into the second part.)
  2. (b) 5.43 = 5 + 0.43. You already have 0.3, and 0.43 − 0.3 = 0.13. (One tenth was regrouped as 10 hundredths: 0.1 + 0.03 = 0.13.)

Check: 4 + 1.43 = 5.43 ✓ and 5 + 0.3 + 0.13 = 5.43 ✓. Always add your parts back together to check.

Worked example 5 — A pack of 1000 sheets of paper is 94 mm thick. How thick is one sheet? Give your answer in mm. [1 mark]

One sheet = 94 ÷ 1000. The digits move 3 places to the right: 94 → 9.4 → 0.94 → 0.094. Answer: 0.094 mm. Check: 0.094 × 1000 = 94 ✓.

Worked example 6 — Sara says: "To multiply by 10 you just add a zero, so 2.5 × 10 = 2.50." Is she correct? Explain. [1 mark]

Answer: No. 2.50 has the same value as 2.5, so adding a zero after the decimal part changes nothing. When you multiply by 10 the digits move one place to the left: 2 ones become 2 tens and 5 tenths become 5 ones, so 2.5 × 10 = 25. ("Add a zero" only works for whole numbers, such as 25 × 10 = 250.)

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up tens and tenths, hundreds and hundredths. If it ends in -ths, it is after the decimal point.
  • "Adding a zero" or "moving the point" with decimals. Think: the point stays, the digits move.
  • Moving the digits the wrong number of places: count the zeros in 10, 100 or 1000.
  • Forgetting the placeholder zero: 38 ÷ 1000 is 0.038, not 0.38.
  • Composing parts in the order they are written: 9 + 0.03 + 0.5 is 9.53, not 9.035.
  • Thinking 0.125 is bigger than 0.5 because 125 is bigger than 5. Compare place by place: 1 tenth is less than 5 tenths.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • "Write the value of the digit …" — give the value (0.08), not just the place name, unless the question asks for the place.
  • Missing-number boxes: 0.6 × ☐ = 600, or ☐ ÷ 100 = 0.35. Work out how many places the digits moved, or use the inverse.
  • Completing a partition: 4.276 = 4 + 0.2 + ☐ + 0.006, or a regrouped one such as 4.276 = 4 + 0.1 + ☐ + 0.006 (answer 0.17).
  • "Ring the number that is 100 times smaller than …" or matching lines between calculations and answers.
  • Reading a point on a number line that goes up in hundredths or thousandths: first work out what one small interval is worth.
  • "Explain why … is wrong" — name the place values in your explanation ("the 5 is in the tenths place, so …").
  • Paper 1 has no calculator, so you must be quick and accurate with × and ÷ by 10, 100 and 1000 in your head.

Practice

Remember
  • After the point: tenths (0.1), hundredths (0.01), thousandths (0.001). Each place is 10 times the place on its right.
  • × 10, 100, 1000: digits move 1, 2, 3 places left. ÷ 10, 100, 1000: digits move 1, 2, 3 places right. The point stays still.
  • Use zeros as placeholders (0.038, 4050), but zeros at the end of a decimal change nothing (0.5 = 0.50).
  • Decompose by the value of each digit: 6.284 = 6 + 0.2 + 0.08 + 0.004.
  • Regroup by exchanging: 1 = 10 tenths, 0.1 = 10 hundredths, 0.01 = 10 thousandths. Always add your parts back to check.
