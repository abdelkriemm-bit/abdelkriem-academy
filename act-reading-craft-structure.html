Craft and Structure

Vocabulary in context, author's purpose and point of view, the function of paragraphs and sentences, narrator's perspective and tone on ACT Reading, with worked examples and a full humanities practice passage.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Craft and Structure questions ask how the passage is written and why the author made certain choices, not just what it says. They make up 26–33% of the scored ACT Reading questions, roughly 7 to 9 of the 27 scored questions, and they appear in every passage type.

Sub-typeTypical stem (in our own words)What you must decide
Vocabulary in context"As it is used in line 12 / paragraph 3, the word x most nearly means:"The meaning in THIS sentence, not the dictionary's first meaning
Purpose of the passage"The main purpose of the passage is to:"Why the author wrote the whole text (explain, argue, reflect, describe…)
Function of a part"The third paragraph mainly functions to:" / "The author includes the story about X in order to:"What job the part does in the whole: example, contrast, objection, conclusion, transition
Point of view"The author would most likely agree that:" / "The author's attitude toward X is:"What the author thinks, based on his evaluative words
Narrator's perspective"The narrator's point of view is that of:"Who tells the story, and from what moment in time
Tone"The tone of the final paragraph is best described as:"The feeling in the word choice: admiring, critical, nostalgic, ironic, neutral…
Text structure"Which choice best describes how the passage is organized?"The order of the moves the author makes

The method, step by step

A. Vocabulary in context

  1. Cover the choices. Reread the full sentence (and the one before it).
  2. Replace the word with a blank and put your OWN simple word in the blank.
  3. Plug each choice into the sentence. The correct one keeps the exact meaning of the sentence.
  4. Be suspicious of the most common meaning of the word: ACT often tests a less common meaning ("carried two voices" = contained, not transported).

B. Purpose and function

  1. Ask: what is the paragraph (or sentence) doing? Use a verb: introduces, illustrates, contrasts, concedes, objects, concludes.
  2. Look at its neighbours. A paragraph that comes right before "And yet" or "However" is often a concession; a paragraph that starts "For example" is an illustration.
  3. Choose the answer that describes the JOB, not just the topic. "Describes printed signs" is a topic; "acknowledges the advantages of printed signs before turning to what is lost" is a job.

C. Point of view, tone and narrator

  1. Collect the author's evaluative words: foolish, sensible, generous, sad, a small pleasure. They show the attitude.
  2. Watch for mixed attitudes. ACT authors are often balanced: "admiring but troubled", "critical but fair". Extreme tones (furious, ecstatic) are rarely correct.
  3. For narrators, decide two things: Is the narrator a character ("I") or outside the story? Is he telling it as it happens, or looking back from later ("years later", "now")?

Worked examples

Excerpt 1 (humanities, shorter than a real ACT passage):

[1] Musicians who play the oud often say that the instrument is never finished. A new oud, however carefully built, sounds thin for its first year; the wood must be played into its voice. [2] Makers are sometimes asked to speed up this process, and a few have tried, leaving new instruments beside loudspeakers for weeks. The results, most players agree, are disappointing. An oud, it seems, learns from hands rather than from noise. [3] This may be why an old instrument is so often passed from teacher to student rather than sold. What is handed over is not only wood and strings but years of someone else's playing.

Worked example 1 — vocabulary in context

Question: As it is used in paragraph 1, the word voice most nearly means:

  • A) opinion.
  • B) characteristic sound.
  • C) singer.
  • D) right to vote.

Answer: B. The oud "sounds thin" at first; the wood must be played until it develops its full sound. Put your own word in the blank: "played into its ______" → "proper sound". B matches.

  • A is wrong — A real meaning of "voice" ("a voice in the decision") but an instrument has no opinions.
  • C is wrong — Confuses the instrument with a person who sings.
  • D is wrong — Another real meaning ("citizens have a voice") that has nothing to do with sound here.

Worked example 2 — function of a sentence

Question: The sentence "An oud, it seems, learns from hands rather than from noise" (paragraph 2) mainly serves to:

  • A) describe how loudspeakers are built.
  • B) prove that all modern instrument makers are dishonest.
  • C) state the conclusion suggested by the makers' failed experiment.
  • D) introduce a new topic that is developed in paragraph 3.

Answer: C. The paragraph gives an experiment (loudspeakers), its result ("disappointing"), and then this sentence, which draws the lesson. Its job = conclusion of the paragraph.

  • A is wrong — Off-topic: nothing explains how loudspeakers are built.
  • B is wrong — Too extreme: only "a few" makers tried, and dishonesty is never mentioned.
  • D is wrong — Half-right at best: paragraph 3 develops the idea, but the sentence's main job is to conclude paragraph 2, not to start a new topic.

Worked example 3 — tone / attitude

Question: The author's attitude toward the tradition described in paragraph 3 is best described as:

  • A) respectful.
  • B) mocking.
  • C) indifferent.
  • D) alarmed.

Answer: A. Handing over "years of someone else's playing" presents the tradition as meaningful and valuable. The tone is respectful.

  • B is wrong — Nothing in the wording makes fun of the tradition.
  • C is wrong — The author clearly cares: he explains the value of what is passed on.
  • D is wrong — Too extreme: no danger or worry is expressed.

Excerpt 2 (literary narrative):

When I was nine, I believed my grandfather was the strongest man in Mansoura. He carried sacks of rice up three flights of stairs, pausing only on the second landing to "look at the view," and he laughed at me when I tried to lift one. It was only years later, sorting through his papers, that I found the doctor's letters and understood what the view had been for.

Worked example 4 — narrator's perspective

Question: The narrator's point of view is best described as that of:

  • A) a nine-year-old boy describing events as they happen.
  • B) an outside observer who did not know the grandfather.
  • C) the grandfather remembering his own childhood.
  • D) an adult looking back who now understands something he missed as a child.

Answer: D. "When I was nine, I believed…" and "It was only years later … that I … understood" show an older narrator looking back. The adult now knows that the grandfather paused because he was ill.

  • A is wrong — Reversed time frame: the past tense and "years later" show he is no longer nine.
  • B is wrong — Wrong narrator type: "my grandfather" shows a family member telling the story in the first person.
  • C is wrong — Confuses the characters: the grandfather is described, not speaking.
Classic traps
  • The common-meaning trap in vocabulary questions: the most familiar meaning of the word is offered first and is usually wrong.
  • Topic instead of function. "It describes X" names a topic; the correct answer usually names the job (illustrates, concedes, contrasts, concludes).
  • Extreme tone. ACT authors are rarely furious, ecstatic or contemptuous. Prefer measured tones: appreciative, concerned, ambivalent.
  • Ignoring "but". When an author praises and then says "but", the answer must include both halves ("admiring but troubled").
  • Mixing the author's view with a quoted view. The elderly painter's opinion is not the author's opinion.
  • Child vs adult narrator. "Years later" and the present tense ("I am an engineer now") mean the narrator is looking back.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Vocabulary: blank out the word, predict your own simple word, then plug in each choice.
  • Function: name the job with a verb before you read the choices.
  • Point of view: underline (mentally) every evaluative word the author uses.
  • Structure: use your 3-word map; the right answer follows the order of the paragraphs.
  • Tone: pick the choice that is moderate and fits ALL the clues, not just one sentence.

Practice

Now try a full humanities passage (about 700 words) with 9 Craft and Structure questions. It is untimed, but aim for about 10 minutes. Before each answer, name the job, the attitude or your own replacement word.

Remember
  • Craft and Structure = 26–33% of scored ACT Reading questions.
  • Vocabulary in context: the meaning in this sentence, not the first dictionary meaning.
  • Function questions want the job a part does: illustrate, concede, contrast, object, conclude.
  • Point of view and tone come from the author's own evaluative words; ACT authors are usually balanced, not extreme.
  • For narrators, decide who is speaking and from what point in time.
