Factors, Multiples and Tests of Divisibility

Find factors, multiples, common factors and common multiples, and use quick tests to see whether a number is divisible by 3, 6 or 9.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Understand common multiples and common factors of two numbers, and use them to solve problems (6Ni.06).
  • Use what you know about factors and multiples to understand and use the tests of divisibility by 3, 6 and 9 (6Ni.07).

Factors and multiples are the "family members" of a number. They help you simplify fractions, find common denominators, check divisions and solve real problems such as "When will the two buses leave together again?". On this page you will also learn quick tests that tell you if a big number can be divided by 3, 6 or 9 — without dividing!

Factor or multiple? Factors are small (they fit into the number): factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Multiples are big (the number fits into them) and go on for ever: multiples of 12 are 12, 24, 36, 48, … The two ideas are linked: "4 is a factor of 24" means the same as "24 is a multiple of 4".

1. Finding all the factors of a number

Work in pairs so you never miss one.

  1. Start with 1 × the number: 1 × 36.
  2. Try 2, 3, 4, 5, … in order. If it divides exactly, write the pair: 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9. (5 does not divide 36.)
  3. Stop when the pairs meet or repeat: 6 × 6. The next one, 9 × 4, is already written.
  4. List the factors in order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.

2. Common factors

List the factors of both numbers, then find the numbers that appear in both lists. A Venn diagram shows this clearly: common factors go in the overlap.

1 is a common factor of every pair of numbers. The highest common factor is useful for simplifying fractions: 24/36 — divide top and bottom by 12 → 2/3.

3. Common multiples

List the first few multiples of each number until you find numbers in both lists.

  • Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, …
  • Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, …
  • Common multiples of 4 and 6: 12, 24, 36, … The lowest common multiple is 12. Every other common multiple is a multiple of 12.

Quick method for the lowest common multiple: go through the multiples of the bigger number and stop at the first one the smaller number divides. LCM of 6 and 8: 8 (no), 16 (no), 24 (yes, 24 ÷ 6 = 4). Careful: the LCM is not always the two numbers multiplied: 6 × 8 = 48 is a common multiple, but not the lowest.

4. Tests of divisibility

You already know some tests from earlier stages. Stage 6 adds the tests for 3, 6 and 9.

Divisible byTestExample
2the last digit is even (0, 2, 4, 6, 8)4 734 ✓
5the last digit is 0 or 57 425 ✓
10the last digit is 06 250 ✓
4the last two digits make a multiple of 43 516 ✓ (16 = 4 × 4)
3the digit sum is a multiple of 32 316: 2 + 3 + 1 + 6 = 12 ✓
9the digit sum is a multiple of 97 425: 7 + 4 + 2 + 5 = 18 ✓
6the number is even AND passes the test for 34 734: even ✓, 4 + 7 + 3 + 4 = 18 ✓

If the digit sum is still big, add its digits again: 5 976 → 5 + 9 + 7 + 6 = 27 → 2 + 7 = 9, so 5 976 is divisible by 9 (and by 3).

Why does the digit sum work? 10 = 9 + 1, 100 = 99 + 1, 1000 = 999 + 1. So 432 = 4 × 100 + 3 × 10 + 2 = (4 × 99 + 3 × 9) + (4 + 3 + 2). The part in the first bracket is always a multiple of 9 (and so of 3). So 432 is divisible by 9 exactly when the digit sum 4 + 3 + 2 = 9 is. This is a good example of convincing — explaining why a rule is always true.

Why does the test for 6 need two parts? 6 = 2 × 3, so a multiple of 6 must be a multiple of 2 and of 3. 15 passes the test for 3 but is odd, so it is not divisible by 6. 14 is even but its digit sum is 5, so it is not divisible by 6.

  • Every multiple of 9 is a multiple of 3 (9 = 3 × 3). But not every multiple of 3 is a multiple of 9: 12, 15, 21 are counter-examples.
  • Every multiple of 6 is even and a multiple of 3.
  • A number with digit sum 9, 18, 27 … is divisible by 3 and 9.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 (1 mark) — Write all the factors of 48.

Pairs: 1 × 48, 2 × 24, 3 × 16, 4 × 12, 6 × 8 (5 and 7 do not divide 48; the next, 8 × 6, repeats). Answer: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48. Common mistake: forgetting 1 and 48 themselves.

Worked example 2 (1 mark) — Find the highest common factor of 18 and 30.

Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. Factors of 30: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30. Common: 1, 2, 3, 6. Answer: 6.

Worked example 3 (1 mark) — Write the two common multiples of 6 and 8 that are less than 50.

Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48. Which are divisible by 6? 24 and 48. Answer: 24 and 48.

Worked example 4 (1 mark) — Tick the numbers that are divisible by 6: 3 582 7 425 2 316 4 105

Common mistake: ticking 7 425 because its digit sum is 18. It is divisible by 3 and by 9, but it is odd, so it is not divisible by 6.

Worked example 5 (2 marks) — 73□ is a 3-digit number that is divisible by 6. Find all the possible missing digits.

It must be even: □ is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8. Digit sum 7 + 3 + □ = 10 + □ must be a multiple of 3: □ = 2 (sum 12) or □ = 8 (sum 18). Both are even. Answer: 2 or 8 (732 and 738). Check: 732 ÷ 6 = 122 ✓, 738 ÷ 6 = 123 ✓. Common mistake: also giving 5 (sum 15) — but 735 is odd.

Worked example 6 (1 mark) — Two lights flash at the same moment. One flashes every 8 seconds, the other every 12 seconds. After how many seconds will they next flash together?

Multiples of 12: 12, 24. 24 ÷ 8 = 3 ✓. Answer: 24 seconds (the lowest common multiple). Common mistake: 8 × 12 = 96 — that is a common multiple, but not the first one.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up factors and multiples. Factors of 12 are ≤ 12; multiples of 12 are ≥ 12.
  • Missing factor pairs — always work systematically from 1 upwards.
  • Forgetting 1 and the number itself as factors.
  • Using the digit-sum test for 6 without checking the number is even.
  • Thinking "divisible by 3" means "divisible by 9" — 12 is divisible by 3 but not 9.
  • Giving a common multiple that is not the lowest (multiplying the two numbers).
  • Using the last digit to test for 3 or 9 — those tests need the digit sum.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • "Write all the factors of …" / "Write a common factor of …" — list in order, in pairs.
  • "Write a number that is a multiple of 6 and a multiple of 9" — any common multiple (18, 36, 54 …) gets the mark.
  • Venn and Carroll diagrams: sort numbers by "factor of 24", "multiple of 3", "divisible by 6" … Check every number against both labels.
  • "Tick the numbers that are divisible by 9" — use the digit sum, write it next to each number.
  • Missing-digit questions: "□ is a digit. 5□4 is divisible by 9. Find the possible values." — there may be more than one answer.
  • "Is Sara correct? Explain." — give a counter-example (one number that breaks the rule) or a test result.
  • Word problems: "together again" = LCM; "largest equal groups / pieces" = HCF.

Practice

Remember
  • Find factors in pairs from 1 upwards; multiples are the times table and never end.
  • Common factor = in both factor lists (HCF = biggest); common multiple = in both multiple lists (LCM = smallest).
  • Divisible by 3 → digit sum is a multiple of 3. Divisible by 9 → digit sum is a multiple of 9.
  • Divisible by 6 → even AND digit sum a multiple of 3.
  • "Together again" → LCM. "Largest equal pieces/groups" → HCF.
