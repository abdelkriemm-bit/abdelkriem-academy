Transitions

The logic families, the exact meaning of every common transition, the before-and-after method, the same-family trap, four worked examples and a 12-question practice quiz.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Transition questions belong to Expression of Ideas, the domain that makes up about 20% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (usually 8–12 questions, shared between transitions and rhetorical synthesis). In each module they usually come near the end, after the grammar questions and just before the student-notes (rhetorical synthesis) questions.

Each question is a short text (25–150 words) with a blank at the start of a sentence or after a semicolon, and the stem "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?" The four choices are transition words or phrases such as *However,* / *Therefore,* / *For example,* / *Similarly,*. Grammar is never the issue — every choice is punctuated correctly. The question tests logic: what is the relationship between the idea before the blank and the idea after it?

The method: name the relationship before you look

  1. Cover the choices. If you read them first, every one will start to sound possible.
  2. Read the sentence BEFORE the blank and the sentence (or clause) AFTER it. In longer texts, read from the beginning — the logic may connect to an idea two sentences back.
  3. Name the relationship in your own simple words: "same direction, more", "opposite", "surprising — despite that", "because of that", "an example", "later in time", "the same thing in other words", "the same is true of something else".
  4. Uncover the choices and eliminate every family that does not match. Usually two or three choices disappear at once.
  5. If two choices are from the same family, choose the precise one using the exact meanings in the table below (for example, *by contrast* vs *nevertheless*, *meanwhile* vs *subsequently*).
  6. Read the full sentence with your choice. It should sound like something a careful writer would say.

The big table: exact meanings

Hard transition questions are decided by precise meaning. Learn what each word really requires in the text around it.

TransitionExact meaning — what the text must containExample
howevergeneral contrast: the next idea goes against or differs from the lastThe plan was popular. However, it was too expensive.
by contrast / in contrastcompares two different things side by side and shows how they differFrogs lay eggs in water. By contrast, the coqui lays them on land.
on the other handthe other side of the same question; often after "on the one hand" or a first pointThe job pays well. On the other hand, the hours are long.
converselythe reverse situation is also trueHeat expands metal; conversely, cold shrinks it.
insteadreplacement: the first option was rejected or did not happen, and this happened in its placeThe council considered a garage. Instead, it expanded bus service.
rathercorrects a wrong idea: not X but YThe sound is not a song. Rather, it is a warning call.
nevertheless / nonethelessdespite the problem just mentioned, the result still happened (surprising result)She was injured. Nevertheless, she finished the race.
still / even sosame as nevertheless, a little less formalThe tickets were costly. Still, the hall was full.
granted / admittedlythe writer admits a point against his or her own view, usually before answering itGranted, the ending is sudden. Yet it is memorable.
regardlessit does not matter; the thing mentioned has no effectSome fans complained. Regardless, the club kept the new kit.
moreover / furthermore / in additionadds another point in the same direction, usually supporting the same argumentTrees cool streets. Moreover, they absorb noise.
in factadds a stronger or more surprising detail, or corrects a belief just mentionedPeople think octopuses live alone. In fact, some share dens.
indeedconfirms the previous statement and backs it up stronglyArabic shaped Spanish. Indeed, thousands of words come from it.
for example / for instancethe next sentence is one specific case of the general idea beforeSome foods were accidents. For example, …
specificallygives more exact detail about the same thing (not a new case)The team studied birds — specifically, desert owls.
in other words / that isrestates the previous idea in simpler or clearer words; no new informationThe drug had no effect. In other words, it did not work.
therefore / thus / consequently / as a resultthe next idea is a result or logical conclusion of the lastSunlight peaks at noon. Therefore, panels make most power then.
for this reason / accordinglybecause of the reason just given, this action was takenDemand rose. Accordingly, the factory added a shift.
likewise / similarlyanother subject does the same thing or has the same featureBees signal food by dancing. Similarly, ants leave trails.
meanwhileat the same time, usually somewhere else or in another situationWorkers dug the canal. Meanwhile, in Europe, ships were designed.
subsequently / afterward / laterafter the event just describedCarter found the tomb. Subsequently, he spent years recording it.
previously / earlierbefore the event just describedThe site is a park now. Previously, it was a factory.
eventually / ultimatelyin the end, after a long time or many stepsUltimately, the city chose the cheaper plan.
in summary / overallsums up the whole textOverall, the results support the theory.

Same family, different meaning: the pairs that decide hard questions

If you are choosing between…Choose the first when…Choose the second when…
however / by contrastone idea simply goes against anothertwo different subjects are compared on the same feature
by contrast / neverthelesstwo things are differentan obstacle did NOT stop a result ("despite this")
however / insteadsimple contrasta rejected option is replaced by another
moreover / in factjust one more supporting pointthe new point is stronger, surprising, or corrects a belief
for example / specificallya new case of a general groupmore detail about the very same thing
for example / indeedthe sentence is clearly one casethe sentence confirms and strengthens the claim
thus / moreoverthe second idea is caused by the firstthe second idea is just another, separate point
meanwhile / subsequentlyboth events happen at the same timethe second event happens later
similarly / likewise vs moreovera second subject shares the same featuremore information about the same subject

Worked examples

Worked example 1: cause and result

Mangrove forests along the Red Sea coast trap sand and mud with their tangled roots, which slows the erosion of the shoreline. ______ when mangroves are cleared for hotels and roads, nearby beaches often begin to shrink within a few years.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  • A) For instance,
  • B) Likewise,
  • C) Consequently,
  • D) Nevertheless,
Answer: C) Consequently,
  • Relationship in my own words: the roots protect the shore → so removing them makes beaches shrink. That is a result, so "Consequently" (C) is correct.
  • A is wrong: the second sentence is not one case of the first idea; it describes what happens when the protection is removed (wrong family: example).
  • B is wrong: "Likewise" needs another subject that does the same thing; nothing here is similar (wrong family: similarity).
  • D is wrong: "Nevertheless" signals a surprising result despite the first fact, but shrinking beaches are the expected result (reversed logic).

Worked example 2: concession vs contrast

The first electric buses tested in the city cost nearly twice as much as diesel buses. ______ the transport authority decided to buy fifty more, arguing that lower fuel and repair costs would save money over the buses' working lives.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  • A) Nevertheless,
  • B) By contrast,
  • C) Similarly,
  • D) For example,
Answer: A) Nevertheless,
  • Relationship: the buses were expensive → despite that, the authority bought more. A surprising action despite an obstacle = concession. "Nevertheless" (A).
  • B is the same-family trap: "By contrast" needs two different subjects compared side by side (for example, diesel buses vs electric buses on one feature). Here the second sentence is a decision made despite the cost.
  • C is wrong: nothing is similar to anything (wrong family).
  • D is wrong: buying fifty more buses is not an example of the high cost (wrong family).

Worked example 3: example

Some plants can defend themselves by releasing chemical signals into the air when they are attacked. ______ when caterpillars begin chewing on the leaves of a tomato plant, the plant releases scents that attract wasps that attack caterpillars.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  • A) In fact,
  • B) For instance,
  • C) Moreover,
  • D) Instead,
Answer: B) For instance,
  • Relationship: general claim (some plants release signals) → one specific case (the tomato plant). "For instance" (B).
  • A is wrong: "In fact" adds a stronger or surprising point or corrects a belief; the tomato is simply one case of the claim, not a stronger claim.
  • C is wrong: "Moreover" adds a separate new point; this sentence is a case of the same point (example vs addition).
  • D is wrong: "Instead" needs a rejected option; nothing is replaced.

Worked example 4: sequence in time

In November 1922, archaeologist Howard Carter uncovered the entrance to the tomb of Tutankhamun in the Valley of the Kings. ______ he spent nearly a decade carefully recording, photographing and removing the thousands of objects inside.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  • A) Meanwhile,
  • B) Previously,
  • C) Likewise,
  • D) Subsequently,
Answer: D) Subsequently,
  • Relationship: first he found the entrance → after that he spent years on the objects. Later in time = "Subsequently" (D).
  • A is the same-family trap: "Meanwhile" means at the same time, but the recording came after the discovery.
  • B is reversed: "Previously" means before the discovery, but he could not remove objects before finding the tomb.
  • C is wrong: no second subject does the same thing (wrong family).
Classic traps
  • Same-family trap: two contrast words or two time words. Use the exact meaning: by contrast (two subjects compared) ≠ nevertheless (despite an obstacle); meanwhile (same time) ≠ subsequently (later).
  • Reading only one sentence: the blank connects two ideas. Always read before AND after.
  • Reversed logic: choosing a result word (therefore) when the result is surprising (nevertheless), or vice versa.
  • "Moreover" for everything: addition is only right when the new sentence is another separate point in the same direction.
  • Instead without a rejected option: "instead" needs something that did not happen or was refused.
  • For example vs in fact / indeed: ask "Is the sentence one case of a general group?" If yes → example.
  • Sounding smart: "conversely", "accordingly" or "hence" are not better because they sound formal.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Cover the choices and say the relationship in two or three words.
  • Ask: same direction, opposite direction, or time? That splits the choices quickly.
  • Same direction → addition, emphasis, example, restatement, similarity, result. Opposite → contrast or concession.
  • If two words survive, use the pair table: who is being compared? Is there an obstacle? Same time or later?
  • Aim for about 45 seconds per question; transitions are short texts, so they help you save time.

Practice

Remember
  • Read the sentence before AND after the blank, and name the relationship before looking at the choices.
  • First decide the direction: same, opposite, or time.
  • By contrast compares two subjects; nevertheless/still means "despite this"; instead replaces a rejected option.
  • In fact adds something stronger or corrects a belief; indeed confirms; for example gives one case.
  • Meanwhile = same time; subsequently = later; previously = before.
