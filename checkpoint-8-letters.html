Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Informal and Formal Letters

How to write informal and formal letters for Checkpoint 8 Paper 1: British layout, greetings and sign-offs, structure, phrases and two strong 350+ word samples.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is a letter, and where does it appear?

A letter is a piece of writing addressed to one reader (or one organisation) and sent by post. It always has a recognisable layout: a greeting at the start, a sign-off at the end, and – depending on the type – addresses and a date at the top. In the Checkpoint 8 exam you meet letters in Paper 1 (Non-fiction), Section B Writing, worth 25 marks with about 35 minutes suggested. There are two kinds, and you must decide which one the task needs before you write a word.

Informal letterFormal letter
Who is it to?Someone you know well: a friend, cousin, grandparent, pen friend.Someone you do not know personally, or someone in authority: a headteacher, a manager, a council, a company, a newspaper editor.
PurposeTo share news, thank, invite, advise, describe an experience, keep in touch.To complain, request, apply, suggest, persuade, give an opinion on a public issue.
Tone (register)Warm, chatty, personal. Contractions (I'm, can't) and light humour are fine.Polite, serious, precise. No contractions, no slang, no jokes, no emojis.
GreetingDear Omar, / Hi Omar,Dear Sir or Madam, (name unknown) / Dear Mr Saleh, (name known)
Sign-offLots of love, / Best wishes, / Take care, + first name onlyYours faithfully, (after Dear Sir or Madam) / Yours sincerely, (after Dear Mr Saleh) + full name
Where letters sit in the exam
  • Paper 1 is Non-fiction: Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks), 1 hour 10 minutes for the whole paper.
  • A letter can be named directly ("Write a letter to your headteacher…"). Persuasive or complaint letters and formal or informal letters are text types in the framework, so they can appear – but you cannot predict which text type will come in a given series.
  • Golden rule from the G.A.A.P. technique: only write a letter if the task names a letter. If it names an article or gives no format, do not invent one.

2. How a letter is marked (out of 25)

Your letter is marked with the same five categories as every Checkpoint writing task. Here is what each band asks for, in simple words, and what it means for a letter.

Category (official name)MarksWhat the bands ask for (in simple words)What it means for this genre
Creation of texts (older name: content, purpose and audience)5Bands 1 · 2 · 3 · 4–5. Low: little link to the task. Middle: relevant, some points developed. Top: every point in the task developed in detail, a clear viewpoint, the right tone for the reader all the way through, and the reader stays interested.Cover every bullet point in the task, in detail. Keep one register from "Dear…" to the sign-off. Show the reader you know who they are (a friend's family, a manager's problem).
Vocabulary and language31 = simple, repeated words · 2 = some well-chosen words that suit the text type · 3 = precise, ambitious vocabulary chosen for its effect on the reader.Informal: lively, natural words and idioms (I can't stop grinning). Formal: precise, polite words (express my concern, reconsider, alternatively).
Structure of texts (older name: text structure)7Bands 1 · 2–3 · 4–5 · 6–7. Low: few or no paragraphs. Middle: paragraphs in a sensible order with simple links. Top: well-controlled paragraphs, smooth links between them, an effective opening and ending, and the correct layout for the text type.Correct layout (address, date, greeting, sign-off), one point per paragraph, an opening that states why you are writing and a closing that asks for a reply or action.
Grammar and punctuation (older name: sentence structure and punctuation)7Bands 1 · 2–3 · 4–5 · 6–7. Low: mostly simple sentences with errors. Middle: some variety, mostly accurate verbs and commas. Top: a wide range of sentence types used for effect, accurate verb forms and a range of punctuation used correctly.Sentences: mix simple, compound and complex sentences. Punctuation: commas after fronted clauses, apostrophes in contractions (informal), and a semicolon, colon or pair of dashes used correctly.
Word structure (spelling)31 = many common words wrong · 2 = most common words right · 3 = nearly all words right, including longer, ambitious words.Spell the letter words correctly: sincerely, faithfully, grateful, convenience, separate, received, recommend.
Three marking rules you must know
  • Wrong text type = heavy penalty. If the task asks for one text type and you write another, Creation of texts is capped at 1 mark and Structure of texts is capped too (at 3, or 2 if the content is also off-task). Layout is not decoration: it protects your marks.
  • Length: 20 words or fewer scores 0, and 21–60 words can score no more than 7 out of 25. A full answer is several developed paragraphs.
  • Your plan is not marked. Plan quickly, then spend your time on the writing itself.

3. Features of letters

Learn the layout features first – they are the easiest marks to protect – then the language features.

FeatureInformal letter – exampleFormal letter – example
Sender's address (top right)Optional between friends – often just the address or the town.Always: 15 Lotus Gardens, New Cairo, Cairo (your name does NOT go here).
Date (under your address)3 June 202614 April 2026 (British style: day, month, year)
Recipient's name and address (left side, below the date)Not used.The Manager, Riverside Sports Club, 3 Nile View Road, Maadi, Cairo
Greeting (salutation) – on its own line, followed by a commaDear Omar,Dear Sir or Madam, / Dear Mrs Mansour,
Subject line (optional, formal only)Not used.Re: Closure of the swimming pool to junior members
Opening paragraphAsk about the reader, apologise for not writing, say why you are writing.State your purpose in the first sentence: I am writing to…
Body paragraphsNews and details, one bullet point per paragraph, chatty asides in brackets.Reasons, evidence and suggestions, one point per paragraph, linked with formal connectives.
Closing paragraphAsk them to write back and send regards to the family.Polite request for action + thanks: I look forward to hearing from you.
Sign-off + comma on one line, name on the next lineLots of love, / YoussefYours faithfully, / signature / Karim Adel
LanguageContractions, questions to the reader, exclamations, idioms, humour.Full forms (I am, do not), formal connectives (Firstly, However, Furthermore), modal verbs for polite requests (could, would).
British conventions examiners notice
  • Dear Sir or Madam (you do not know the name) → Yours faithfully. Memory trick: never put two S words together – Dear Sir … Yours sincerely is wrong.
  • Dear Mr Saleh / Dear Mrs Mansour / Dear Dr Fahmy (you know the name) → Yours sincerely.
  • In British English, Mr, Mrs, Ms and Dr are usually written without a full stop. Use the surname, never the first name: Dear Mr Saleh, not Dear Mr Ahmed Saleh or Dear Ahmed.
  • Capital Y in Yours, small letters in faithfully and sincerely, and a comma after the sign-off.
  • The greeting and the sign-off each go on their own line; your name goes on the line below the sign-off – first name only for informal letters, full name for formal letters.

4. How to structure a letter

Both types follow the same skeleton; only the tone and the content change. Each bullet point in the task normally becomes one body paragraph.

PartInformal letter – what to writeFormal letter – what to write
LayoutYour address (optional) and the date, top right.Your address and the date top right, and the recipient's title/name and address on the left.
GreetingDear / Hi / Hello + first name + comma (no comma after Hi itself).Dear Sir or Madam, or Dear + title + surname + comma. Optional Re: line.
Paragraph 1 – openingHow are you? Mention the reader's life or family, apologise for not writing for so long and give a reason, then say why you are writing now.Who you are and why you are writing, in one or two sentences: I am writing, as a…, to…
Paragraph 2 – first bullet pointDevelop it with details, feelings and an example.Explain the problem or first point with evidence (numbers, times, examples).
Paragraph 3 – second bullet pointDescribe, explain or tell a short story, and add a question to the reader.Second reason or effect. Show you understand the other side if relevant.
Paragraph 4 – third bullet pointAdvice, plans or an invitation.Your suggestions or solution, clearly and politely.
Closing paragraphWrap up warmly, ask for a reply, send regards.Polite request for action, mention anything enclosed, thank them.
Sign-offLots of love, / Best wishes, + first name on the next line.Yours faithfully, / Yours sincerely, + signature + full name.
  1. Read the task and decide: Do I know this person well? → informal. Is it an official person or organisation? → formal.
  2. Underline every bullet point. Each becomes a paragraph.
  3. Decide the greeting and the matching sign-off before you start, so you do not forget.
  4. Write the layout first (address, date, greeting), then the opening paragraph.
  5. Develop each body paragraph with at least three sentences: the point, the detail, the example or feeling.
  6. Finish with a closing paragraph and the correct sign-off.

5. How to start and how to end

Informal openings

  • How are you? I hope you and your family are all well.
  • I'm so sorry I haven't written for ages; exams have taken over my life!
  • Thank you so much for your letter – it made my whole week.
  • You won't believe what happened last weekend!
  • I've got some amazing news, and you're the first person I wanted to tell.

Formal openings

  • I am writing to express my concern about…
  • I am writing, as a student at…, to request…
  • I am writing to enquire about…
  • I would like to bring to your attention a problem that…
  • I am writing in response to your recent announcement regarding…

Informal closings

  • Anyway, I'd better stop before this letter turns into a book!
  • Write back soon and tell me all your news.
  • I can't wait to see you – only six weeks to go!
  • Give my love to everyone at home.
  • Lots of love, / Best wishes, / Take care, / See you soon,

Formal closings

  • I would be grateful if you could consider these suggestions.
  • I have enclosed… for your reference.
  • Should you require any further information, please do not hesitate to contact me.
  • Thank you for your time and consideration.
  • I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience.

6. Useful phrases and strong vocabulary

Informal: linking and chatting

  • Anyway, back to my news…
  • By the way, have you heard that…?
  • Guess what?
  • Honestly, I couldn't believe it.
  • You'll never guess who I bumped into…
  • Oh, and before I forget…

Informal: inviting and advising

  • Why don't you come over in the holidays?
  • How about we…?
  • If I were you, I'd…
  • You really must try…
  • Make sure you bring…

Formal: giving reasons

  • Firstly, … Secondly, … Finally, …
  • Furthermore, / In addition, / Moreover,
  • As a result, / Consequently,
  • This is particularly important because…
  • For example, / For instance,

Formal: polite requests and suggestions

  • I would be grateful if you could…
  • Would it be possible to…?
  • I would like to suggest that…
  • Alternatively, the club could…
  • I fully understand that… However, …

8. Strong sample 1 – informal letter

Informal letter: a cousin's visit405 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your cousin, who lives in another country, is coming to stay with your family for two weeks in the summer holidays. Write a letter to your cousin. In your letter you should: • say how you feel about the visit • describe two places you will take your cousin and explain why • give advice about what to bring

18 Al-Yasmine Street, Palm Gardens, Cairo

3 June 2026

Dear Omar,

How are you? I hope you, Aunt Salma and the twins are all well, and that the Manchester rain has finally given you a break. I'm really sorry I haven't written for so long; the end-of-year exams swallowed my whole life, and I've only just come up for air. Last night, however, Mum told me the best news of the year, and I had to write straight away: you're coming to stay with us for two whole weeks in August!

Honestly, I can't stop grinning. The last time you visited, we were both eight, and all I can remember is the two of us arguing over the television remote. This time will be completely different. I've already cleared half of my wardrobe for you (a huge sacrifice, believe me), and Dad has promised to take a few days off work so that we can explore properly.

There are two places I'm desperate to show you. The first is the Grand Egyptian Museum, which stands close to the Pyramids of Giza. As soon as you walk in, a colossal statue of Ramesses II towers over you, and then a gigantic staircase lined with ancient kings leads you up towards a window with a view of the pyramids themselves. You love history more than anyone I know, so I'm sure you'll want to spend the whole day there. The second place is Alexandria. We'll take the early train, walk along the Corniche while the sea breeze cools us down, explore the Citadel of Qaitbay and finish with grilled fish at a tiny restaurant my grandfather swears is the best in Egypt.

Before you pack, here's some friendly advice. August in Cairo is scorching – often above 35 degrees – so bring light cotton clothes, a hat, sunscreen and a refillable water bottle. You'll also need comfortable trainers, because we'll be walking for hours, and your swimming things for Alexandria. Oh, and don't forget your football boots: I've told all my friends you're a star striker, so please don't make me a liar!

Anyway, I'd better stop here before this letter turns into a book. Write back soon and tell me what you'd most like to do; I'll make sure it's on our list. Give my love to everyone, and tell the twins I'm saving them a box of my mum's famous basbousa.

Lots of love,

Youssef

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: all three bullet points are covered and developed – feelings (paragraph 2), two places with reasons (paragraph 3), advice on packing (paragraph 4). The writer knows the reader (Aunt Salma, the twins, Omar's love of history), which builds a real writer–reader relationship.
  • Vocabulary 3/3: lively, precise word choices that suit a friend – swallowed my whole life, come up for air, colossal, towers over you, scorching, desperate.
  • Structure 7/7: correct informal layout (address and date top right, Dear Omar, on its own line, Lots of love, + first name on the next line). The opening asks about the reader, apologises for not writing and gives a reason. Each body paragraph handles one bullet point, and the closing asks for a reply. Links between paragraphs: This time will be completely different… / Before you pack… / Anyway…
  • Grammar & punctuation – sentences: a range of sentence types – a short simple sentence for effect (Honestly, I can't stop grinning.), compound sentences and complex sentences (As soon as you walk in, a colossal statue…).
  • Grammar & punctuation – punctuation: accurate apostrophes in contractions, a colon for explanation (I had to write straight away: you're coming…), a semicolon joining two related sentences (…for so long; the end-of-year exams…), brackets for extra information, and a pair of dashes for extra information (– often above 35 degrees –). Together these earn 7/7.
  • Spelling 3/3: ambitious words spelt correctly – sacrifice, colossal, gigantic, refillable, restaurant, grandfather, comfortable.
  • Note: the address and date are shown on single lines here; in the exam, write each part of the address on its own line in the top-right corner, with the date underneath.

9. Strong sample 2 – formal letter

Formal letter: a complaint with suggestions392 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your local sports club has announced that its swimming pool will be closed to members under sixteen on weekday evenings. Write a letter to the manager of the club. In your letter you should: • explain why this decision is a problem for young members • describe how it affects others • suggest a fairer solution

15 Lotus Gardens, New Cairo, Cairo

14 April 2026

The Manager, Riverside Sports Club, 3 Nile View Road, Maadi, Cairo

Dear Sir or Madam,

Re: Closure of the swimming pool to junior members on weekday evenings

I am writing, as a junior member of Riverside Sports Club for the past five years, to express my concern about the recent announcement that the swimming pool will be closed to members under sixteen between 5 p.m. and 9 p.m., Sunday to Thursday, from next month.

Firstly, this decision would make it almost impossible for young people to swim during the week. Most of us finish school at half past three and attend lessons or complete homework until five, so the evening is the only time we can train. The club's junior swimming team, which has won three regional medals this year alone, trains on exactly these evenings. If the new timetable goes ahead, the team will lose four of its five weekly sessions – and, very probably, its best swimmers.

Secondly, the closure affects far more than competitions. Swimming is one of the few activities that keeps teenagers active without the pressure of exams; it is also a skill that can save lives. Parents pay a full family membership on the understanding that every member, regardless of age, can use the facilities. Several families I have spoken to feel, quite understandably, that they would no longer be receiving the service they have paid for.

I fully understand that adult members have complained about crowded lanes in the evenings, and I agree that the pool can become uncomfortably busy. However, I believe there are fairer solutions. The club could reserve two lanes for juniors and the remaining lanes for adults; alternatively, junior members could be given three evenings a week instead of none. A supervised junior session from 5 p.m. to 6:30 p.m. would also keep the pool calmer for adults later in the evening.

I would be grateful if you could reconsider this decision before it takes effect. I have enclosed a petition signed by sixty-three junior members and their parents, and I would be happy to meet you, together with our swimming coach, to discuss these suggestions in more detail.

Thank you for your time and consideration. I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience.

Yours faithfully,

(signature)

Karim Adel

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: every bullet point has its own developed paragraph – the problem (paragraph 2), the wider effects (paragraph 3), fair solutions (paragraph 4). The viewpoint is clear but reasonable, and the writer shows understanding of the club's side (I fully understand that adult members have complained…), which persuades a formal reader.
  • Vocabulary 3/3: precise formal vocabulary – express my concern, regardless of age, quite understandably, reconsider, alternatively, enclosed, at your earliest convenience.
  • Structure 7/7: full British formal layout – sender's address and date, recipient's title and address, Dear Sir or Madam, a Re: subject line, and Yours faithfully, followed by the full name. The opening states who is writing and why in one sentence. Firstly, Secondly and However guide the reader, and the closing asks for action and thanks the reader.
  • Grammar & punctuation – sentences: no contractions anywhere, varied complex sentences (If the new timetable goes ahead, the team will lose…), a relative clause (…team, which has won three regional medals this year alone, trains…) and modal verbs for polite suggestions (could, would).
  • Grammar & punctuation – punctuation: a semicolon joining two related sentences (…without the pressure of exams; it is also a skill…), pairs of commas for extra information (feel, quite understandably, that…) and a single dash for emphasis (– and, very probably, its best swimmers). Together these earn 7/7.
  • Spelling 3/3: faithfully, convenience, consideration, petition, regional, understandably, alternatively – all correct.
  • Note: Dear Sir or Madam → Yours faithfully. If the task gave the manager's name, the letter would open Dear Mr Hassan, and close Yours sincerely,.

Dear Sir or Madam … Yours sincerelyWrong convention – a structure and accuracy slip a marker spots at once.Unknown name → faithfully. Known name → sincerely.

Hi, Omar, (a comma after Hi) or Dear Omar on the same line as the first sentenceLayout error.No comma after Hi itself: Hi Omar, or Dear Omar, on its own line, then start the letter on the next line.
Sign-off and name on the same line, or your full name in an informal letterLayout error.Sign-off + comma on one line, then the name below: first name only for friends.
Skipping one bullet pointCreation of texts cannot reach the top band.Tick each bullet point on the question paper as you write its paragraph.

11. Checklist before you hand in

  • I chose the right type (formal/informal) for the reader named in the task.
  • Address and date are top right; for a formal letter, the recipient's address is on the left.
  • Greeting + comma on its own line. Sign-off + comma on its own line, with my name below it.
  • Sir or Madam → Yours faithfully. A named person → Yours sincerely.
  • Every bullet point has its own developed paragraph.
  • The register is the same from the first line to the last.
  • I used at least three sentence types (simple, compound, complex).
  • I used at least one of: a semicolon joining two related sentences, a colon, a pair of dashes, brackets.
  • I checked the spelling of sincerely, faithfully, grateful and every ambitious word.

12. Practice tasks

  1. Task A (informal): Your best friend has just moved to a new school in another city. Write a letter to your friend. You should: • tell them your news from your class • ask about their new school • suggest a plan for the next holiday. Write a quick plan.

    Show answer
    Plan – Layout: address + date top right, Dear Karim, → Lots of love, / Best wishes, + first name. P1: How are you? Sorry for not writing sooner (football tournament). Writing because I miss you. P2: class news – new science teacher, the class trip to the Fayoum desert, a funny moment. P3: questions about the new school – friends, teachers, is the food better than ours? P4: holiday plan – you stay with us for a week in the mid-year break, with cycling in the park, a football match. P5: write back soon, regards to your parents. Words to use: hilarious, unforgettable, I can't wait. Punctuation: brackets for an aside, a semicolon joining two related sentences.

  2. Task B (formal): Your town council plans to turn the only public park in your area into a car park. Write a letter to the head of the council, Mr Tarek Shawky. You should: • explain why the park matters to local people • describe what would be lost • suggest an alternative. Which greeting and sign-off do you use, and what goes in each paragraph?

    Show answer
    Greeting: Dear Mr Shawky, → Yours sincerely, (the name is known), then your full name. Layout: your address and date top right. Mr Tarek Shawky, Head of … Council, address on the left. Re: Proposed car park on … Park. P1: I am writing, as a resident of …, to express my concern about… P2: why it matters – the only green space, children, elderly people walking, clean air. P3: what would be lost – trees, the playground, community events, plus one number for evidence. P4: alternatives – a multi-storey car park on the empty land near the market, or better bus services. P5: I would be grateful if you could reconsider… I look forward to hearing from you.

  3. Correct the layout errors: "Hi Omar, how are you? … Love Youssef Ahmed."

    Show answer
    Hi Omar, (or Dear Omar,) on its own line, then How are you? on the next line. The ending should be: Love, (with a comma, on its own line) and then Youssef on the line below – first name only in an informal letter.

  4. A student writes: "Dear Sir or Madam, I'm writing cos the pool is closed and it's so unfair!!! Yours sincerely, Karim." Give four corrections.

    Show answer
    1) I'm → I am, and cos → because (formal register, no contractions or slang). 2) so unfair!!! → one polite, precise statement, e.g. I believe this decision is unfair to junior members. (no multiple exclamation marks). 3) Yours sincerely → Yours faithfully, because the letter starts Dear Sir or Madam. 4) Karim → full name, Karim Adel, on the line below the sign-off.
