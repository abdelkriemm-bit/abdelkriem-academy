Cross-Text Connections

Master the paired-text questions: summarise each text, name the relationship between them, and predict how one author would respond to the other.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Cross-Text Connections is the only SAT Reading and Writing question type with two passages. You read a short Text 1 and a short Text 2 on the same topic (each passage or pair of passages on the test is short: 25–150 words), and you answer one question about how the two texts relate. Usually one text makes a claim or reports a finding, and the other text responds to that topic in some way: by agreeing, disagreeing, adding a condition, or offering a different explanation.

Typical question stems (in our own words):

  • "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim in Text 1?"
  • "Based on the texts, how would [the researcher named in Text 2] most likely respond to the conclusion of the researchers in Text 1?"
  • "Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?"
  • "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely describe the approach discussed in Text 1?"

This skill is part of the Craft and Structure domain, with Words in Context and Text Structure and Purpose. Craft and Structure makes up about 28% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (13–15 questions across both modules). Cross-Text questions usually appear near the start of a module, among the other Craft and Structure questions.

The big idea

You are a referee in a conversation. Text 2's author has NOT read the question; you must imagine what that author would say to Text 1 using ONLY what Text 2 actually says. The correct answer is the most modest, best-supported reply.

The method in six steps

  1. Read the question first (just the stem). Note who responds to whom, and to what: "Text 2 → the claim in Text 1", or "both agree".
  2. Summarise Text 1 in one line: WHO claims WHAT, and on what basis. Example: "Ecologists: city foxes are bold because of inherited change."
  3. Summarise Text 2 in one line, focusing on what it says about the same point. Example: "Saleh: city cubs moved to the country became cautious → boldness is learned."
  4. Name the relationship with one word: agree, disagree, qualify, extend, alternative explanation, or question the method/evidence (see the table below).
  5. Predict the reply in your own words, as if Text 2's author were speaking: "You're right that city foxes are bold, but it's learning, not genes."
  6. Eliminate choices that misstate either text, swap who said what, use a reason Text 2 never gives, or are more extreme than Text 2.
Read Text 1 carefully too

Many wrong answers describe Text 2 correctly but describe Text 1 wrongly (or the other way around). An answer can only be right if it is true to BOTH texts.

The language of relationships

Text 2 almost never says "I disagree with Text 1." You must infer the relationship from signals. Learn these patterns:

RelationshipWhat Text 2 doesSignals in Text 2Typical right-answer wording
Agree / supportgives more evidence for the same claimsimilarly, also found, confirms, consistent with"would see the finding as consistent with her own results"
Disagree / rejectsays the claim is wronghowever, in fact, contrary to, this view ignores"would argue that the claim is mistaken because…"
Qualifyaccepts part of the claim but limits itit is true that… but; only when; not enough on its own"would agree in part but argue that… alone is not sufficient"
Extendapplies the claim to new cases or adds a further consequencealso, even, other species/groups, more broadly"would suggest that the effect also applies to…"
Alternative explanationaccepts the facts but explains them differentlyrather than, instead, may actually be due to"would argue that another factor better explains…"
Question the method / evidencesays the study cannot support its conclusioncannot rule out, did not account for, only a correlation, small sample"would caution that the evidence does not establish…"

Correlation and causation: a very common Text 2 move is to point out that Text 1 found two things happening together (more time outdoors and better mood) and then assumed that one causes the other. If Text 2 says the study "cannot rule out" another cause, the answer will be about the evidence being insufficient, not about the claim being false.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Alternative explanation (science)

Text 1: Some ecologists have argued that urban foxes are bolder than rural foxes because city life has favored individuals that are less fearful of people. In this view, generations of living near humans have changed fox behavior in a lasting, inherited way. Text 2: Biologist Omar Saleh studied young foxes born in cities and in the countryside. He found that city-born cubs that were moved to rural areas soon became as cautious as rural foxes. Saleh concludes that boldness in urban foxes is mainly learned from experience with people rather than inherited.

Question: Based on the texts, how would Saleh (Text 2) most likely respond to the ecologists' view presented in Text 1?

  • A) He would agree with it, since his study confirmed that city foxes are bolder than rural foxes.
  • B) He would argue that the boldness of urban foxes is better explained by learning than by inherited change.
  • C) He would claim that rural foxes are in fact bolder than urban foxes.
  • D) He would suggest that the ecologists studied too few foxes to reach any conclusion.

Summary lines: T1 = boldness inherited. T2 = boldness learned. Relationship = alternative explanation.

Answer: B

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Half-right. Saleh accepts that city foxes are bold, but he rejects the ecologists' EXPLANATION (inheritance). Agreeing with "the view" is wrong.
  • B — ✓ Correct. Text 1: bold because of inherited change. Text 2: cubs became cautious when moved → learned, "rather than inherited." Same facts, different explanation. Correct.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Reversed / unsupported. Neither text says rural foxes are bolder.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Wrong reason. Saleh never comments on sample size; he answers with his own experiment's result.

Worked example 2 — Qualify (literary criticism)

Text 1: Critics often praise the novelist Nadia Rostom for her realistic dialogue. Her characters, they say, speak exactly as ordinary people do, with all the hesitations and interruptions of real conversation. Text 2: Rostom's dialogue certainly feels natural on the page. But a careful reading shows that her characters rarely waste a word: every line, however casual it sounds, reveals something about the speaker or moves the plot forward. Real conversation is far less efficient. Rostom's achievement is not to copy everyday speech but to create the illusion of it.

Question: Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the critics' claim in Text 1?

  • A) By agreeing that Rostom's characters speak exactly as real people do
  • B) By arguing that Rostom's dialogue sounds unnatural and should be revised
  • C) By claiming that the critics have not actually read Rostom's novels
  • D) By accepting that the dialogue seems realistic while arguing that it is more carefully shaped than ordinary speech

Signal: "certainly… But…" is the qualify pattern: agree with the surface, correct the detail.

Answer: D

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Too much agreement. Text 2 says her dialogue is NOT exact copying: "Real conversation is far less efficient."
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Too extreme / reversed. Text 2 says the dialogue "certainly feels natural" and calls it an "achievement".
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Unsupported. Text 2 disagrees with an interpretation; it never accuses the critics of not reading.
  • D — ✓ Correct. "Certainly feels natural" (accepts) + "But… rarely waste a word" (limits) = qualify. Correct.

Worked example 3 — Both authors agree (education research)

Text 1: Several schools have moved the start of the school day for teenage students from 7:30 to 8:30 a.m. A study of three such schools found that afterward students slept longer and missed fewer first-period classes. The researchers recommend that other schools make the same change. Text 2: Sleep researcher Dina Fouad agrees that teenagers need more sleep but warns that later start times alone may not be enough. In her study, students who started school later but kept using their phones late at night gained little extra sleep. Fouad argues that schools should combine later start times with guidance about evening screen use.

Question: Based on the texts, the researchers in Text 1 and Fouad (Text 2) would most likely agree with which statement?

  • A) Teenagers' lack of sleep is worth addressing, and later school start times can be part of the solution.
  • B) Later school start times alone are enough to solve teenagers' sleep problems.
  • C) Phone use at night is the main cause of poor sleep among teenagers.
  • D) Schools should not change their start times until more research has been done.

Rule for "both agree" questions: find the overlap. The answer must be supported by Text 1 AND by Text 2. Anything mentioned in only one text is out.

Answer: A

  • A — ✓ Correct. Text 1 recommends later starts; Fouad wants later starts COMBINED with screen guidance. Both accept the problem and see later starts as useful. Correct.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Fouad rejects this directly: "alone may not be enough."
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Only Text 2 discusses phones, and even Fouad does not say phones are the MAIN cause. A "both agree" answer needs support in both texts.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Neither text says this; both actually support later start times.

Worked example 4 — Question the evidence (social science)

Text 1: Linguist Samir Haddad surveyed 2,000 university students and found that those who grew up speaking two languages scored higher on a test of attention control than those who grew up speaking one. Haddad concludes that bilingualism improves attention. Text 2: Studies like this can be hard to interpret. In many countries, children who grow up bilingual are more likely to come from families with higher incomes and more years of schooling, factors that are themselves linked to stronger test performance. Unless researchers account for such differences, they cannot be sure that bilingualism, rather than family background, explains the results.

Question: Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to Haddad's conclusion?

  • A) By pointing out that Haddad's test of attention control was too difficult for most students
  • B) By agreeing that bilingualism is the only factor that explains the students' scores
  • C) By cautioning that the higher scores might be explained by other differences between the two groups
  • D) By arguing that students who speak one language have better attention control than bilingual students

Answer: C

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Unsupported. Text 2 never discusses how hard the test was.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Reversed. Text 2's whole point is that OTHER factors might explain the scores.
  • C — ✓ Correct. Text 2 says researchers "cannot be sure" because of family background. That is a caution about evidence, not a rejection of the result. Correct.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Too extreme. Text 2 does not claim the opposite result; it only says the cause is uncertain.
Classic traps
  • Swapped texts: the choice gives Text 2's idea to Text 1, or describes Text 1 incorrectly.
  • Too strong: Text 2 only questions or qualifies, but the choice says it "rejects completely" or "proves wrong".
  • Too much agreement: Text 2 accepts the facts but not the explanation; a choice saying "would agree" is half-right.
  • New reason: the choice gives a criticism Text 2 never makes (sample size, cost, cruelty, test difficulty).
  • One-text-only ("both agree" questions): an idea mentioned in only one text cannot be something both authors agree on.
  • Off-topic response: the choice answers a different part of Text 1 than the one the question asks about.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Read the stem first so you know who is responding to what.
  • Write two summary lines: T1 = …, T2 = …. Then one relationship word.
  • Find Text 2's signal words: but, however, rather than, alone, cannot rule out, also, even.
  • Prefer the most modest choice that both texts fully support.
  • If stuck between two, test each against Text 1 as well as Text 2; the wrong one usually misstates Text 1. Never leave a question blank: there is no penalty for wrong answers.

Practice

Ten original paired-text questions in real test format. Summarise each text in one line, name the relationship, then choose.

Remember
  • Summarise each text in one line: who claims what, based on what.
  • Name the relationship: agree, disagree, qualify, extend, alternative explanation, or question the evidence.
  • Text 2 often accepts the facts but changes the explanation; "would agree" is then a half-right trap.
  • For "both agree", the answer must be supported by BOTH texts.
  • Choose the most modest reply that uses only what Text 2 actually says.
