Colons: After a Complete Sentence

When a colon is correct (after a complete sentence, for a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information), where it is never allowed, and how it compares with dashes and semicolons — with 10 practice questions.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Colon questions are tested in the Boundaries skill of the Standard English Conventions domain (about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, 11–15 questions). A colon can link two clauses or introduce a list or a supplement, so it appears in many boundary questions — sometimes as the key, very often as a trap placed right after a verb or after “such as.”

The format: a short passage (25–150 words) with a blank and the stem “Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?” The choices often show four marks in the same place — “ingredients;” / “ingredients:” / “ingredients,” / “ingredients” — and you must know exactly what a colon is allowed to do.

النقطتين بعد جملة كاملة بس

النقطتين (:) لازم قبلهم جملة كاملة تقدر تحط بعدها نقطة. بعدهم ممكن ييجي: قائمة، شرح، كلمة للتأكيد، أو معلومة زيادة. ممنوع تحطهم بعد فعل (were / includes) أو بعد حرف جر (for / of) أو بعد such as.

The rule: complete sentence first

The teacher's rule: a colon comes after a complete sentence (an independent clause) and introduces a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information about what came before it.

Job✓ Example
ListThe recipe needs only three ingredients: flour, water, and salt.
Explanation (a phrase)The match was cancelled for one reason: a power cut.
Explanation (a full clause)The results surprised the team: the smallest plants produced the most seeds.
Emphasis (often one word or name)After weeks of debate, the committee chose the project's name: Horizon.
Extra information / a quotationThe guide gave us one piece of advice: carry more water than you think you need. / The sign gave one instruction: “Do not touch the paintings.”

The period test before every colon: stop at the colon and read only the part before it. Could a period go there? If yes, the colon may be correct. If no, the colon is wrong — whatever comes after it.

The colon is an arrow

The first sentence makes a promise (“three ingredients,” “one reason,” “a simple conclusion,” “the following students”), and what comes after the colon delivers it. If nothing after the colon lists, explains or names what came before, the colon is not the right mark.

Where a colon is never allowed

Never put a colon …✗ Wrong✓ Right
after a verb (is, are, were, include, contains, needs)The winners were: Hana, Omar, and Rana.The winners were Hana, Omar, and Rana.
between a verb and its objectThe recipe needs: flour, water, and salt.The recipe needs three things: flour, water, and salt.
after a preposition (for, of, to, with, by, about)The recipe calls for: dates, honey, and almonds.The recipe calls for dates, honey, and almonds.
after such as / including / for example / likeCrops such as: rice and cotton grow in the delta.Crops such as rice and cotton grow in the delta.
after an incomplete sentence or a dependent clauseBecause the storm was coming: the ferry stayed in port.Because the storm was coming, the ferry stayed in port.
inside a list, or twice in one sentenceThe tour visits: Luxor: Aswan, and Cairo.The tour visits three cities: Luxor, Aswan, and Cairo.

How to make the left side complete: add a noun that names what is coming — “three things,” “the following students,” “several events,” “one reason.” Words like the following and as follows are made for colons: “The rules are as follows: …” ✓.

  • ✗ The festival includes: a poetry contest, a film night, and a book fair.
  • ✓ The festival includes several events: a poetry contest, a film night, and a book fair.
  • ✓ The festival includes a poetry contest, a film night, and a book fair. (no colon at all)
  • ✗ The festival includes several events, including: a poetry contest … (never after “including”)

Colon between two complete sentences

A colon can join two independent clauses when the second explains, proves or gives the result promised by the first: “The researcher's conclusion was simple: the birds were following the river.” The second clause answers the question the first one raises (What was the conclusion? Why? What exactly?).

  • ✓ The village had a serious problem: its only well had dried up.
  • ✓ The plan worked: within a year, the canal was full of fish again.
  • ✗ The village had a serious problem, its only well had dried up. (comma splice)
  • ✗ The village had a serious problem: because its only well had dried up. (dependent clause after the colon — no explanation sentence)

If two complete sentences are only side by side (two parallel facts) and the choices also offer a semicolon or a period, those are the safer choices. The test will not offer a correct colon and a correct semicolon as two different answers — if both appear, one of the choices has another error.

Colon, dash or semicolon?

After a complete sentence comes …Colon :Single dash —Semicolon ;
a list✓✓ (less common)✗
one word or phrase (emphasis)✓✓✗
a complete sentence that explains the first✓✓✓ (joins; does not explain)
a dependent clause (because …)✗✗✗

Colons and single dashes overlap: both can introduce a final word or phrase that gets emphasis. Because both are correct, a question normally offers only one of them. If a colon and a dash both appear in the choices, the other words in those choices will decide.

طريقة التأكد السريعة

غطّي اللي بعد النقطتين واقرا اللي قبلهم: لو جملة كاملة (فيها فاعل وفعل والمعنى كامل) يبقى ممكن. لو بتخلص بفعل أو حرف جر أو such as يبقى غلط أكيد. ولو اللي بعدهم بيشرح أو بيعدد أو بيأكد اللي قبلهم، يبقى النقطتين في مكانهم الصح.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — colon after an incomplete clause

Text: Members of a desert research expedition must carry everything they need for ten days, because there are no shops for hundreds of kilometers. The expedition's supply list included ______ water filters, solar lamps, and several kilograms of dried dates.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) included:
  • B) included
  • C) included;
  • D) included—

Answer: B

  • A) ✗ Wrong: “The expedition's supply list included” is not a complete sentence — the verb has no object. A colon cannot follow it.
  • B) ✓ Correct: No punctuation: the verb “included” goes straight to its object, the list.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon needs complete sentences on both sides; neither side is one.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: A dash cannot separate a verb from its object.

Worked example 2 — colon for an explanation

Text: The bus drivers' strike had been expected to last for weeks. Instead, it ended after only two days for one simple reason ______ the city agreed to all of the drivers' demands.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) reason; because
  • B) reason,
  • C) reason:
  • D) reason, which

Answer: C

  • A) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon cannot come before a dependent clause, and the left side promises a reason that should be stated directly.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: A comma here joins two independent clauses: comma splice.
  • C) ✓ Correct: The left side is complete and promises “one simple reason”; the colon introduces the clause that gives it.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: “Which the city agreed to …” does not explain the reason and makes the sentence ungrammatical.

Worked example 3 — the “such as” trap

Text: Fertile soil and water from the river make the north of Egypt one of the country's richest farming regions. Several crops ______ rice, cotton, and wheat, are grown in large quantities in the Nile Delta.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) , such as
  • B) such as:
  • C) , such as:
  • D) : such as

Answer: A

  • A) ✓ Correct: “Such as rice, cotton, and wheat” is extra information here, opened by a comma and closed by the comma after “wheat.” No colon is needed after “such as.”
  • B) ✗ Wrong: Never a colon after “such as,” and the extra information is closed by a comma but never opened.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: Opening comma ✓, but a colon after “such as” is never allowed.
  • D) ✗ Wrong: A colon after “Several crops” — not a complete sentence.

Worked example 4 — colon for emphasis

Text: The small engineering team had worked in a borrowed garage for three years, often without pay. Last month, they finally had what they had been working toward ______ a working prototype of their water-cleaning machine.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) toward; a
  • B) toward, it was a
  • C) toward a
  • D) toward: a

Answer: D

  • A) ✗ Wrong: A semicolon needs a complete sentence after it; “a working prototype …” is a phrase.
  • B) ✗ Wrong: “It was a working prototype” is an independent clause joined by only a comma: comma splice.
  • C) ✗ Wrong: With no mark, “what they had been working toward a working prototype” runs together and becomes confusing.
  • D) ✓ Correct: The left side is complete, and the colon introduces the phrase that names what they achieved — emphasis.
Classic traps
  • Colon after a verb: “The winners were:” / “The kit includes:” / “The list included:”.
  • Colon after a preposition: “calls for:” / “consists of:” / “made of:”.
  • Colon after “such as,” “including,” “for example,” “like.”
  • Colon after a dependent clause: “Because …:” — the left side must be complete.
  • Semicolon where a colon belongs: before a list or a single phrase.
  • Comma splice where a colon belongs: “for one reason, the city agreed …”.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Period test: cover the right side; is the left side a complete sentence? If not, cross out every colon choice.
  • Does the left side end with a verb, a preposition or “such as”? Colon is wrong.
  • Does the left side promise something (three things, one reason, the following)? A colon is likely correct.
  • After a colon: list, explanation, emphasis or extra information — never a dependent clause on its own.

Practice

Remember
  • Colon = complete sentence first, then a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information.
  • Period test: the part before the colon must be able to end with a period.
  • Never after a verb, a preposition, “such as,” “including” or “for example.”
  • Words like “the following,” “three things,” “one reason” make the left side complete.
  • Only one colon per sentence; never inside a list.
