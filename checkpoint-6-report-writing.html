Checkpoint 6 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Report Writing

How to write a formal, factual report for a reader such as the head teacher — purpose, headings, facts, survey results and recommendations — with a plan, phrases, vocabulary and a strong sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is report writing?

A report gives factual, organised information about a topic, an event or a place for a particular reader — often a head teacher, a teacher, a school council or a club. Its job is to inform clearly and fairly, so the reader can understand what happened or what was found, and sometimes decide what to do next.

A report is objective: it is based on facts, numbers and what people said, not on the writer's feelings. If the task asks for your view (for example, whether a trip was worthwhile), you give it in a separate, clearly labelled section at the end — usually called Conclusion or Recommendations — and you support it with the facts in your report.

QuestionAnswer for a report
PurposeTo inform a reader clearly and objectively about a topic, event or place, and sometimes to recommend what should happen next.
AudienceNamed in the task: the head teacher, your teacher, the school council, parents. Usually an adult, so the tone is formal.
ToneFormal, calm and factual. No slang, no jokes, no exclamation marks.
Person and tenseMostly the third person (Pupils visited…, The survey showed…). Past tense for an event that has happened, and present tense for facts that are still true (The library has 2,000 books).
Where it appearsPaper 1 (Non-fiction), Section B Writing, 25 marks.

Report, recount, newspaper report or non-chronological report?

These text types look similar. The task words tell you which one is wanted. This site has a separate page for the newspaper report and the non-chronological report — use this table to tell them apart.

Text typeWhat it doesHow it looks
Report (for a reader such as the head teacher)Gives organised facts and findings about an event, a place or a problem. May end with recommendations.Title, 'To / From / Date / Subject' line (optional), headings, formal paragraphs, survey numbers, Conclusion or Recommendations.
RecountTells the story of something you did, in time order, with your feelings.First person (I, we), past tense, chronological paragraphs.
Newspaper reportTells the public about a news event.Headline, opening sentence with who/what/where/when, quotations from witnesses, past tense.
Non-chronological reportGives information about a topic (an animal, a country), grouped by subject, not by time.Title, introduction, subheadings by topic, present tense, facts.
How reports come in the exam
  • A report task may name a reader and give you points to include, for example: where the class went, what happened, what pupils thought, and whether it should happen again.
  • Reports about school life are likely topics: a school trip, a sports day, a club, the canteen, the playground, the library, a survey of pupils' opinions.
  • If the task says 'a report for the school newspaper' about a recent event, it wants a newspaper report (headline, quotations). If it says 'a report for the head teacher / your teacher', it wants the formal report on this page. Read the task words carefully.

Where it sits in the exam and how it is marked

Year 6 students sit Cambridge Primary Checkpoint English (0058). There are two papers: Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction. Each paper lasts 1 hour and has Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks). About 30 minutes is suggested for each section. Report writing is a Paper 1 task, because it is non-fiction.

Your writing is marked out of 25 in five categories. Here is what each category means for a report. The teacher's older names are in brackets.

CategoryMarksWhat the top band needs in this genre
Creation of texts (content, purpose and audience)5Clearly a report: factual, organised and written for the named reader. Every point in the task is covered with specific details (numbers, times, places, what people said). Any opinion is kept for the conclusion and supported by the facts.
Vocabulary and language3Formal, precise words (visited, organised, the majority of pupils, in addition, recommend, improve, facilities). Numbers and names instead of vague words like 'lots' or 'stuff'.
Structure of texts (text structure)7Title, introduction stating the purpose, headed sections with one topic each, a conclusion or recommendations. Paragraphs are linked with connectives (In addition, However, As a result, Overall).
Grammar and punctuation (sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7Accurate tenses, complex sentences (Although…, Because…, which…), passive forms where useful (The trip was organised by…). Correct commas, colons before lists, brackets for extra information, apostrophes.
Word structure (spelling)3Correct spelling of formal and topic words: museum, organised, government, environment, survey, recommend, separate, necessary.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • Wrong text type: If the task asks for a report for the head teacher and you write a diary entry or a story about the trip, that is the wrong text type. If you write the wrong kind of text, Creation of texts can be capped at 1 mark and Structure of texts at 3 (or 2), however good your English is.
  • Too short: 20 words or fewer scores 0, and an answer of 21–60 words can earn no more than 7 marks out of 25. Train yourself to write 250–350 words in about 30 minutes.
  • Markers use best fit: they read the whole answer and choose the band that matches it best. One small mistake will not ruin a strong answer, but many careless ones will pull it down.

How to get full marks: checklist

  • A clear title that names the subject: Report on the Year 6 Trip to…
  • An introduction that says what the report is about, who it is for and why it was written.
  • Headings for each section, matching the points in the task.
  • One main idea per paragraph, with specific facts: numbers, times, places, names of activities.
  • Formal, objective language: no 'I loved it!' in the main sections.
  • Evidence such as survey results or what people said (reported, not chatty).
  • A conclusion or recommendations section that sums up and, if the task asks, gives a supported view.
  • Correct tenses: past for the event, present for facts that are still true.

FeatureExample
Clear titleReport on the Year 6 Trip to the Nile Science Centre
Memo line (optional)To: the Head Teacher · From: Year 6 School Council · Date: 14th March · Subject: Science trip
Introduction stating the purposeThe purpose of this report is to describe the trip and to suggest whether it should be repeated.
Headings / subheadingsIntroduction · The Activities · Pupils' Opinions · Problems · Recommendations
Facts and numbersSixty pupils and five teachers travelled by coach.
Survey resultsFifty-two out of sixty pupils rated the trip 'excellent' or 'good'.
Third person, objective toneMost pupils said that… (not 'Everyone was SO happy!')
Passive voice for formal toneThe trip was organised by the science department.
Formal connectivesIn addition, However, As a result, Overall
Bullet points (where helpful)Recommendations: • book the planetarium earlier • bring packed lunches
Conclusion / recommendationsOverall, the trip was a great success, and it is recommended that…
Fact or opinion?

In the main sections, change opinions into reported facts. Not 'The robot show was amazing!' but 'Most pupils described the robot show as the highlight of the day.' The information is the same, but now it is objective — and it sounds like a real report.

3. How to structure a report

Use this plan. In 30 minutes aim for 250–350 words. Turn the bullet points in the task into your headings — that way you cannot forget one.

SectionJobWhat to write
Title (+ memo line)Name the subjectReport on… Optionally: To / From / Date / Subject.
IntroductionPurposeWhat the report is about, who it is for, the key facts (when, where, who). 2–3 sentences.
Section 1 (heading)First main pointe.g. The Activities — what happened or what was found, with specific details.
Section 2 (heading)Second main pointe.g. Pupils' Opinions — survey numbers, what people said, reported formally.
Section 3 (heading)Third main pointe.g. Problems or Areas to Improve — stated fairly, with facts.
Conclusion / RecommendationsSum up + adviseOverall result in one sentence. Then 2–3 clear recommendations, each with a reason. If the task asks for your view, give it here.
Plan with G.A.A.P. in the first 3–4 minutes
  • G — Genre: a report (title, introduction, headings, conclusion/recommendations).
  • A — Audience: the head teacher or a teacher = formal. The school council = formal but friendly.
  • A — Area of content: every bullet point in the task becomes a heading. Invent realistic facts: numbers, times, activities.
  • P — Purpose: to inform clearly — and to recommend, if the task asks.

How to start: introductions that work

  • Purpose: This report describes the Year 6 trip to the Nile Science Centre and suggests whether it should be repeated next year.
  • Who asked for it: As requested by the Head Teacher, this report presents the results of our survey on school lunches.
  • Key facts first: On Tuesday 11th March, sixty Year 6 pupils visited the Nile Science Centre in Cairo.
  • The problem: In recent weeks, several pupils have complained about the condition of the playground. This report examines the problem and suggests solutions.
  • What was done: The school council asked 120 pupils for their opinion of the library. This report presents what they said.

How to end: conclusions that work

  • Sum up: Overall, the trip was a great success and met its main aim.
  • Recommend: It is therefore recommended that the trip is repeated next year, with two changes.
  • Balance: Although there were some small problems, the advantages were far greater.
  • Next steps: If these suggestions are followed, the playground will be safer for everyone.
  • Thank the reader (formal): Thank you for considering these recommendations.

5. Strong sample: report on a school trip

Sample: report for the head teacher293 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your class recently went on a school trip. The head teacher has asked the school council to write a report about it. Write the report. Include: • where you went and what you did • what pupils thought of the trip • any problems • whether the trip should happen again next year, and why.

Report on the Year 6 Trip to the Nile Science Centre

To: The Head Teacher · From: Year 6 School Council · Date: 14th March

Introduction

On Tuesday 11th March, sixty Year 6 pupils and five teachers visited the Nile Science Centre. The trip was organised by the science department to support our unit on forces and electricity. The purpose of this report is to describe the visit, present pupils' opinions and suggest whether the trip should be repeated next year.

The Activities

After a forty-minute coach journey, pupils were divided into three groups. Each group took part in a hands-on workshop, in which pupils built simple electrical circuits and tested which materials conduct electricity. In addition, all the groups watched a robot show and spent thirty minutes in the planetarium (a room with a domed ceiling that shows the night sky).

Pupils' Opinions

The school council asked every pupil to complete a short survey after the trip. Fifty-two out of sixty pupils rated it 'excellent' or 'good', and only three described it as 'poor'. The majority said that the workshop was the highlight of the day because it helped them to understand circuits more clearly than a textbook. Several teachers commented that pupils asked thoughtful questions throughout the visit.

Problems

However, there were two difficulties. The coach arrived twenty minutes late, which shortened the time in the planetarium. Furthermore, the café was crowded and expensive, so some pupils had very little time to eat.

Recommendations

Overall, the trip was a great success and clearly supported pupils' learning. The school council recommends that it is repeated next year, with two changes:

• book the coach company earlier and confirm the time the day before

• ask pupils to bring packed lunches

Thank you for considering these recommendations.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: clearly a formal report for the head teacher, with a memo line and a stated purpose. All four points in the task have their own heading: what you did (The Activities), what pupils thought (Pupils' Opinions), problems (Problems), happen again? (Recommendations). Specific invented facts make it realistic: sixty pupils, forty-minute journey, fifty-two out of sixty.
  • Creation of texts (continued): the tone stays objective: opinions are reported as survey results and comments. The council's own view appears only in Recommendations and is supported by the facts above it.
  • Vocabulary 3/3: formal, precise words — organised, divided, hands-on workshop, conduct electricity, planetarium, the majority, thoughtful, recommends, considering.
  • Structure 7/7: title → memo line → introduction with purpose → three headed sections with one main idea each → recommendations with bullet points → formal closing line. Connectives (In addition, However, Furthermore, Overall) link the sections. The recommendations answer the problems raised earlier.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7: Past tense is used for the event and present for the council's recommendation. Passive forms (was organised, were divided) and relative clauses (…a hands-on workshop, in which pupils built… / …late, which shortened…) are accurate. Brackets give extra information (a room with a domed ceiling that shows the night sky). A colon introduces the list of changes. Inverted commas quote the survey words 'excellent' and 'good'. Apostrophes show possession (pupils' opinions, pupils' learning).
  • Spelling 3/3: organised, electricity, planetarium, thoughtful, expensive, recommendations, considering.

Timing (about 30 minutes for writing)
  • 3–4 minutes: G.A.A.P., then write your headings from the task's bullet points and jot 2–3 facts under each.
  • about 22 minutes: write 250–350 words.
  • 4 minutes: check spelling, tenses, apostrophes and that every heading is there.
Checklist before you hand in
  • Clear title (and memo line if useful).
  • Introduction that states the purpose.
  • A heading for each point in the task.
  • Specific facts: numbers, times, places.
  • Objective, formal tone in the main sections.
  • Survey results or reported comments as evidence.
  • Conclusion / recommendations with reasons.
  • Correct tenses and spelling.

Practice tasks

  1. The head teacher wants to know what pupils think of the school library. Write a report from the school council. Include: what the library offers, how pupils use it, any problems, and your recommendations.

    Show answer
    Title: Report on the School Library. Introduction: purpose. Survey of 100 pupils in Years 4–6. What the Library Offers: about 3,000 books, six computers and a quiet reading corner, open 8 a.m.–3 p.m. How Pupils Use It: 70 out of 100 visit every week, mostly to borrow adventure stories. Some use computers for homework. Problems: closed at lunchtime, not enough new books and slow computers. Recommendations: open at lunchtime with pupil helpers, add a monthly new-books shelf and upgrade two computers. Formal closing line.

  2. Your class held a charity bake sale. Write a report for your teacher.

    Show answer
    Headings: Introduction (when, where, why) · Preparation (who baked, how many cakes) · The Sale (how much money was raised, best-selling item) · Problems (ran out of change, rain) · Recommendations (bring a cash box with coins, hold it indoors). Past tense, third person, numbers throughout.

  3. Change these into objective report sentences: (a) 'The robot show was totally awesome!' (b) 'Loads of people came.'

    Show answer
    (a) Most pupils described the robot show as the highlight of the trip. (b) Approximately 200 parents and pupils attended the event.

  4. A task says: 'Write a report for the school newspaper about the new school garden.' Which text type is wanted, and what features should you include?

    Show answer
    A newspaper report — because it is for the school newspaper. Include a headline, an opening sentence that answers who, what, where and when, quotations from people involved, and past tense for what happened. (See the newspaper report page.)

  5. Why do reports often use the passive voice, e.g. 'The trip was organised by the science department'?

    Show answer
    It makes the writing sound formal and objective, and it puts the focus on the event (the trip) rather than on the people who did it.
