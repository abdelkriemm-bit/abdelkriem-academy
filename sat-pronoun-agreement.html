Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement, Clarity and Consistency

Match every pronoun to its antecedent in number (a company, a team and each of … are "it"), replace unclear pronouns with nouns, and keep one/you/we consistent.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

A pronoun (it, its, they, their, this, one …) stands in for a noun. The noun it replaces is called its antecedent. On the Digital SAT, this lesson's questions check three things: the pronoun must match its antecedent in number (singular or plural), the reader must be able to tell which noun it means, and the passage must stay consistent (no jumping between *one*, *you* and *we*). The rules are short and fixed, so these are points you can win every time.

What this question type tests

These questions belong to Standard English Conventions, which is about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (about 11–15 questions across the two modules). Inside that domain, the official skill group Form, Structure, and Sense covers pronoun–antecedent agreement, together with subject–verb agreement, verb forms and the sound-alike pairs. Convention questions usually come in the later part of each module, after Information and Ideas.

The stem is always: "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?" You know it is an agreement question when the four choices are pronouns that differ in number or when one choice replaces the pronoun with a noun, for example:

  • its / their / it's / they're
  • it / they / this / the new lamps
  • one / you / they / we

In "The museum reopened its doors", the antecedent of *its* is *museum*. In "The turtles returned to their beach", the antecedent of *their* is *turtles*. Almost every question in this lesson is solved the same way: find the antecedent first, then choose.

أول خطوة دايمًا

قبل ما تختار الضمير، دوّر على الاسم اللي الضمير راجع له (الـ antecedent). لو الاسم مفرد (شركة، جامعة، فريق، متحف، نوع حيوان) يبقى it / its. لو جمع يبقى they / their. الشركة مش they حتى لو فيها آلاف الموظفين.

Rule 1: Singular with singular, plural with plural

A singular antecedent takes a singular pronoun (it, its, he, she, his, her). A plural antecedent takes a plural pronoun (they, them, their). That sounds easy, so the test makes it hard in two ways: it uses nouns that feel plural but are grammatically singular, and it puts the antecedent far away from the pronoun.

AntecedentPronoun✓ Correct✗ Wrong
One company, university, museum, government, city, country, organizationit / itsThe company moved its factory to Sadat City.The company moved their factory.
A species or one animal described in general (the fennec fox, the honeybee)it / itsThe fennec fox uses its large ears to lose heat.The fennec fox uses their large ears.
Plural nouns (scientists, turtles, the pyramids, data from many sites)they / them / theirThe turtles return to their home beach.The turtles return to its home beach.
A and B (joined by *and*)they / theirCairo and Giza share their water supply.Cairo and Giza share its water supply.
A population, a collection, a series, a group (of + plural)it / itsThe population of ibex has doubled its range.The population of ibex has doubled their range.

Rule 2: Groups and organizations are "it"

Collective nouns name one group made of many members: team, committee, band, class, jury, family, staff, crew, audience, council, company, government. In American Standard English, which the SAT follows, a collective noun used as one unit is singular: it takes it/its and a singular verb.

  • ✓ The committee announced its decision on Monday.
  • ✗ The committee announced their decision on Monday.
  • ✓ The band released its first album in 2019.
  • ✗ The band released their first album in 2019.
  • ✓ The Egyptian national team has won its group. (one team = it)
British school students: careful with groups

British English often says "The team have won their match." The SAT follows American Standard English: a team, company, university, government or band is one thing — "The team has won its match." A useful check: look at the rest of the passage. If another sentence already says "its" or "has" about the same group, keep the singular.

Rule 3: each, every, either, neither, one of …

These words point to one item at a time, so they are singular even when a plural noun follows them in an *of*-phrase. The noun in the *of*-phrase is bait: do not let it decide the pronoun.

Antecedent wordNumber✓ Example (things)
each (of the …), everysingularEach of the bridges has its own toll gate.
either / neither (of the …)singularNeither of the two satellites has lost its signal.
one of the …singularOne of the statues still has its original paint.
both, several, few, manypluralBoth of the dams are close to their limits.

A note on people. With words like *everyone*, *each student* or *a visitor*, modern style accepts they/their as well as *he or she* ("Each student brought their own laptop"), so a question cannot fairly depend on that choice. The agreement questions you can be sure about are the ones where the antecedent is a thing, an animal or an organization: there, singular means it/its, always.

Rule 4: Compound antecedents — and / or / nor

  • A and B → plural: "The museum and the library have updated their opening hours."
  • A or B, either A or B, neither A nor B → the pronoun agrees with the nearer antecedent (the one after *or / nor*).
  • ✓ "Neither the lighthouse nor the two towers still use their original lamps." (nearer = towers, plural)
  • ✓ "Neither the two towers nor the lighthouse still uses its original lamp." (nearer = lighthouse, singular)
  • Tip: the verb follows the same nearer noun (*use* / *uses*), which gives you a second clue.

Rule 5: Find the real antecedent (the distance trap)

The test likes to put a different-number noun between the antecedent and the pronoun. Your ear hears the nearest noun and chooses the wrong pronoun. Go back and ask: *who or what actually does or owns this?*

  • "The collection of ancient coins will move to its new gallery." — The *collection* moves; *coins* is only inside the *of*-phrase.
  • "The lamps in the old library, which the city installed in 1950, still keep their original glass." — *library* and *city* are closer, but it is the *lamps* that keep the glass.
  • Strip the sentence: cross out *of*-phrases and the extra information between commas, then read subject + pronoun together: "The collection … its", "The lamps … their".
Use the passage as a witness

The passage often contains a second pronoun that refers to the same noun, outside the blank. If you see "The university … its libraries" in the next sentence, the blank about the university must also be singular. One pronoun in the passage tells you the number of all the others.

Rule 6: Clear antecedents — no confusing pronouns

A pronoun is wrong if the reader cannot be sure which noun it means. This happens when two or more nouns of the same number come before the pronoun, or when *this / that / which / it* points to a whole idea instead of a noun. On the test, the correct choice is then often a specific noun instead of a pronoun.

✗ UnclearWhy✓ Clear
When the scientist showed the robot to the engineer, she was impressed.She = scientist or engineer?… the engineer was impressed.
The new lamps replaced the old ones, and they use less electricity.They = new lamps or old ones?… and the new lamps use less electricity.
The report compared the two dams. This surprised the government.This = the report? the comparison? one result?This comparison surprised the government.
The council compared a one-panel pump with a two-panel pump, and it was chosen.It = which pump? (or the council?)… and the two-panel pump was chosen.

How to decide: list every noun before the pronoun that has the same number as the pronoun. Only one candidate? The pronoun is clear — keep it. Two or more? Choose the answer that names the noun. Do not pick the noun version when the pronoun is already clear: the SAT does not reward extra words for no reason, and a noun choice can also be the wrong noun.

Rule 7: Stay consistent — one / you / we

When a passage talks about people in general, it can use one, you or we. Once it chooses, it must keep the same pronoun. The most tested switch is *one → you*.

  • ✓ If one wants to see the sunrise, one should start early.
  • ✗ If one wants to see the sunrise, you should start early.
  • ✓ If you want to see the sunrise, you should start early.
  • ✗ When we visit Siwa in summer, you quickly learn to rest in the afternoon.
  • ✓ Visitors to Siwa soon learn that they should rest in the afternoon. (plural noun → they)

The 5-step method for test day

  1. Read the choices. Pronouns that differ in number, or a pronoun vs a noun? It is an agreement or clarity question.
  2. Find the antecedent. Go back in the sentence (or the sentence before) and ask "Who or what does this mean?" Strip *of*-phrases and comma extras.
  3. Check number. Singular (including company, team, species, each/neither/one of) → it/its. Plural (and A and B) → they/their. With or/nor → the nearer noun.
  4. Check clarity. Two possible antecedents of the same number → choose the choice that names the noun.
  5. Check consistency. Keep one/you/we the same, and match any other pronoun the passage already uses for that noun.

Worked examples

Worked example 1: a company is "it"

Passage: "The textile company began as a small workshop in El-Mahalla El-Kubra in the 1950s. Today, ______ products are sold in more than thirty countries, although most of its cotton is still grown in the Nile Delta."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) their
  • B) its
  • C) it's
  • D) they're

Answer: B) its. The antecedent is "The textile company" — one company, so it is singular. A possessive is needed ("the company's products"), so "its" is correct. The passage confirms it later with "its cotton": the same noun, the same pronoun.

  • A) their — Plural. A company is one thing on the SAT, even though many people work for it. This is the most common agreement trap.
  • C) it's — "It's" means "it is": "it is products are sold" makes no sense.
  • D) they're — "They're" means "they are", and it is plural as well.

Worked example 2: each of … is singular

Passage: "Five lighthouses once guided ships along the coast between Alexandria and Rashid. Each of the lighthouses had ______ own keeper, who lived in a small house at the base of the tower and lit the lamp every evening."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) their
  • B) there
  • C) its
  • D) it's

Answer: C) its. The subject is "Each", not "lighthouses": each lighthouse, one at a time, had a keeper. "Each" is singular, so the possessive must be "its". The rest of the sentence agrees: "the tower", "the lamp".

  • A) their — Plural: the bait is "lighthouses" inside the of-phrase. "Each" decides the number.
  • B) there — "There" is a place word, not a possessive: "had there own keeper" is meaningless.
  • D) it's — "It's" means "it is" or "it has": "had it is own keeper" makes no sense.

Worked example 3: unclear pronoun → name the noun

Passage: "In the 1820s, Jean-François Champollion compared the Greek text on the Rosetta Stone with the hieroglyphic text above it. Since scholars could already read the Greek, ______ helped him identify the hieroglyphic signs for royal names such as Ptolemy."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) it
  • B) they
  • C) this
  • D) the Greek inscription

Answer: D) the Greek inscription. Three singular nouns come before the blank: the Rosetta Stone, the hieroglyphic text and the Greek. A pronoun would not make clear which one helped. Only the noun "the Greek inscription" removes the doubt.

  • A) it — Unclear: "it" could mean the stone, the Greek text or the hieroglyphic text.
  • B) they — Plural, and the only plural noun, "scholars", is not what helped him: the readable Greek text did.
  • C) this — Vague: "this" does not name what it refers to — the comparison? the stone? the reading?

Worked example 4: keep "one" consistent

Passage: "Travel writers often warn about the heat in Siwa Oasis. When one visits in July or August, ______ quickly learns why local families close their shops in the early afternoon and open them again after sunset."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) one
  • B) you
  • C) they
  • D) we

Answer: A) one. The sentence has already chosen "one" for people in general ("When one visits"). It must continue with "one". The verb "learns" (singular, with -s) also fits "one" and confirms the answer.

  • B) you — Switches from "one" to "you" in the same sentence; "you learns" is also ungrammatical.
  • C) they — Plural, and the only plural noun it could point to, "Travel writers" or "families", is not the person who visits.
  • D) we — Switches to "we", and "we learns" does not agree.
Classic traps
  • "Their" for a company or group: company, university, museum, team, committee, government, band → its.
  • The of-phrase bait: "each of the bridges", "the collection of coins", "the population of ibex" — the first noun decides.
  • The far antecedent: a long sentence puts a closer noun with a different number next to the blank.
  • Neither … nor: the pronoun (and the verb) follows the nearer noun.
  • Unclear it / they / this: if two nouns of the same number could be meant, the choice that names the noun wins.
  • Unnecessary noun: if only one noun fits, the plain pronoun is better — don't choose a wordy noun choice just because it looks safe.
  • Switching one ↔ you ↔ we: keep the pronoun the passage started with.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Point to the antecedent before you read the choices, and say the pair aloud in your head: "the company … its", "the turtles … their".
  • Cross out of-phrases and comma extras to find the real antecedent.
  • Look for a witness: another pronoun or a verb (has/have) in the passage that shows the number of the same noun.
  • Choose a noun instead of a pronoun only when there really are two possible antecedents.
  • Target time: about 30–40 seconds per question.

Practice

Ten original questions in Digital SAT format. For each one, find the antecedent first, then check number, clarity and consistency. The explanation appears after you answer.

Remember
  • Find the antecedent first. Singular (company, university, team, species, population, each / neither / one of) → it/its; plural (and A and B) → they/their.
  • Cross out of-phrases and comma extras: the first noun, not the noun in the of-phrase, decides the number.
  • With or / nor, agree with the nearer noun.
  • If two nouns of the same number could be the antecedent, choose the answer that names the noun; otherwise keep the pronoun.
  • Keep the same pronoun for people in general: one … one, you … you.
