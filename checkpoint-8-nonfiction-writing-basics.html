Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Paper 1 Writing Basics: Purpose, Format, Register and G.A.A.P.

Everything you need before any non-fiction genre: purpose vs format, formal vs informal language, how to read the task, the G.A.A.P. technique, a 5-minute plan, paragraphing and a strong sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Every Paper 1 writing task is worth 25 marks — half of the paper. Before you learn any single genre (autobiography, letter, article, review…), you need the skills on this page: knowing why you are writing (purpose), knowing what shape the writing must take (format), choosing the right register (formal or informal), reading the task properly with the G.A.A.P. technique, planning in five minutes and building strong paragraphs. Every genre pack in this course is built on these foundations.

Year 8 students sit the Cambridge Lower Secondary Progression Test Stage 8 in school, and later the Checkpoint (0861) test. Both use the same shape: Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction, each with Reading 25 + Writing 25. Recent Paper 1 writing tasks have included an article for a school magazine, a travel article about a holiday place, a competition entry describing a small object and, on a Stage 8 test, a review for a school newsletter. Other non-fiction types from the curriculum (letters, reports, speeches, recounts, biographies, diaries, blogs, leaflets) can also appear.

Rules that can cost you marks before quality is even judged
  • 20 words or fewer = 0 marks for the whole task.
  • 21–60 words = a maximum of 7 marks out of 25, however good the writing is (60 words is about 8 handwritten lines).
  • Wrong text type (for example, a story when an article was asked for): Creation of texts is capped at 1 mark, and Structure is capped too.
  • Your plan is not marked — but a good plan raises the marks of everything else.

2. Writing purposes vs writing formats

Every writing task asks you to do two separate jobs. Purpose is *why* you are writing — the effect you want on your reader (to inform, explain, describe, persuade, argue, recount, instruct, entertain). Format (also called layout or text type) is *how the writing is packaged* — an article, a letter, an email, a diary entry, a report, a speech, a leaflet.

Students lose marks because they mix these up. They think a format comes with one fixed purpose ("a report can only inform") or that a task must have a format even when none is named. In fact, one purpose can be delivered in many different formats, and one format can serve many different purposes.

PURPOSEFORMAT (layout)
Question it answersWhy am I writing this? What do I want the reader to think, feel or do?What must this look like on the page?
Decided byThe intention in the scenario (convince, explain, describe, recount…)Only what the task states directly — never guessed
If the task does not sayYou still work it out from the wordingWrite normal, well-paragraphed prose with no special layout
How many in one piece?Can be more than one (a main purpose + a secondary one)Only ONE format per piece
The Golden Rule (from the teacher's book)

A piece of writing can carry more than one purpose, but it can only ever have ONE format. A format's special features — a greeting, an address, a headline, a date line — are used ONLY when the task names that format. If no format is named, do not invent one: write clear, organised paragraphs that fulfil the purpose.

Main purpose first

Many tasks mix purposes: a travel article describes AND informs AND entertains; a leaflet informs AND persuades. Ask yourself: "What is the MAIN effect I want on my reader?" Build the whole piece around that, and let the other purposes support it.

  1. Task A: "Write a letter to your headteacher asking for a quiet study room to open at lunchtime." Purpose? Is a format named?

    Show answer
    Purpose: to persuade / request. Format: named — a formal letter. Use the full letter layout (greeting 'Dear Mr/Ms …', polite formal paragraphs, 'Yours sincerely,').

  2. Task B: "You recently took part in an event you will always remember. Write about what happened and how you felt."

    Show answer
    Purpose: to recount (with description and reflection). Format: NOT named. Write a normal, well-paragraphed recount in the first person — no 'Dear Diary', no headline, no greeting.

  3. Task C: "Your school website is publishing pieces about healthy habits. Write a piece encouraging students to sleep more."

    Show answer
    Purpose: to persuade (and inform). Format: the publication is named (school website) but the word 'article' is not. The safest choice is a clear piece with a title and paragraphs for students — a title is fine; do not add letter or email features.

  4. Task D: "Explain to a new student how to get the most out of the school library."

    Show answer
    Purpose: to explain / instruct. Format: not named. A person is mentioned, but that does not make it a letter or an email. Write normal explanatory prose in clear paragraphs.

  5. Task E: "Write a diary entry about the day your family moved house."

    Show answer
    Purpose: to recount and reflect. Format: named — diary entry. Use the date, 'Dear Diary', first person, informal private tone.

3. Formal vs informal language (register)

Register means how formal or informal your language is. It is decided by your audience (who reads it), your purpose and the format — never by your personal style. Once you choose a register, keep it the same from the first line to the last.

FeatureFormalInformal
ContractionsNever: do not, cannot, I amCommon: don't, can't, I'm
Slang and chatty wordsAvoidedAllowed in moderation ("loads", "guess what")
VocabularyPrecise, sometimes elevated: assist, require, obtainEveryday: help, need, get
Phrasal verbsReplaced: reduce, postpone, tolerateUsed: cut down, put off, put up with
SentencesComplete, often longer, carefully linkedCan be short, even fragments: "What a day!"
TonePolite, respectful, serious, some distanceFriendly, personal, relaxed, close
Exclamation marksVery rareUsed to show feeling
Typical audienceHeadteacher, council, newspaper readers, strangersFriends, family, yourself (diary)
Typical formatsFormal letter, report, biography, speech to officials, article for a serious publicationDiary, informal letter or email, many blogs, a chatty article for teenagers

Between the two is semi-formal register: correct and polite but warm, with no slang and few or no contractions. It is the right choice for most school-magazine articles, school-website pieces and speeches to classmates.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
get → obtain, receive"Students can obtain a library card from reception."
help → assist, support"Volunteers assisted elderly residents."
ask for → request"I would like to request a meeting."
tell → inform, notify"Please inform the office of any changes."
start → begin, commence"The festival commenced at noon."
find out → discover"We discovered that the bins were never emptied."
cut down → reduce"The school could reduce plastic waste."
put off → postpone"The match was postponed because of the storm."
a lot of → numerous, a great deal of"Numerous students have complained."
kids → children, young people"Young people need safe places to play."
stuff → items, materials"Please bring the necessary materials."
bad → poor, unsatisfactory"The food was of poor quality."
say sorry → apologise"I apologise for any inconvenience caused."
make sure → ensure"Please ensure that all doors are locked."

Formal openers and linkers

  • I am writing to…
  • I would be grateful if…
  • It is my view that…
  • Furthermore, / Moreover, / In addition,
  • However, / Nevertheless,
  • As a result, / Consequently,
  • In conclusion, / To summarise,
  • I would like to express my gratitude for…

Informal openers and linkers

  • Guess what?
  • You'll never believe what happened…
  • Anyway, …
  • Honestly, …
  • So, …
  • The best bit was…
  • Can't wait to…
  • Speak soon!

  1. Rewrite formally: "Can you guys sort out the broken lights in the gym?"

    Show answer
    "Could you please arrange for the broken lights in the gym to be repaired?" (no slang 'guys', 'sort out' → 'arrange… to be repaired', and a polite question form)

  2. Rewrite formally: "Loads of students can't get to the bus on time 'cause it leaves too early."

    Show answer
    "Many students are unable to reach the bus on time because it departs too early."

  3. Rewrite formally: "The trip was really good and we learnt tons of stuff."

    Show answer
    "The trip was extremely worthwhile, and we learnt a great deal."

  4. Rewrite informally (for a diary): "I am extremely anxious regarding tomorrow's examination."

    Show answer
    "I'm so nervous about tomorrow's exam!"

  5. Spot the register mistake: "Dear Sir, I am writing to complain about the noise. It's driving me nuts."

    Show answer
    "It's driving me nuts" is slang with a contraction — it breaks the formal register. Better: "It is causing considerable disturbance."

4. How to read a Paper 1 writing task

A Checkpoint writing task is rarely a one-line order like "Write a persuasive article". It usually has four parts. Read every word before you plan.

Part of the taskWhat it tells youWhat to underline
1. The scenario (the situation)Why the writing exists: a competition, a school magazine, a website, a council decision…Where it will be published and who will read it → AUDIENCE and register
2. The instructionWhat you must do: write an article / describe / persuade / recount…The format word (if any) and the purpose verb
3. The bullet promptsThe content the examiner expects to seeEvery bullet — each one becomes at least one paragraph
4. The extra adviceReminders such as 'plan your writing' or 'write in paragraphs'Anything about length, viewpoint or what to include

Worked example (an original task in exam style):

Sample task

Your school magazine is running a series called 'People Who Make Our Town Work'. Write an article about a person in your local area whose job helps the community. In your article you should: • describe the person and what they do each day • explain why their work matters to the community • say what you have learnt from them.

Clue in the taskWhat it means for you
"school magazine"Audience = students, teachers and parents. Register = semi-formal and engaging, no slang.
"Write an article"Format is named → use article features: a headline, a lively opening, paragraphs (sub-headings optional).
"describe… explain… say what you have learnt"Three purposes: describe (main), explain, reflect. Use sensory detail, reasons and a personal ending.
Three bulletsThree content areas — all must appear, each developed in detail, not one sentence each.
'Consider' vs 'Think' (the teacher's rule)

Read the verb before the bullet points. If the task says "Consider…", treat the bullets as required: include at least THREE of the given ideas, then add your own if you wish. If it says "Think about…", the bullets are suggestions to help you — you may use them or your own ideas. Either way, the safest route to full Creation marks is to cover the bullets in depth.

5. The G.A.A.P. technique (in detail)

G.A.A.P. is the teacher's four-part method for breaking down any Paper 1 task in the first 3–5 minutes. It is sometimes shortened to "GAP", but it has four parts: Genre · Audience · Area of content · Purpose. Each letter matches something the marker is checking.

G — Genre (two parts: layout + topic)

Layout: what text type are you writing? This decides the features you must show. Using the wrong layout can cap your Creation mark at 1, so this is the fastest way to protect marks.

LayoutConventions to include
ArticleHeadline, sometimes a byline, engaging opening, sub-headings in longer pieces
ReportClear title, formal tone, headings, factual and objective, recommendations
ReviewName of the thing reviewed early, description + opinion, recommendation at the end
LetterAddresses and date (formal), 'Dear…', correct sign-off (Yours sincerely / Yours faithfully / Best wishes)
EmailSubject line, greeting, clear paragraphs, suitable sign-off
RecountChronological order, past tense, first person if personal, focus on what happened + reflection
SpeechDirect address to the audience, rhetorical questions, strong opening and closing
Diary entryDate, 'Dear Diary', first person, informal, reflective
No format namedClear paragraphs only — a simple title is acceptable, but no special layout

Topic: what exactly is the task about? Be precise. Not "the environment" but "plastic waste in our school canteen". A precise topic keeps every paragraph relevant.

A — Audience

  • Where will it be read? A school magazine, a local newspaper, a website, a letter to one person, a private diary?
  • Who exactly will read it? Teenagers, parents, a headteacher, a council, yourself?
  • What register does that audience need? Formal, semi-formal or informal — and keep it consistent.

A — Area of content

  1. Underline every point the task gives you (usually the bullets).
  2. Use all of them. Do not skip the hardest one — a missing point loses Creation marks directly.
  3. Develop each point: one sentence per bullet is never enough. Add explanation, an example, a detail, a feeling or a result.
  4. Add one or two ideas of your own if they fit — this shows a 'wide range of developed ideas'.

P — Purpose

If the purpose is……your toolkit is
Inform / explainFacts, clear topic sentences, examples, neutral tone, connectives of cause (because, as a result)
PersuadeDirect address (you), rhetorical questions, emotive words, rule of three, facts/statistics, a call to action
Argue / discussBoth sides, evidence, balanced connectives (however, on the other hand), a reasoned conclusion
DescribeSenses, precise adjectives and verbs, similes and metaphors, a clear viewpoint
RecountTime order, past tense, time connectives, feelings, reflection at the end
Advise / instructImperatives, modal verbs (should, could, must), sequence words, reassuring tone

Why G.A.A.P. earns marks

G.A.A.P. stepMark criterion it protects
Genre — layoutCreation of texts (correct text type and features) and Structure of texts
Genre — topic + Area of contentCreation of texts (relevance, all points covered, ideas developed)
AudienceVocabulary and language (tone and word choice suit the reader) and Creation (writer–reader relationship)
PurposeCreation of texts (right techniques for the job) and Grammar (sentence choices for effect)

  1. Do G.A.A.P.: "Write a report for your headteacher about the after-school clubs at your school. Include: which clubs are popular, any problems students have, and one improvement you recommend."

    Show answer
    G: report (title, sub-headings, formal, factual) · topic = after-school clubs. A: headteacher → formal, no contractions. A: (1) popular clubs (2) problems (3) one recommendation. P: inform + recommend → facts, clear headings, polite recommendation.

  2. Do G.A.A.P.: "Write a speech for your class encouraging them to join a beach clean-up. Think about: why the beach needs help, what volunteers will do, and why it will be fun."

    Show answer
    G: speech (greeting the audience, direct address, strong ending) · topic = beach clean-up. A: classmates → semi-formal, lively. A: 'Think about' = suggestions, but cover all three for full marks. P: persuade → rhetorical questions, 'we/you', rule of three, call to action.

  3. Do G.A.A.P.: "You have been asked to write about the most important object you own for a display called 'My Treasure'."

    Show answer
    G: no format word → clear paragraphs, a simple title is fine · topic = one treasured object. A: visitors to a school display → semi-formal, engaging. A: implied content = what it is, its story, why it matters. P: describe + explain → sensory detail and reasons, a reflective ending.

6. The 5-minute plan

The planning space is not marked, but the plan is where Structure (7 marks) and Creation (5 marks) are won. Use this routine every time.

MinuteWhat you do
0–1Read the whole task twice. Underline the format word, the audience, the purpose verb and every bullet.
1–2Write G.A.A.P. in the planning space in four short lines.
2–4Plan paragraphs: write P1, P2, P3… down the margin. Next to each, write 3–4 key words: the point + a detail/example + a feeling or effect.
4–5Choose your opening technique, your ending idea and 4–5 'power words' you will use (ambitious vocabulary for this topic).

8. Paragraphing

Structure of texts is worth 7 marks, and the top band needs paragraphs that are well organised and clearly linked. A paragraph is a group of sentences about one main idea.

Start a new paragraph when you change…Example
Time"Later that afternoon, …"
Place"Back at home, …"
Topic / point"Another reason hobbies matter is…"
Person (or speaker)"My grandfather, however, saw things differently."

This is easy to remember as TiPToP: Time, Place, Topic, Person.

The shape of a strong body paragraph

PartWhat it doesExample
Linking topic sentenceLinks back and states the main pointFurthermore, a hobby is a powerful shield against stress.
DevelopmentExplains the pointWhen your mind is calm, you learn faster and forget less.
Detail / exampleMakes it real and specificImagine spending thirty minutes kicking a ball before returning to your desk.
Effect / linkShows why it matters or moves onYou will not lose that half-hour; you will win it back in concentration.
Weak vs strong

Weak: "Hobbies are good. They help with stress. You should have one." (three ideas, no development, no link). Strong: "Furthermore, a hobby is a powerful shield against stress. Imagine the night before a difficult test… You will not lose that half-hour; you will win it back in concentration." (one idea, developed, linked).

9. Strong sample (no format named)

This sample shows what to do when the task does not name a format: no headline, no greeting — just a powerful, well-paragraphed piece built around the purpose.

Sample — 'A Hobby Is Worth It' (persuasive, no format named)445 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Many students say they are far too busy with schoolwork to have a hobby. Write to convince students of your age that a hobby is worth making time for. Think about: • what a hobby can give you that schoolwork cannot • how a hobby can help with stress • how to find the time • what you might miss if you never try.

Be honest: when did you last do something simply because you loved it? Not for a mark, not for a parent and not for a certificate — just for the joy of it. If you cannot remember, you are not alone. Most students our age rush from school to private lessons to homework without a single pause, and many of us have decided that hobbies are a luxury we cannot afford. I want to convince you that the opposite is true: a hobby is not a waste of your precious time; it is one of the best investments you will ever make.

Firstly, a hobby gives you something that no timetable can offer: the freedom to choose. At school, every lesson is decided for you. When you pick up a paintbrush, a football or a chess piece, however, you are in charge. My cousin Omar, who is fourteen, started building model aeroplanes two years ago. At first, it was simply a way to fill a quiet Friday afternoon. Now he understands angles, measurements and patience better than anyone in his class. Hobbies quietly teach the skills that exams later reward, and they do it without a single worksheet.

Furthermore, a hobby is a powerful shield against stress. Imagine the night before a difficult test. Your mind races, your stomach twists and every page of your notes begins to blur. Now imagine spending thirty minutes kicking a ball with friends, or practising the oud, before you return to your desk. A short break doing something you enjoy allows your tired brain to rest and reset. You will not lose that half-hour; you will win it back in concentration.

Of course, some of you are already thinking, “But I really do not have time!” I understand, because the school week can be exhausting. However, time is rarely missing; it is usually hidden. How many minutes disappear each evening while you scroll through videos that you will have forgotten by morning? If you exchanged only twenty of those minutes for a hobby, you would gain more than two hours every week. Start small: one evening, one activity, one month.

Finally, think about what you might miss if you never try. The talent you never discover cannot grow, and the friends you never meet at a club cannot cheer you on. Years from now, nobody will ask about a single quiz mark from Year 8, but you may still be playing the instrument, speaking the language or running the races that you started this year. So, choose one thing you have always wanted to try, and give it a real chance this week. Your future self will thank you.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: all four bullets are covered in depth — P2 what a hobby gives, P3 stress, P4 finding time, P5 what you might miss. No format was named, so there is no headline or greeting: the correct decision. The writer–reader relationship is kept up throughout with 'you', 'Be honest', 'Imagine' and a final call to action.
  • Persuasive toolkit throughout, not just at the start: rhetorical questions (P1, P4), direct address, rule of three ('a paintbrush, a football or a chess piece'), a personal example (cousin Omar), a simple calculation as evidence (P4), a counter-argument answered (P4), imperatives ('Start small', 'choose one thing').
  • Vocabulary 3/3: words chosen for effect — 'luxury', 'investment', 'powerful shield', 'blur', 'exhausting', 'discover', the metaphor 'shield against stress' and the antithesis 'You will not lose that half-hour; you will win it back'.
  • Structure 7/7: five paragraphs, one idea each, ordered so the counter-argument comes late and the emotional 'what you might miss' comes last. Linking topic sentences ('Firstly', 'Furthermore', 'Of course… However', 'Finally') and a circular ending (P1 asks about doing things for joy; P5 promises a future self who is grateful).
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 (sentences): a variety of sentence types for effect — short simple sentences for impact ('Start small'), compound sentences, complex sentences with fronted subordinate clauses ('If you exchanged only twenty of those minutes…'), a conditional, accurate verb forms throughout.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 (punctuation): a colon for explanation ('the opposite is true: …'), semicolons that join two related independent clauses ('…waste of your precious time; it is one of the best…'), a single dash for emphasis ('— just for the joy of it' in P1), inverted commas for the imagined objection (P4), commas after fronted adverbials.
  • Spelling 3/3: accurate ambitious and tricky words — 'certificate', 'luxury', 'measurements', 'concentration', 'exhausting', 'practising' (British verb spelling), 'exchanged'.

10. Common mistakes

  • Inventing a format that was not asked for (adding 'Dear…' or a newspaper headline when no format is named).
  • Ignoring a format that WAS named (an article with no headline, or a diary with no date).
  • Covering a bullet in one sentence, or skipping a bullet completely.
  • Mixing registers: starting formally and slipping into slang or contractions.
  • One huge paragraph, or a new paragraph for every sentence.
  • Repeating the same connective ('and then… and then…').
  • No plan, so the piece runs out of ideas halfway through.
  • No time left to check, so careless spelling and punctuation errors stay in.
Before-you-write checklist
  • I have done G.A.A.P. in the planning space.
  • I know whether a format is named — and its features if it is.
  • I have chosen formal, semi-formal or informal register, and I will keep it.
  • Every bullet has its own paragraph (or more) in my plan.
  • I have an opening technique and an ending idea ready.
  • I have 4–5 ambitious topic words to use and spell correctly.
  • I will use at least one colon, one semicolon (joining two related sentences) and one pair of dashes or brackets — correctly.
  • I have kept 3 minutes to check my work.

اكتب أكثر من 60 كلمة دائماً (من 21 إلى 60 كلمة = 7 درجات كحد أقصى، و20 كلمة أو أقل = صفر). إذا قال السؤال Consider فاكتب عن ثلاث نقاط على الأقل من النقاط المعطاة، وإذا قال Think فهي مجرد اقتراحات — لكن الأفضل أن تغطيها كلها بالتفصيل. اجعل لكل نقطة فقرة كاملة، واربط الفقرات بأدوات ربط متنوعة، واترك آخر 3 دقائق لمراجعة الإملاء وعلامات الترقيم.

11. Practice task

  1. Practice: "A local radio station is asking young people to send in their views. Write to persuade adults in your town to spend more time listening to teenagers. Consider: • what teenagers worry about • what adults could learn from them • one simple change families could make." Write your G.A.A.P. and a five-paragraph plan.

    Show answer
    G: no format word for the text itself (it is sent to a radio station, but no 'letter' or 'speech' is named) → clear paragraphs, no greeting or headline · topic = adults listening to teenagers. A: adults in the town → semi-formal, respectful, no slang. A: 'Consider' → include at least three ideas: worries, what adults could learn, one change. P: persuade. Plan — P1: opening scene (a teenager talking, a parent looking at a phone) + thesis. P2: what teenagers worry about (exams, future, friendships) + example. P3: what adults could learn (technology, fresh ideas, honesty). P4: counter-argument 'teenagers never talk' → because nobody asks. One simple change: a phone-free family dinner once a week. P5: emotional call to action + circular ending back to the opening scene.
