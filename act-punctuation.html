American Diploma · ACT · English: Conventions

ACT Punctuation

Every punctuation rule ACT English tests — commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes, question marks and when to use no punctuation — with ✓/✗ examples and an ACT-style quiz.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

Punctuation is the largest single group of rules in the Conventions of Standard English category, and it is the easiest place to gain points quickly: the rules are fixed and the same traps appear on every test. This page teaches every punctuation mark ACT English uses, with ✓/✗ pairs, then shows worked items and a quiz in real test format.

What this question type tests

Conventions of Standard English is 38–43% of the 40 scored English questions — about 15–17 questions — and punctuation makes up a large part of it. A punctuation question looks like every other ACT English question: a portion of the essay is underlined, and you choose from four versions that differ only in their punctuation. Usually there is no written question, only the choices, with NO CHANGE first.

When you scan the choices and see that only the punctuation changes, ask two questions:

  1. What is on each side of the mark? A complete sentence (independent clause), an incomplete part, an extra-information phrase, a list item?
  2. Which mark is allowed between those two things? Use the tables on this page.
The one test you will use most

To decide if a part is a complete sentence (independent clause), cover everything else and ask: does it have a subject and a verb, and can it stand alone as a sentence? "The answer is simple" → yes. "Such as fans and lamps" → no. "Which my grandfather built" → no.

1. Commas: the six correct uses

UseRule✓ Correct
A. Two complete sentences + FANBOYSComma before for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so when it joins two complete sentences.The bus was late, so we walked to school.
B. After an introductory partComma after an opening word, phrase or clause that comes before the main sentence.After the storm, the streets were quiet. / However, the plan failed.
C. Extra information (a pair)Non-essential words in the middle of a sentence go between two commas.My uncle, who lives in Tanta, grows mangoes.
D. Extra information at the endOne comma before non-essential words at the end.We visited Luxor, a city full of temples.
E. Lists of three or moreCommas between the items; a comma before the final "and" is acceptable.She packed bread, cheese, and olives.
F. Equal adjectivesComma between two adjectives that each describe the noun (you could say "and" between them).It was a long, tiring journey. (✓ long and tiring)

Essential or extra? This is the most tested comma idea. Remove the words: if the sentence still identifies the same person or thing and keeps its main meaning, the words are extra → use commas. If removing them changes which person or thing you mean, they are essential → no commas.

  • ✓ Students who finish early may leave. (Essential: only those students may leave → no commas.)
  • ✗ Students, who finish early, may leave. (This now says all students finish early.)
  • ✓ My brother, Omar, studies in Alexandria. (If I have one brother, his name is extra → commas.)
  • ✓ The novelist Naguib Mahfouz won the Nobel Prize. (The name tells us which novelist → essential → no commas.)
  • ✓ "which" with commas for extra information; "that" with no commas for essential information: The car that I bought is blue. / My car, which is blue, is old.

2. Commas: the misuses the test loves

Most wrong answers on ACT punctuation contain an extra comma somewhere it is not allowed. Learn these "no comma" places:

Never put a comma…✗ Wrong✓ Right
between a subject and its verb (even a long subject)The students in the back row, laughed.The students in the back row laughed.
between a verb and its object or complementThe flood covered, many villages.The flood covered many villages.
to join two complete sentences on its own (comma splice)The road was closed, we walked.The road was closed, so we walked. / The road was closed; we walked.
between two verbs that share one subjectShe opened the door, and walked in.She opened the door and walked in.
after "and" or "but" in a list or a joinbread, cheese, and, olivesbread, cheese, and olives
after such as / including / likeitems such as, fans and lampsitems such as fans and lamps
between an adjective and its nouna long, tiring, journeya long, tiring journey
before or after an essential name or phraseThe novelist, Naguib Mahfouz, wrote it. (if you mean which novelist)The novelist Naguib Mahfouz wrote it.
only once around extra information (half a pair)My uncle, who lives in Tanta grows mangoes.My uncle, who lives in Tanta, grows mangoes.

3. Semicolons

A semicolon (;) works like a soft period. Its main job is to join two complete sentences that are closely related. The test is simple: if you could put a period there, you can usually put a semicolon there.

  • ✓ The library closes at six; the reading room closes at five.
  • ✓ The road was flooded; therefore, the buses stopped. (A semicolon before a transition word such as however, therefore, moreover, which joins two complete sentences, with a comma after the transition.)
  • ✗ The road was flooded, therefore, the buses stopped. (Comma splice: a transition word is not a FANBOYS conjunction.)
  • ✗ Because the road was flooded; the buses stopped. ("Because the road was flooded" is not a complete sentence.)
  • ✗ She bought three things; bread, cheese and olives. (A list after a complete sentence needs a colon, not a semicolon.)
SAT/ACT extra rule: semicolons in a complex list
  • On the SAT and the ACT there is one more use. When the items in a list already contain commas, separate the items with semicolons so the reader can see where each item ends.
  • ✓ The team visited Kom Ombo, where many families had resettled; Aswan, the city closest to the old villages; and Cairo, the capital.
  • ✗ The team visited Kom Ombo, where many families had resettled, Aswan, the city closest to the old villages, and Cairo, the capital.
  • Note the semicolon before the final "and". Use this rule only for lists whose items already contain commas.

4. Colons

A colon (:) comes after a complete sentence and introduces something that the sentence points to: a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information. Before the colon you must have a complete sentence; after it you may have a word, a list, a phrase or another complete sentence.

Use✓ Example
ListShe packed three things: bread, cheese and olives.
ExplanationThe answer was simple: every tool would be checked.
EmphasisThere was only one thing he feared: silence.
Extra informationThe project began with one question: how much of the music survived?
  • ✗ The ingredients are: flour, water and salt. ("The ingredients are" is not complete — the colon cuts the verb from its complement.)
  • ✗ We need things such as: tape, glue and wire. (Never after such as / including.)
  • ✓ We need three things: tape, glue and wire.

5. Dashes

  • A pair of dashes = extra information, exactly like a pair of commas or brackets. The pair must match: dash … dash. ✓ Our teacher — who once rebuilt a radio — will lead the club. ✗ Our teacher — who once rebuilt a radio, will lead the club.
  • A single dash = emphasis or an interruption, usually near the end of a sentence. ✓ He had trained for one moment — the final lap. ✓ "I think we should —" she began, but the bell rang.
  • The part of the sentence outside the dashes must still be a complete, correct sentence when you remove what is inside them.
Commas, dashes, brackets: matching rule

For extra information in the middle of a sentence you may use (, … ,) or (— … —) or ( ( … ) ). All three are correct. What is never correct is mixing them: (, … —) or (— … ,). When the choices mix marks, choose the matching pair.

6. Apostrophes

SituationRule✓ Example
One ownernoun + 'sthe station's design, the child's bag
Several owners ending in -snoun + 'the singers' voices, the students' results
Plural not ending in -snoun + 'sthe children's room, the women's team
Simple plural (no owning)no apostropheMany stations were built. ✗ Many station's were built.
its / it'sits = belonging to it; it's = it is / it hasThe tree lost its leaves. It's raining.
their / they're / theretheir = belonging to them; they're = they are; there = placeThey're proud of their station over there.
whose / who'swhose = belonging to whom; who's = who is / who hasThe boy whose bike was stolen… / Who's coming?

Test for its/it's and the others: expand the contraction. If "it is" or "they are" makes sense in the sentence, use the apostrophe form; if not, use the possessive.

7. Question marks

  • Direct question → question mark: ✓ How much of the music survived?
  • Indirect (reported) question → period: ✓ Some parents asked whether the club is safe. ✗ Some parents asked whether the club is safe?
  • Words such as asked whether, wondered if, want to know how are signals of an indirect question.

8. When NO punctuation is correct

Many students believe that a long sentence needs commas. It does not. On ACT English, the correct answer is often the choice with no punctuation at all. Choose no punctuation:

  • between a subject and its verb, and between a verb and its object;
  • before an essential clause or name (Students who finish early may leave; the poet Ahmed Shawqi);
  • between a preposition and its object (in the garden, not in, the garden);
  • before a clause that begins with that used as an object (She said that she was tired);
  • between two items joined by and (bread and cheese);
  • before a short essential time or reason clause at the end of a sentence (We left when the film ended).

Boundary toolkit: joining two complete sentences

Correct ways to join Sentence 1 + Sentence 2Example
PeriodThe road was closed. We walked.
SemicolonThe road was closed; we walked.
Comma + FANBOYSThe road was closed, so we walked.
Colon (when sentence 2 explains sentence 1)We had no choice: the road was closed.
✗ Comma alone (comma splice)The road was closed, we walked.
✗ Nothing (run-on)The road was closed we walked.

Worked examples

Each example is part of an ACT-style essay. The underlined portion is shown in bold.

Worked example 1. "The museum's newest exhibit, a collection of Pharaonic jewellery opened last week."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) , a collection of Pharaonic jewellery,
  • C) — a collection of Pharaonic jewellery,
  • D) ; a collection of Pharaonic jewellery

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. "a collection of Pharaonic jewellery" is extra information about the exhibit; it opens with a comma and must close with a comma before the verb "opened".
  • A is half a pair: the comma opens the phrase but nothing closes it.
  • C mixes a dash and a comma (no match).
  • D uses a semicolon, but "The museum's newest exhibit" is not a complete sentence.

Worked example 2. "Hoda practised the piano every evening, her neighbours never complained."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) evening her
  • C) evening, and her
  • D) evening, and, her

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. Both parts are complete sentences, and a comma plus the FANBOYS word "and" joins them correctly.
  • A is a comma splice (two complete sentences joined by a comma alone).
  • B is a run-on (no punctuation at all).
  • D adds a second comma after "and", which is never correct.

Worked example 3. "The volunteers brought everything the clinic needed: bandages, blankets and bottled water."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) needed; bandages,
  • C) needed, such as: bandages,
  • D) needed bandages,

Answer: A.

  • A is correct. "The volunteers brought everything the clinic needed" is a complete sentence, and the colon introduces a list.
  • B uses a semicolon before a list, but a semicolon needs a complete sentence after it.
  • C puts a colon straight after "such as", which is never correct.
  • D has no punctuation, so "needed bandages" runs together and the sentence no longer makes sense.

Worked example 4. "Scientists who study desert plants have found that some seeds can wait decades for rain."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) , who study desert plants,
  • C) , who study desert plants
  • D) — who study desert plants,

Answer: A.

  • A is correct. The clause tells us which scientists: only those who study desert plants. It is essential, so no commas.
  • B would mean that all scientists study desert plants (false and not the writer's meaning).
  • C leaves a single comma between the subject "Scientists" and its verb "have found".
  • D mixes a dash and a comma.

Worked example 5. "The school trip had three stops: Alexandria, where we saw the library, Rosetta, famous for its stone, and Tanta."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) library; Rosetta, famous for its stone; and
  • C) library: Rosetta, famous for its stone: and
  • D) library, Rosetta, famous for its stone and

Answer: B.

  • B is correct (SAT/ACT extra rule). The items already contain commas, so semicolons separate the items, including before "and".
  • A uses commas everywhere, so the reader cannot tell where each item ends.
  • C uses colons between list items, which is never correct.
  • D keeps only commas and drops the one before "and".
Classic traps
  • Half a pair: extra information opened with a comma or dash but not closed.
  • Mixed pair: a comma at one end and a dash at the other.
  • Long-subject comma: a comma before the verb because the subject is long.
  • Comma splice: two complete sentences joined by a comma alone, or by a comma + however / therefore.
  • Semicolon + fragment: a semicolon with an incomplete part on one side.
  • Colon after an incomplete start: "The reasons are: …", "such as: …".
  • Apostrophe for plurals: "station's" when you only mean "stations".
  • Question mark on an indirect question: "She asked whether it was safe?"
Fast strategy for test day
  • Scan: if only punctuation changes, find what is on each side of the mark.
  • Two complete sentences? Period, semicolon, comma + FANBOYS, or colon if the second explains the first.
  • Extra information? Check that it opens and closes with the same mark.
  • Subject–verb or verb–object? Choose no punctuation.
  • Two choices identical except for a mark you think is equally correct? Look again — the test only keys one answer, so one of them must break a rule.
الكولون والسيميكولون

السيميكولون (;) بيربط جملتين كاملتين مرتبطين ببعض، وفي SAT/ACT بس بيفصل عناصر قائمة لو كل عنصر فيه فاصلة. الكولون (:) لازم قبله جملة كاملة، وبعده قائمة أو شرح أو تأكيد أو معلومة زيادة. ممنوع الكولون بعد such as أو بعد فعل زي are.

Remember
  • Never a single comma between subject and verb or between verb and object.
  • Extra information: (, … ,) or (— … —) or ( ( … ) ) — always a matching pair; essential information has no commas.
  • Semicolon = joins two complete, closely related sentences. SAT/ACT extra rule: it also separates list items that contain commas.
  • Colon = after a complete sentence, to introduce a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information.
  • Apostrophes: singular 's, plural s'; its = belonging to it, it's = it is. Indirect questions end with a period.
