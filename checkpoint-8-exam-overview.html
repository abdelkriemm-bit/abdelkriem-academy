The Exam: Overview

Everything about the Year 8 English exam (Progression Test Stage 8 and Checkpoint 0861): both papers, timings, marks, what the reading questions and writing tasks ask, results, and a 12-week study plan.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

This page is your map of the Year 8 English exam. Read it first, before any topic page. It tells you what the papers look like, how many marks each part carries, what kinds of questions and writing tasks to expect, how the results are reported, and how to use this course week by week. When you know the shape of the exam, every lesson you study has a clear place in it.

Two tests, one format

Year 8 students in British and international schools usually meet two Cambridge English tests that share exactly the same format:

  • Cambridge Lower Secondary Progression Test English, Stage 8. Your school gives this test in school, often near the end of the year. Your teachers mark it using the Cambridge mark scheme, and the results go into a Cambridge reporting and analysis tool so the school can see your strengths and weaknesses.
  • Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint English (0861). This is the end-of-stage test for Lower Secondary. It is set for the end of Stage 9, but some Egyptian British schools enter students in Year 8 and others in Year 9, so ask your school which year you will sit it. Checkpoint is marked by Cambridge, not by your teachers.

The good news: because the format is the same, everything in this course prepares you for both. The papers, the sections, the mark totals and the way writing is marked are identical. The main differences are who marks the paper and the level of the texts (Checkpoint is set for the end of Stage 9, so its texts may be a little harder).

Key facts to remember
  • Two papers: Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction.
  • Each paper lasts 1 hour 10 minutes and is worth 50 marks.
  • Each paper has Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks).
  • Total for both papers: 100 marks (Reading 50 + Writing 50).
  • Speaking and Listening is part of the curriculum but is not tested in Checkpoint or the Progression Test.

The insert is a separate booklet with the reading text(s). In Paper 1 you may get one non-fiction text, and in some series there are two texts (Text A and Text B) that you may be asked to compare. In Paper 2 the insert is an extract from a novel or short story.

Suggested time is printed on the paper. Recent papers suggest 35 minutes for each section; the 2023 papers said 30 minutes. The suggestion is advice, not a rule, but follow it: students who spend 50 minutes on reading always rush the writing, and writing is half the marks.

Why the time split matters

Section A and Section B are worth the same: 25 marks each. If you spend too long on one 2-mark reading question, you are stealing time from a 25-mark writing task. Wear a watch, write the time you must start Section B at the top of the paper (your start time + 35 minutes), and move on when you reach it.

Section A: Reading — what the questions ask

Section A has roughly 9 to 12 numbered questions, many with parts (a), (b), (c). Each part is usually worth 1 to 4 marks, and they add up to 25. The questions mostly follow the order of the text, and the mark in brackets tells you how much to write. The types below are the ones that keep coming back. Each has its own page in this course.

The reading paper is a grammar paper too

Many students think reading means only 'understanding the text'. In fact, a large part of Section A tests grammar, punctuation and structure in context: word classes, clauses, sentence types, connectives, dashes, colons and semicolons. That is why this course puts the grammar pages first. Learn the terms exactly — 'subordinate clause', 'prepositional phrase', 'abstract noun' — because the questions use them.

Section B: Writing — what the tasks ask

Section B is one extended writing task worth 25 marks. There is a planning space on the paper. Planning is not marked, but a short plan makes your writing better organised, and organisation alone is worth 7 marks.

Paper 1 (non-fiction). Recent papers have asked for pieces such as an article for a school magazine about improving the environment of your area, a competition entry describing a small object you value, a travel article about a place you visited on holiday, and a review for a school newsletter. Article and review are confirmed in recent papers. Other text types in the curriculum can also appear, so this course prepares you for all of them: letters, emails, reports, persuasive and argumentative writing, leaflets, diaries and blogs, biography and autobiography, recounts, instructions and advice.

Paper 2 (fiction). Recent papers have asked for a complete story on a theme linked to the reading extract — for example an unexpected event on holiday with friends in a remote place, a character whose dream comes true, or a person learning a new skill. The task usually gives bullet prompts to guide you (setting, characters, what happens, how it ends). Good stories describe setting and character, not only events.

Both writing tasks are marked with the same five categories: Creation of texts 5, Vocabulary and language 3, Structure of texts 7, Grammar and punctuation 7, Word structure (spelling) 3. The next page, How your writing is marked, explains each one and how to reach the top band.

Length rules that can wipe out your mark
  • A piece of 20 words or fewer scores 0.
  • A piece of 21–60 words can score at most 7 out of 25, however good it is. Sixty words is only about eight handwritten lines.
  • Writing the wrong text type (for example, a story when the task asks for an article) limits your marks heavily.
  • This course trains you to write full answers of 350+ words in 35 minutes, which is what strong students produce.

When the tests happen

Progression Tests are taken in school on dates your school chooses, usually towards the end of the school year. Ask your teacher for the date.

Checkpoint is taken on fixed days set by Cambridge. From 2026 there are three series a year: March (a new series), May (which replaced the old April series) and October. In 2026, for example, the March series ran in early March, the May series in mid-May and the October series in mid to late October. Your school chooses which series you enter and will give you your exact timetable. The papers and durations are the same in every series.

Old paper codes

Until 2023, Lower Secondary Checkpoint English used the code 1111; since the April 2023 series it has been 0861. If you practise with older papers, the reading and writing skills are still useful, but the newest 0861 papers show the current style most accurately.

How results are reported

Checkpoint: Cambridge marks your papers and sends a statement of results to your school. Since May 2023, results are reported on a scale from 0 to 50, with a score for English overall and scores for the strands (Reading and Writing). (Older statements used a 0 to 6.0 scale.) Reporting details can change, so treat this as a guide and check the current format with your school's exams officer. There is no pass or fail: the score shows how far you have progressed in the Cambridge curriculum.

Progression Test: your teachers mark it and enter the marks into Cambridge's reporting and analysis tool. Your school may give you your marks out of 50 for each paper or a summary of strengths and areas to improve.

Either way, the best use of a result is to diagnose: it shows whether Reading or Writing needs more work, and which question types you lose marks on.

How to use this course

  1. Start here (2 pages). Read this overview and the writing marking page. Keep the five writing categories in your head for every piece you write.
  2. Reading: grammar and punctuation (6 pages). Parts of speech, phrases and clauses, sentence types, connectives and linking, punctuation, grammar in use. Each page ends with exam-style practice and answers. Do these first: they also improve your own writing (Grammar and punctuation is worth 7 writing marks).
  3. Reading: understanding texts (7 pages). Text types and features, purpose and viewpoint, inference and summary, language effects, vocabulary skills, poetry and drama, and reading exam technique (how to answer 1-, 2- and 4-mark questions).
  4. Paper 1 writing (20 packs). One pack per non-fiction genre: what it is, features, structure, phrases, vocabulary, a strong 350+ word sample, common mistakes and a practice task.
  5. Paper 2 writing (13 packs). Story-writing basics, setting, character and suspense, then one pack per fiction genre with a strong sample.

For every topic page: read the explanation, cover the answers and do the practice questions, then check every answer and read why it is right. For every writing pack: learn the structure and the vocabulary words, read the sample with its notes, then write the practice task in 35 minutes and check it against the five categories.

A 12-week study plan

Use this plan for the last twelve weeks before your Progression Test or Checkpoint. Each week has about 4–5 hours of work: two or three short sessions on reading topics and one timed writing task. If you have more time, do the plan twice: the second time, add full papers under timed conditions.

Keep a mistakes notebook

Every time you lose a mark, write down the question type, your answer, the correct answer and the reason. Read this notebook every week. Most students lose marks on the same five or six question types again and again — the notebook shows you which ones are yours.

Final advice from your teacher

  • Learn the grammar terms exactly. A question that asks for 'the main clause' or 'an abstract noun' gives no marks for something close.
  • Read the whole insert once before answering. Then read each question twice and underline the key word (identify, explain, effect, own words, quotation).
  • Let the marks guide your length: one mark = one clear point. Two marks = a point + explanation, or two points. Four marks = two points, each with a quotation and an explanation.
  • In the writing, cover every point in the task, use paragraphs, vary your sentences and punctuation, and leave three minutes to check spelling.
  • Practise against the clock. Knowing the content is not enough; you must produce it in 35 minutes.
لأولياء الأمور: ما هو امتحان Checkpoint لطلاب Year 8؟

طالب Year 8 يمتحن امتحان Progression Test Stage 8 داخل المدرسة، ثم امتحان Cambridge Checkpoint 0861 (في Year 8 أو Year 9 حسب المدرسة). الامتحانان بنفس الشكل تمامًا: ورقتان، الأولى نصوص غير قصصية (Non-fiction) والثانية قصة (Fiction). كل ورقة ساعة و10 دقائق و50 درجة: 25 للقراءة و25 للكتابة، والمجموع 100. لا يوجد امتحان استماع أو تحدث. امتحان Checkpoint تصححه كامبريدج، أما Progression Test فيصححه مدرسو المدرسة. النتيجة ليست نجاحًا أو رسوبًا، بل توضح مستوى الطالب في القراءة والكتابة.

كيف تساعد ابنك؟
  • تأكد أنه يذاكر القواعد أولًا (أقسام الكلام، الجمل، علامات الترقيم) لأن أسئلة القراءة تعتمد عليها كثيرًا.
  • اطلب منه أن يكتب موضوعًا كاملًا كل أسبوع في 35 دقيقة بالضبط، لا يقل عن 350 كلمة.
  • انتبه: لو كتب 60 كلمة أو أقل لن يحصل على أكثر من 7 من 25 في الكتابة مهما كان مستواه.
  • شجعه على كراسة للأخطاء يكتب فيها كل سؤال أخطأ فيه وسبب الخطأ، ويراجعها كل أسبوع.
