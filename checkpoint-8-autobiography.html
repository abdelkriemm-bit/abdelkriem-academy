Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Autobiography

How to write your own life story for Paper 1: features, the four-paragraph structure, phrases, vocabulary, a full-mark sample and a practice task.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is an autobiography?

An autobiography is the story of a person's life — or an important part of it — written by that person. The word explains itself: *auto* = self, *bio* = life, *graphy* = writing. It does not just list events. It shares the writer's thoughts, feelings and reflections on those events, so the reader comes to understand the writer's personality.

QuestionAnswer
PurposeTo recount key events of your own life and to reflect on how they shaped you. It often also aims to entertain and to inform the reader about who you are.
AudienceUsually readers who do not know you: a school book or website, a competition judge, classmates, a magazine's readers.
VoiceFirst person (I, me, my, we) — you are telling your own story.
Where in the examPaper 1 (Non-fiction), Section B Writing, 25 marks, about 35 minutes. Autobiography is one of the recount-type texts in the curriculum that can appear; the task may call it 'a chapter of your autobiography', 'your life story' or 'write about yourself for…'.
Text typeWho writes about whom?PersonMain focus
AutobiographyYou write about your own lifeFirst personSeveral key parts of your life + reflection on who you are
BiographyYou write about someone else's lifeThird personFacts, dates and achievements of another person
RecountYou retell one event or experienceUsually first personOne event in time order + a reflection
DiaryYou write privately to yourself about recent daysFirst personToday's events and feelings, informal, dated

What earns marks in an autobiography

CriterionMarksWhat full marks look like in an autobiography
Creation of texts5Every bullet covered and developed, a clear personal voice, and real feelings and reflection, not just facts. The reader feels they know you.
Vocabulary and language3Precise words for feelings and memories (nostalgia, formative, resilience) and vivid description of key moments.
Structure of texts7Title + clear paragraphs (sub-headings are a bonus), logical order from past to future, time connectives, a strong opening and a reflective ending that links back.
Grammar and punctuation7Past tenses used accurately (with present for 'now' and future for dreams), varied sentence types, and wide, accurate punctuation.
Word structure (spelling)3Accurate spelling of ambitious words and of tricky everyday ones.

3. How to structure an autobiography

This is the teacher's four-paragraph structure. You may use sub-headings for each paragraph to show your organisation clearly. If the task gives bullet points, fit each bullet into the matching paragraph.

PartWhat to writeTips
TitleA short, intriguing title that captures your storyUse a metaphor or a key object/idea: 'The Compass in My Pocket'.
Paragraph 1 — About myselfDate and place of birth, your country/city, family members and what they are like, early childhood, friends and schoolDo not just list facts: give each one a detail or a feeling. One vivid opening sentence.
Paragraph 2 — Key aspects of my lifeThe most influential person, the object you value most, something that worries or frightens you, and your hobbiesExplain WHY each one matters, with a small memory or example.
Paragraph 3 — The most unforgettable momentWhat happened (in order), your thoughts, feelings and wishes at the time, and the impact it had on youThis is your 'mini recount' — slow it down, use the senses, and end with the lesson.
Paragraph 4 — Reflection and future goals(a) Describe yourself: strengths and weaknesses with examples, and (b) your lifelong dreamEnd by linking back to your title or opening image.
Turn a fact into a strong sentence

Fact only: "I have one brother." Developed: "My little brother, Adam, is a whirlwind of questions, and no meal in our house ends without at least one of them." Every fact you include should carry a detail, a feeling or a picture.

4. Useful phrases and sentence starters

Openings

  • I was born on [date] in [place], a time and place that would shape much of who I am today.
  • If you had told me as a child that…, I would never have believed you.
  • Every life has a turning point; mine came when I was…
  • My story begins in a small flat above a bakery, where the smell of fresh bread woke me every morning.
  • People who meet me today see…; few of them know the story behind it.

Childhood and family

  • As a child, I spent most of my days…
  • My parents always taught me the importance of…
  • I remember my early years as a time of…
  • Our house was never quiet, because…

Influential people and objects

  • The person who has shaped me most is…
  • I will never forget the day he/she told me…
  • The object I treasure most is…, because…
  • Whenever I hold it, I remember…

Fears, struggles and challenges

  • If I am honest, the thing I fear most is…
  • One of the hardest moments of my life was when…
  • I struggled with…, but it pushed me to grow stronger.

The unforgettable moment

  • The most unforgettable moment of my life took place…
  • At that moment, I felt…
  • For one terrifying second, …
  • Suddenly, I realised that…

Reflection

  • Looking back, I realise that…
  • That experience taught me that…
  • I would describe myself as… because…
  • However, I can sometimes be…, especially when…

Closings

  • My story is only just beginning, but I already know which direction I am heading in.
  • As I look towards the future, I carry every lesson from my past with me.
  • Though I have faced many challenges, I am proud of the person I am becoming.
  • The next chapter is still unwritten, and I cannot wait to write it.

5. Strong sample

Sample — 'Learning to Swim Against the Current'505 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school is producing a book called 'Our Stories', in which every Year 8 student writes one chapter of their autobiography. Write your chapter. Consider: • where you come from and your family • the people and things that matter most to you • one moment that changed you • the person you hope to become.

Learning to Swim Against the Current

About Myself

I was born on a stormy February night in 2013 in Mansoura, a busy city on the banks of the Nile, and my mother still jokes that I arrived as noisily as the thunder outside. I am the middle child of three. My older sister, Nour, is the serious one who organises everybody, while my little brother, Adam, is a whirlwind of questions. My father is an engineer and my mother teaches chemistry, so our dinner table has always been part restaurant, part laboratory. When I was eight, we moved to Cairo for my father's work, and I had to leave behind the only school and the only friends I had ever known.

Key Aspects of My Life

The person who has shaped me most is my grandfather, Hassan. Every summer, he took me to his small farm outside Mansoura, where he taught me to plant tomatoes, mend a fence and — most importantly — listen before I speak. The object I treasure most is his old brass compass, which he gave me on my tenth birthday. “So you never lose your way,” he said, pressing it into my hand. If I am honest, the thing I fear most is disappointing the people who believe in me. When I am not worrying, I am usually drawing comic strips or swimming, which is strange, because water once terrified me.

The Moment That Changed Me

That fear ended on a grey morning when I was ten. My school in Cairo held a swimming gala, and I had secretly signed up to impress my classmates, even though I could barely float. As I stood at the edge of the pool, my legs trembled and my heart hammered against my ribs. The whistle blew. I jumped, swallowed what felt like half the pool and, for one terrifying second, sank. Then I remembered my grandfather's calm voice: “Slowly, Youssef. One stroke at a time.” I kicked. I reached. I breathed. I finished last, a whole length behind everyone else, but the entire stand rose to its feet and cheered. Climbing out, dripping and shaking, I realised something that has stayed with me ever since: courage is not the absence of fear; it is moving forward while you are still afraid.

Who I Am and Who I Hope to Become

Looking back, I can see both my strengths and my weaknesses clearly. I am determined and loyal; once I start something, I refuse to give up on it or on the people involved. However, I can be impatient, especially when a drawing does not turn out the way I imagined, and I have torn up more than one comic in frustration. My lifelong dream is to become an architect who designs safe swimming pools and green parks for children in crowded cities, so that no child grows up as afraid of the water as I once was. My story is only just beginning, but I already know which way my compass is pointing.

Why this gets the marks
  • Word count: the figure given counts the whole piece, including the title and four sub-headings (the body paragraphs alone are well over 350 words).
  • Creation of texts 5/5: all four bullets are covered and developed — family and origins (P1), people and things that matter: grandfather, compass, fear, hobbies (P2), the moment that changed me (P3), the person I hope to become (P4). The voice is personal and honest, feelings and reflection appear in every paragraph, and the hobby of swimming in P2 sets up the story in P3, which keeps the reader interested.
  • Text type features: title, first person throughout, sub-headings, past tense for the life story, present tense for 'now' (I am determined…), future aims at the end, remembered speech, a turning point and an honest self-description (strengths AND a weakness with an example).
  • Vocabulary 3/3: precise and vivid choices — 'whirlwind of questions', 'part restaurant, part laboratory', 'trembled', 'hammered against my ribs', 'determined and loyal', 'frustration'. The compass works as a symbol for direction in life.
  • Structure 7/7: the teacher's four-part structure with sub-headings, and clear time links ('When I was eight', 'Every summer', 'That fear ended…', 'Looking back'). P2 ends with the fear of water and P3 opens with 'That fear ended…', a strong link between paragraphs. The ending returns to the compass from P2, giving a circular, satisfying close.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 (sentences): deliberate variety — very short simple sentences at the climax ('The whistle blew.' 'I kicked. I reached. I breathed.') to slow the moment down, long complex sentences with subordinate clauses for background ('When I was eight, we moved to Cairo…'), accurate past simple, past continuous and present forms.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 (punctuation): a pair of dashes for extra information ('— most importantly —'), a colon introducing an explanation ('…ever since: courage is…'), semicolons joining two related independent clauses ('…absence of fear; it is moving forward…', 'determined and loyal; once I start…'), inverted commas and commas for direct speech, and commas after fronted adverbials and around a relative clause.
  • Spelling 3/3: ambitious and commonly misspelt words are accurate — 'organises' (British -ise), 'laboratory', 'treasure', 'disappointing', 'terrifying', 'frustration', 'architect'.

6. Common mistakes

  • Writing a list of facts (birthday, school, favourite food) with no feelings or reflection — this sounds like a form, not an autobiography.
  • Trying to tell your whole life in tiny pieces instead of slowing down at ONE key moment.
  • Slipping into the third person ('Youssef was born…') — that turns it into a biography.
  • Tense confusion: jumping between past and present without a reason. Use past for the story, present only for what is true now.
  • Skipping a bullet (often the 'future' or 'what changed you' point).
  • Describing only strengths. An honest weakness with an example shows maturity and earns Creation marks.
  • Ending suddenly ('That's it. The end.') instead of a reflective closing that links back.
  • Using too many informal expressions ('it was so cool', 'loads of') for a school book audience.
Autobiography checklist
  • Title + (optional) sub-headings.
  • First person all the way through.
  • Paragraph 1: birth, place, family, early childhood — each with a detail.
  • Paragraph 2: influential person, treasured object, a fear, a hobby — each explained.
  • Paragraph 3: one unforgettable moment, slowed down, with senses, feelings and a lesson.
  • Paragraph 4: strengths and a weakness with examples + lifelong dream.
  • Time connectives link the paragraphs; the ending links back to the opening or title.
  • Checked: tenses, a colon, a semicolon joining two related sentences, a pair of dashes or commas, spelling of ambitious words.

Practice task

  1. Practice: "A youth magazine is running a competition called 'The Year Everything Changed'. Write a chapter of your autobiography for the competition. Think about: • what your life was like before • the change that happened • how you felt at the time • how it has changed you." Write a short plan.

    Show answer
    G.A.A.P.: autobiography chapter (title, first person, sub-headings optional) · audience = magazine readers and judges → semi-formal, engaging · four content points · purpose = recount + reflect + entertain. Title: 'The Year the House Fell Silent' (e.g. the year an older brother left to study abroad). P1 Before: family routines, noisy house, the brother's jokes. P2 The change: the airport goodbye — senses, short sentences, remembered words. P3 Feelings at the time: loneliness, anger, then slowly new responsibility (helping a younger sister with homework). P4 How it changed me: more independent and patient, a weakness that remains, a dream for the future, and a link back to the 'silent house' image, now filled with your own voice.
