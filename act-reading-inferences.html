American Diploma · ACT · Reading: Key Ideas and Details

Inference Questions: "It Can Reasonably Be Inferred"

How to answer ACT Reading inference questions: take one small step from the text, prove every choice with evidence, and reject extreme or invented answers.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Inference questions ask what the passage suggests or implies: something that is not written word for word but that the text makes very clear. They belong to Key Ideas and Details, the largest reporting category on ACT Reading (44–52% of the scored questions). You will meet them with every passage type: in literary narratives they usually ask about a character's feelings or motives; in informational passages they ask what a finding, example or claim implies.

Stem (in our own words)What it means
"It can reasonably be inferred that …" / "It can most reasonably be inferred from paragraph 3 that …"Which conclusion does the text strongly support?
"The passage most strongly suggests that …" / "…implies that …"Same idea: the text points to it without saying it directly
"The detail that X most strongly suggests that the character:"What does this action or description reveal?
"The narrator most likely …" / "…most likely in order to:"The most probable reading of a motive or situation, based on the text
"Which statement is best supported by the passage?"Pick the choice with the strongest textual evidence

The words *reasonably, most strongly, most likely* and *best supported* are important. The ACT is not asking what COULD be true. It is asking which choice the text supports better than all the others, and on the ACT that is usually a choice very close to the words of the passage.

One small step

An ACT inference is one small step beyond the text. You start from a sentence (or two) in the passage, and you move to a conclusion that almost anyone reading those sentences would agree with. If you need to imagine a second fact, a hidden reason or a future event, you have gone too far.

The text says✓ One step✗ Too far
"He had told his mother it had gone fine. He had not told anyone about the half-page he had left empty."✓ He is more worried than he has admitted.✗ He will fail chemistry. / ✗ He often lies to his mother.
"The newspaper lay open at the same page … for the past hour."✓ He was not really reading.✗ He cannot read. / ✗ He hates the newspaper.
"The number of people using the study tables nearly doubled."✓ Many visitors come to use the space.✗ Visitors no longer read books at home.
"Cities with more trees recorded lower summer temperatures."✓ Trees and temperature appear to be linked.✗ Planting trees will end summer heat.
الـ Inference = خطوة واحدة صغيرة

الاستنتاج في الـ ACT مش تخمين ولا تأليف. ابدأ من جملة في القطعة واسأل: إيه الحاجة اللي أي حد يقرا الجملة دي هيوافق عليها؟ دي الإجابة. لو لقيت نفسك بتقول «يمكن عشان…» وبتضيف حاجات مش مكتوبة، يبقى الاختيار ده غلط.

The method

  1. Find the evidence first. Go to the paragraph, line or detail named in the stem. If none is named, find the part of the passage the question is about.
  2. Say literally what the text says. "He made tea nobody asked for, stood at the window, and never turned the page."
  3. Take one step. "He is restless and anxious." This is your prediction.
  4. Test every choice with the finger test: can you put your finger on words in the passage that support this choice? If the support is "maybe" or "in real life", eliminate it.
  5. Prefer the cautious choice. When two choices seem possible, the one with softer, more limited language (*may, some, partly, suggests*) is usually safer than the one with absolute language — unless the text itself is absolute.
  6. Check the whole passage. A correct inference never contradicts anything elsewhere in the text, especially the ending.

Extreme language: the inference killer

Wrong inference choices often take a reasonable idea and make it too strong. Learn to spot the words that do it.

Extreme (usually wrong)Cautious (often right)
always, never, all, none, every, onlyoften, rarely, many, some, a few, mainly
proves, certain, must, completely, entirelysuggests, likely, may, partly, to some extent
the best, the worst, the most important of allvaluable, useful, one of the …
  • ✗ "He never shows any emotion to his family." (two absolutes: never + any)
  • ✓ "He prefers to show his feelings through actions rather than words."
  • ✗ "She was certain he would get top grades in every subject."
  • ✓ "He had prepared a gift before the results came out."
  • An extreme word is correct only if the passage is equally extreme. If the narrator says "I lost every game for two weeks," then "every" is supported.

Inference in literary passages

Writers of fiction often show feelings instead of naming them. The questions ask you to name what is shown. Read actions, small details and dialogue as evidence:

  • Actions: making tea nobody asked for, standing instead of sitting → restlessness, nerves.
  • What a character does NOT say: "No reason." after an obviously meaningful story → he is hiding his purpose, not lacking one.
  • Small physical details: a crushed ribbon on a gift → it was carried for a long time.
  • Excuses that do not match behaviour: "I came only because the light was bad" + anxious behaviour → the excuse is cover.
  • The ending: the final lines often confirm (or reveal) what earlier details only hinted. Check your inference against the last paragraph.

Worked examples

Read this short social science excerpt (shorter than a real ACT passage, so we can focus on the method).

When the city library extended its opening hours until ten at night, staff expected most of the evening visitors to be university students. Instead, the largest group turned out to be adults between thirty and fifty, many of them still dressed in their work clothes. The number of books borrowed in the evening rose only slightly, but the number of people using the study tables and the free internet terminals nearly doubled within six months. The library has since added more electrical sockets along the walls of the reading room.

Worked example 1 — inference from data

Question: It can most reasonably be inferred that many of the evening visitors come to the library mainly to:

  • A) borrow more books than daytime visitors do.
  • B) escape from crowded and noisy homes.
  • C) use its space and equipment rather than to borrow books.
  • D) prepare for university entrance exams.

Answer: C. Evidence: borrowing "rose only slightly," while use of the study tables and internet terminals "nearly doubled." One step: the space and equipment are the main attraction.

  • A is wrong — Contradicted: borrowing rose only slightly, and no comparison with daytime visitors is given.
  • B is wrong — Two steps / outside the text: possible in real life, but nothing in the excerpt mentions homes.
  • D is wrong — Unsupported, and the visitors are mostly working adults, not the students staff expected.

Worked example 2 — what a detail suggests

Question: The detail about the electrical sockets most strongly suggests that:

  • A) the library had serious electrical problems before.
  • B) many visitors bring devices that need power while they work.
  • C) evening visitors prefer laptops to books in every situation.
  • D) the library plans to remove its internet terminals.

Answer: B. More sockets along the reading-room walls after evening use of the tables grew: one step is that visitors need to plug things in.

  • A is wrong — Outside the text: no earlier problems are mentioned.
  • C is wrong — Too extreme: "in every situation" goes far beyond one detail.
  • D is wrong — Unsupported: nothing suggests removing the terminals; their use doubled.

Now a short literary excerpt. Read the actions, not just the words.

Karim's father read the school report standing in the doorway, his coat still on. He read it twice. Then he put it on the shelf beside the keys, hung up his coat and asked Karim to help him carry the shopping in from the car. Neither of them mentioned the report at dinner. Later that night, Karim noticed that the report had been moved from the shelf to the drawer where his father kept important papers: the papers for the flat, his own old certificates, the children's birth certificates.

Worked example 3 — character's attitude

Question: The father's moving the report to the drawer most strongly suggests that he:

  • A) is angry about Karim's grades and wants to hide the report.
  • B) never discusses school with any of his children.
  • C) plans to show the report to Karim's teachers.
  • D) considers the report worth keeping with his most important papers.

Answer: D. The drawer holds certificates and family documents. One step: he values the report enough to keep it there.

  • A is wrong — Two steps: nothing shows anger, and the drawer is for keeping valuable papers, not hiding bad ones.
  • B is wrong — Too extreme: one silent dinner does not mean "never … any."
  • C is wrong — Invented: no plan is mentioned.

Worked example 4 — best supported

Question: Which statement is best supported by the excerpt?

  • A) The father's actions reveal a reaction that his words do not.
  • B) The father was too tired to read the report carefully.
  • C) Karim was disappointed with his own report.
  • D) The father always puts important papers on the shelf by the keys.

Answer: A. He says nothing about the report, but he reads it twice and later files it with important papers. A is a cautious summary of that evidence.

  • B is wrong — Contradicted: he "read it twice."
  • C is wrong — Unsupported: Karim's feelings about his grades are never described.
  • D is wrong — Reversed and extreme: the report was moved FROM the shelf TO the important-papers drawer.
Classic traps
  • Two steps too far. The choice could be true, but you need an extra, unstated fact to get there.
  • Extreme language. always, never, all, only, certain, proves, completely — unless the text is just as strong.
  • Outside knowledge. True in real life or common sense, but not in this passage.
  • Taking an excuse literally. In fiction, characters often say one thing and show another; the actions usually win.
  • Contradicting the ending. A choice that fits one paragraph but clashes with the final paragraph is wrong.
  • Reversed attitude. Using the passage's words to say the opposite of what the character feels or the author thinks.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Find the evidence lines first; never answer an inference question from general impression.
  • Say the evidence literally, then take one small step: that is your prediction.
  • Use the finger test on every choice.
  • Cross out choices with extreme words unless the text is equally extreme.
  • Between two good choices, pick the more cautious one that the text supports more directly.

Practice

Now try a full literary narrative (a short story of about 650 words) with 9 inference questions. It is untimed, but aim for about 10 minutes. Collect the small details as you read: the actions tell you more than the words.

Remember
  • Inference questions are part of Key Ideas and Details, 44–52% of scored ACT Reading questions.
  • An inference is ONE small step from the text, never a guess.
  • Every correct choice passes the finger test: you can point to the supporting words.
  • Extreme words (always, never, all, only, proves) usually signal a wrong choice.
  • In fiction, read actions and details as evidence, and check your answer against the ending.
