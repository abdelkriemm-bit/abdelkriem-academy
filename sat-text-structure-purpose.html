Text Structure and Purpose

Learn to name the job of every sentence so you can answer main purpose, overall structure and underlined-sentence function questions quickly and accurately.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Text Structure and Purpose questions ask you to think like a writer. The question is not "What does the text say?" but "What is the text doing, and how is it built?" You read one short passage (25–150 words) and answer one four-option question about the job of the whole text, the order of its ideas, or the job of one underlined sentence.

The question usually comes in one of three forms (in our own words):

  • Main purpose: "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?"
  • Overall structure: "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"
  • Function of a part: "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?" (Sometimes the underlined part is a phrase or the last sentence.)

This skill belongs to the Craft and Structure domain, together with Words in Context and Cross-Text Connections. Craft and Structure is about 28% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (13–15 questions across both modules). Craft and Structure questions usually come near the start of each module, so these questions are often among the first you see. Passages come from literature, history/social studies, the humanities and science.

The big idea

Every sentence in an SAT passage has a job. One sentence introduces a topic, the next gives an example, the next challenges an old idea. If you can name each job in two or three words, structure and purpose questions become simple matching.

The method: label every sentence's job

Use this method on every purpose, structure and function question. It takes about 40 seconds once you have practised it.

  1. Read the whole text before the choices. Do not read the answers first; they are written to sound good.
  2. Label each sentence in 2–3 words. Use a verb: "states old view", "gives example", "rejects old view", "describes setting", "shows feeling changes". Write the labels on your scratch paper as S1, S2, S3…
  3. Find the turn. Words like however, yet, but, in fact, recently and surprisingly often mark the most important move in the text. The sentence after the turn is usually the author's real point.
  4. Answer the exact question. Purpose = the job of ALL the labels together (usually the main claim). Structure = the labels in order ("X, then Y"). Function = the job of ONE sentence in relation to the sentences around it.
  5. Predict before you look. Say your answer in one short line: "to show that an old belief about dandelions is wrong".
  6. Check the verb first, then the content. Eliminate any choice whose verb is wrong ("criticize" when the text only describes). Then check that every word in the remaining choice is true. A choice that is half right is all wrong.

A quick demonstration. Imagine a four-sentence science text. S1: "Scientists long believed that X." → *states old view*. S2: "A new study found Y." → *gives new evidence*. S3: "Y cannot happen if X is true." → *shows conflict*. S4: "The authors suggest that X may need revision." → *proposes change*. Purpose: to present evidence that challenges an earlier belief. Structure: an old view, new evidence, and a suggested revision. Function of S3: it explains why the new evidence conflicts with the old view.

Function verbs: the vocabulary of the answer choices

The answer choices are built from a small set of verbs. Learn exactly what each one means. Many wrong answers use a verb that is too strong (criticize, refute, prove) or simply the wrong kind of move.

Attitude check

Before you choose "criticize", "praise", "mock" or "refute", find words in the passage that show that attitude. Most SAT science and history texts are fairly neutral: they describe, explain, present and suggest. An extreme verb with no matching words in the text is a trap.

Literature passages

In fiction, plays and poems, the "purpose" is usually to show something about a character, a relationship, a setting or a mood. The structure is often a shift: from calm to worry, from memory to the present, from outside description to inner feeling. Useful labels:

  • "describes setting", "introduces character", "shows character's feeling"
  • "shift in mood" (often after but, yet, then, suddenly)
  • "flashback / memory", "returns to present"
  • "reveals what character values", "ends with a realization"

Literature choices often say "to portray", "to depict", "to convey" or "to characterize". Ask: *portray what, exactly?* The right answer names the specific feeling or change that the whole passage shows, not just one image.

Science and social-science passages

Science texts usually follow one of a few patterns. If you recognise the pattern, you can predict the structure answer before reading the choices.

PatternTypical labelsTypical correct purpose / structure wording
Old view → new findingstates assumption · gives study · draws conclusionto present a finding that challenges a long-held assumption
Puzzle → explanationdescribes puzzle · offers hypothesis · says what would confirm itto describe a puzzle and a possible explanation for it
Question → experiment → resultasks how · describes method · gives resultto describe a study that investigated how…
Phenomenon → how it worksintroduces ability · explains process · gives effectto introduce a feature and explain how it works
Problem → proposed solutiondescribes problem · proposes response · gives reasonto identify a problem and propose a way to address it

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Main purpose (science)

Many gardeners pull up dandelions the moment they appear, treating the plant as nothing more than a weed. Yet in early spring, when few other flowers have opened, dandelions are among the only sources of pollen and nectar available to bees coming out of winter. Agricultural researcher Leila Mansour has suggested that leaving some dandelions in lawns until other plants bloom could give local bee populations a valuable early-season boost.

Question: Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

  • A) To argue that dandelions are more attractive than most garden flowers
  • B) To present a view of dandelions that challenges a common attitude toward them
  • C) To explain the process by which bees collect pollen from early-season plants
  • D) To criticize researchers who have ignored the role of weeds in bee survival

Prediction before looking: "to show that dandelions, usually seen as weeds, are useful for bees."

Answer: B

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Off-topic / distorted. The text is about the value of dandelions to bees, not about how attractive they look.
  • B — ✓ Correct. Labels: S1 common attitude (dandelions = weeds) · S2 "Yet" → shows their value to bees · S3 researcher suggests keeping some. Together the text pushes against the common attitude. Every part of this choice matches.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Too narrow / off-topic. Bees are mentioned, but the text never explains how they collect pollen.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Wrong verb and wrong target. The only researcher mentioned (Mansour) is presented positively; no one is criticized.

Worked example 2 — Overall structure (literature)

Amal had rehearsed the speech so often that the words had worn smooth, like stones in a stream. She could recite it in the shower, on the bus, half asleep. But when she stepped onto the stage and saw three hundred faces turned toward her, the first sentence vanished. For a long moment she heard only the hum of the lights. Then she found her father in the fourth row, nodding slowly, and the words came back one by one.

Question: Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

  • A) It describes a character's thorough preparation, presents a moment when that preparation seems to fail her, and then shows how she recovers.
  • B) It introduces a character's fear of public speaking and then explains how that fear began in her childhood.
  • C) It contrasts a character's careful preparation with her father's lack of interest in her performance.
  • D) It describes a stage in detail before revealing the character's reaction to the audience.

Answer: A

  • A — ✓ Correct. Labels: S1–S2 well prepared · S3 "But" → mind goes blank · S4 silence · S5 "Then" → sees father, recovers. Three parts in the right order: preparation, failure, recovery.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Half-right / unsupported. She does freeze, but nothing in the text explains where a fear came from or mentions her childhood.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Reversed detail. The father is "nodding slowly"; he supports her and helps her recover. He is not uninterested.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Wrong structure. The stage is barely described (only the faces and the hum of the lights), and the text starts with her preparation, not the stage.

Worked example 3 — Function of an underlined sentence (science)

Octopuses can change the color of their skin in a fraction of a second, a skill they use both to hide from predators and to signal to one another. Strangely, their eyes appear to lack the types of cells that allow many other animals to tell colors apart. __Some researchers have proposed that light-sensitive proteins found in octopus skin may let the animals sense light through their bodies as well as their eyes.__ If this idea is confirmed, it could help explain how a seemingly color-blind animal can match its surroundings so precisely.

Question: Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  • A) It describes evidence confirming that octopuses can see color with their eyes.
  • B) It challenges the claim that octopuses change color to hide from predators.
  • C) It summarizes the findings of a study that settled a long-running debate.
  • D) It presents a possible explanation for a puzzle described in the previous sentence.

Answer: D

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Contradicts the text. The previous sentence says their eyes seem to LACK color cells, and the underlined idea is about skin, not eyes.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Wrong target. Nothing in the text questions why octopuses change color; the first sentence states it as fact.
  • C — ✗ Wrong. Too extreme. "Proposed", "may" and "If this idea is confirmed" show the idea is NOT yet settled.
  • D — ✓ Correct. Labels: S1 amazing ability · S2 "Strangely" → puzzle · S3 (underlined) possible answer · S4 what it would explain. "Proposed… may" = a possible explanation. Correct.

Worked example 4 — Function of an underlined sentence (history)

Historians have often described the printing press as the invention that ended the age of handwritten books. __It is true that printed books quickly became cheaper and more common than manuscripts in fifteenth-century Europe.__ However, scribes continued to copy texts by hand for decades, partly because wealthy buyers valued decorated manuscripts as luxury objects. The shift from pen to press, in other words, was far slower than the familiar story suggests.

Question: Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  • A) It states the main claim that the author defends throughout the text.
  • B) It introduces a counterargument that the author goes on to reject completely.
  • C) It acknowledges a fact that supports the common view before the author qualifies that view.
  • D) It explains why wealthy buyers preferred decorated manuscripts to printed books.

Signal to notice: "It is true that… However…" is the classic concede-then-qualify move. The sentence after "It is true that" is almost always a concession.

Answer: C

  • A — ✗ Wrong. Wrong sentence. The author's main claim is the LAST sentence: the shift was slower than people think.
  • B — ✗ Wrong. Too extreme. "It is true that" means the author ACCEPTS this fact. The author does not reject it but goes on to limit the common story.
  • C — ✓ Correct. Labels: S1 common view · S2 (underlined) "It is true that" → concession · S3 "However" → limits the view · S4 author's point. A concession followed by a qualification. Correct.
  • D — ✗ Wrong. Wrong sentence. The reason about wealthy buyers is in the NEXT sentence, not the underlined one. A true detail from the text, but not this sentence's job.
Classic traps
  • Wrong verb: the content is right but the action is wrong ("criticize" for a neutral description; "prove" for a suggestion).
  • Half-right: the first half of the choice matches the text, the second half adds something false or unmentioned. Read every word to the end.
  • Too narrow: a purpose answer that describes only one sentence or one detail of the text.
  • Wrong sentence: in function questions, a choice that describes what the NEXT or PREVIOUS sentence does. Ask only what the underlined part does.
  • Reversed: the right ideas in the wrong order or with the relationship flipped (supports ↔ challenges).
  • Too extreme: "settles the debate", "proves", "completely rejects" when the text uses may, might, suggests, if confirmed.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Label each sentence in 2–3 words on your scratch paper. It feels slow at first; it is faster than rereading.
  • Circle the turn word (however, yet, but, in fact). The sentence after it usually carries the main point.
  • For function questions, read one sentence before and one after the underlined part.
  • Eliminate by verb first; you can often remove two choices in five seconds.
  • If two choices remain, pick the one that is more modest and fully supported. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so never leave a question blank.

Practice

Twelve original questions in real test format. Label the sentences first, predict, then choose. Each answer shows an explanation.

Remember
  • Label every sentence's job in 2–3 words, using a verb.
  • Purpose = the job of the whole text; structure = the jobs in order; function = one sentence's job relative to its neighbours.
  • The turn word (however, yet, but) usually introduces the main point.
  • Check the verb first: neutral texts need neutral verbs (describe, explain, present, suggest).
  • Half-right is all wrong: every word of the choice must match the text.
