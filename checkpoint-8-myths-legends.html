Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Myths and Legends

How to write a myth or legend for Paper 2: gods, heroes, quests and explanations of the natural world — features, a five-stage plan, phrases, vocabulary, a Greek mythology quick reference and a full-mark 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What are myths and legends?

Myths and legends are grand, ancient-feeling stories starring gods, heroes and fantastic creatures, set in a distant and mystical past. Where a fable ends with a neat, stated moral, a myth often exists to explain how something in the world came to be (why the moon changes shape, why the Nile floods, why the spider weaves), and its hero proves their worth through a dangerous quest.

MythLegend
What it isA sacred-feeling story about gods and the beginning of thingsA story about a great human hero, often said to be based on a real person or place, exaggerated over time
Main charactersGods, goddesses, spirits, monsters and the mortals they chooseA heroic person (a warrior, a king, a clever girl) who does extraordinary things
PurposeTo explain nature, traditions or human behaviourTo celebrate courage, loyalty, cleverness — and to inspire
SettingBefore or at the dawn of history: the sky, the underworld, the first seaA real-sounding place long ago: a city, a fortress, a mountain pass
Typical ending“And that is why… to this very day.”“And his name is still spoken in the city…”

Purpose: to entertain, to amaze and to explain. Audience: in the exam, the examiner — who wants to see that you can create a larger-than-life world and still control a clear plot. Point of view: third person, past tense, in a grand storyteller’s voice.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Any story you write goes in Paper 2 (Fiction), Section B Writing — 25 marks. The paper lasts 1 hour 10 minutes. About 35 minutes is suggested for the writing, and planning space is given.
  • Myths and legends have not been set as a named genre in the recent Year 8 / Checkpoint papers we have reviewed. The task gives a theme linked to the extract, usually with bullet prompts.
  • Some themes suit a myth very well: a journey or quest, a great danger, courage, a sacrifice, a gift, saving others, a challenge no one else could face. If the extract is itself a myth or an old tale, a myth is an excellent choice.
  • Never let the gods and monsters take over: the examiner marks the same five categories as for any story, so you still need a clear plot, controlled paragraphs and accurate punctuation.

Themes that suit myths and legends: courage, sacrifice, pride punished by the gods, wisdom beating strength, a journey into the unknown, loyalty, a gift that changes the world, a promise to a god, the origin of something in nature.

Myth / legend or fable / fairy tale?

Gods, a heroic quest, monsters or an explanation of nature → myth or legend (this pack). A short story with talking animals or a simple test and a stated moral about behaviour → the Fables and Fairy Tales pack.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in a myth or legend
Creation of texts5Every bullet prompt is answered. The mythical world feels ancient and believable on its own terms. The hero has a clear quest and wins through wisdom or courage. The explanation or truth at the end grows from the events.
Vocabulary and language3Grand, genre-specific vocabulary (divine, mortal, prophecy, formidable, vanquish) and powerful, exaggerated imagery that makes events larger than life.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7Five clear stages: mystical setting and hero → the call → the quest and trials → climax → resolution and explanation. Paragraph lengths are varied for pace. The ending links back to the opening.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7Long, flowing sentences for the grand storytelling voice and short ones for shock. Speech punctuated correctly. Colon, semicolon, dashes and ellipsis used accurately.
Word structure (spelling)3Ambitious words spelt correctly (prophecy, immortal, sacrifice, vengeance, mysterious). Names of gods and places with capital letters.
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. About 21-60 words can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • Wrong text type (for example, an information text about Greek gods instead of a story) caps Creation at 1 and Structure too.
  • A long list of gods and monsters with no plot is not a myth. Build the story around ONE god, ONE hero and ONE monster.

2. Features of myths and legends

FeatureWhat it meansExample sentence

Supernatural beingsGods, goddesses, spirits and magical messengersSelene, goddess of the moon, wept tears of light.
A chosen hero or heroineOften humble, young or unexpected — marked by fateYet it was Dion, the son of a net-mender, who was chosen.
A call to adventureA disaster, curse, prophecy or divine command starts the quest“Let the bravest of them sail east,” Zeus thundered.

Use real gods carefully — or invent your own

Borrowing ONE real god (Zeus, Athena, Poseidon) instantly signals the genre, but use them correctly (see the quick reference below). You can also invent gods, monsters and places — Nyxar and the island of Thalassa in the sample are invented. What matters is that your world has clear rules (for example: no god can harm what was born before the gods).

3. How to structure a myth or legend

Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (about 5 minutes):

  1. G — Genre: myth (explains nature, gods) or legend (great human hero). Write your “And that is why…” line FIRST if it is a myth.
  2. C — Characters: one hero (humble, with one strength), one god or helper, one monster or danger.
  3. S — Setting: a mystical past place: an island, a desert kingdom, a mountain, the edge of the world.
  4. P — Point of view: third person, past tense, grand voice.
  5. P — Plot: five stages, with three short trials in the quest.
StageWhat to writeLength
1. IntroductionThe mystical past setting and what the world was like before the problem. Hint at the hero (blessed, humble, unexpected).1 paragraph
2. Build-up: the callA disaster, curse or divine command. A god may announce the challenge. Show the suffering of the people with exaggerated imagery.2 paragraphs
3. Rising action: the questThe hero is chosen and sets out, with a helper or a piece of advice. Three quick trials (First… Next… Finally…).2 paragraphs
4. ClimaxThe confrontation. The hero must use wisdom, courage or the helper’s advice, not only strength. Short sentences and one-line paragraphs.2-3 short paragraphs
5. Resolution and explanationOrder is restored and the hero honoured. End with what lasts: a natural event, a tradition or a truth — “And that is why… to this very day.”1-2 paragraphs
MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph. Write the explanation or truth first, then plan the five stages and the three trials.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). Keep the trials short (one or two sentences each) so you have time for a strong climax.
30–35Proofread: capital letters for gods and places, speech punctuation, tense, spelling of ambitious words.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Openings

  • Long ago, when the world was still young and the skies were ruled by [god]…
  • In the time before mortals walked the earth freely, [setting detail].
  • There once lived a [hero / heroine] named [name], whose destiny was shaped by the gods themselves.
  • In those days, [what the world was like] — until [the problem].

Describing the setting

  • Beyond the [mountains / river of fire], the halls of the gods stood wrapped in cloud.
  • The land lay scorched and silent, punished for a crime no one would name.
  • In that age the forests were older than memory, and the rivers still had names of their own.

Introducing hero, god and monster

  • [Name], born of [a humble family / a river god], carried the mark of [god] upon [his/her] shoulder.
  • The gods had gifted [character] with [strength / cunning / foresight] and cursed [him/her] with [flaw].
  • [Monster] was [larger / older / darker] than [comparison], and [frightening detail].

The call and the quest

  • One day, a great disaster struck the land, sent by [god / monster] to punish [reason].
  • It was then that the hero received a prophecy from [god], commanding [him/her] to [task].
  • Days stretched into weeks as [hero] crossed [obstacle 1] and [obstacle 2].
  • First [trial 1]. Next [trial 2]. Finally [trial 3].

Climax

  • With courage burning in [his/her] heart, [hero] [action].
  • The skies roared, lightning split the heavens, and [event] with a fury that shook both earth and sky.
  • At that moment, [hero] realised that the fate of [people] rested entirely on [his/her] shoulders.

Resolution and explanation

  • From that day forward, the people of [land] never forgot [hero].
  • The gods, pleased with such bravery, rewarded [hero] with [gift].
  • And that is why, to this very day, [natural event / tradition].
Word / phraseMeaning / use
divine (adj.)coming from or relating to a god — a divine gift from the sea god
mortal (adj./n.)human, certain to die one day — No mortal had climbed the mountain and returned.
immortal (adj.)living for ever — the immortal gods of the sky
prophecy (n.)a prediction of what is destined to happen — The prophecy named a child of the sea.
omen (n.)a sign that warns of a future event — The black birds were an omen of disaster.
wrathful (adj.)filled with fierce anger — the wrathful god of storms
valiant (adj.)very brave — The valiant girl stood alone at the gate.
formidable (adj.)inspiring fear through great strength — a formidable serpent
vanquish (v.)to defeat completely — Only wisdom could vanquish the beast.
vengeance (n.)punishment in return for a wrong — The god swore vengeance on the proud king.
sacred (adj.)holy, deserving deep respect — No one entered the sacred grove.
summon (v.)to call someone or something to appear — She summoned the wind with three words.
banish (v.)to send away as a punishment — The gods banished him to the edge of the world.
fateful (adj.)having important, often disastrous results — On that fateful night, the moon vanished.
reverence (n.)deep respect mixed with awe — The villagers spoke of the mountain with reverence.

5. Strong sample answer

Myth — Why the Moon Grows Thin566 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: In the extract you have read, a young person is given a task that seems impossible. Write a story in which a young person must save their people from a great danger. In your story you should: describe the place where the people live and the danger they face, explain how the young person sets out to defeat it, and show how the story ends and what changes for ever.

Long ago, when the mountains were still warm from the hands that had shaped them and the sea had not yet learned its name, the moon shone full and round on every night of the year. In those days the fishermen of the island of Thalassa never feared the dark; they rowed out under its silver light and came home with nets that sagged with fish.

Then Nyxar woke.

Nyxar was a serpent of shadow, longer than any river and older than the stars. For a thousand years it had slept, coiled at the bottom of the eastern sea. Now it rose, hungry, and with one stretch of its endless body it reached the sky and began to swallow the moon.

Night after night, the moon grew thinner. The tides became confused, boats were smashed on rocks that nobody could see, and the children of Thalassa were afraid to close their eyes. Selene, goddess of the moon, wept tears of light that fell into the sea and vanished. Even Zeus could not help her, for Nyxar had been born before the gods, and no god could harm it.

“Only a mortal can face what came before us,” Zeus thundered from the clouds. “Let the bravest of them sail east.”

The warriors of Thalassa sharpened their spears and polished their shields. Yet it was Dion, the son of a net-mender, who was chosen. He was fourteen, slight and quick-eyed, and he could read the moods of the water better than any old sailor. On the night he set out, a white owl — the messenger of wise Athena — landed on his shoulder and whispered, “Strength will not win this. Stillness will.”

For seven nights, Dion rowed east. First he passed the Weeping Rocks, where a hundred voices begged him to turn back. Next he crossed the Boiling Strait, where the sea hissed like a thousand kettles. Finally he outran a storm that came screaming out of the north. Each time, he remembered the owl’s words, and each time he did not panic.

At last, he reached the place where the sea meets the sky. Above him, Nyxar’s coils blotted out the stars, and its jaws were closing around the last thin sliver of the moon.

Dion did not raise his spear. Instead, he laid down his oars and waited… and waited, until the wind dropped and the sea became as still as polished bronze. There, on the black water, shone a second moon: its reflection, perfect and round.

The serpent saw it.

With a roar that shook both earth and heaven, Nyxar let go of the sky and plunged into the sea, snapping at the moon on the water. Its jaws closed on nothing but waves. It dived deeper and deeper, chasing a moon it could never catch.

Slowly, night by night, the real moon healed.

Zeus rewarded Dion with a boat that could never sink, and the people of Thalassa sang his name for a hundred generations. But Nyxar was not destroyed. Every month it still creeps up from the deep and takes one bite of the moon after another — until it glimpses the reflection on the sea and dives after it once more.

And that is why, to this very day, the moon grows thin and then grows full again, and why the fishermen of the islands still pray for calm water on the darkest nights.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — all three bullets answered: the place and the danger (paragraphs 1-4). How Dion sets out and faces the trials (5-8). How it ends and what changes for ever (9-14). The myth explains a real natural event (the phases of the moon), and the world has a clear rule that makes the story work: no god can harm a creature born before the gods, so a mortal must go.
  • Creation — genre control: mystical past, real gods used correctly (Zeus, Selene, Athena’s owl), an invented monster (Nyxar) and island (Thalassa), a humble chosen hero, a call from a god, a quest with three trials, and the traditional closing “And that is why, to this very day…”.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — grand, exaggerated imagery: “longer than any river and older than the stars”, “tears of light”, “a roar that shook both earth and heaven”, “the sea hissed like a thousand kettles”, “as still as polished bronze”. Personification: the sea “had not yet learned its name”. The storm came “screaming out of the north”. Precise verbs: thundered, blotted out, plunged, snapping, creeps.
  • Structure 7/7 — five clear stages: introduction (1) → the call: Nyxar and the suffering (2-5) → the quest with three trials, signposted by First / Next / Finally (6-8) → climax (9-11) → resolution and explanation (12-14). One-line paragraphs (“Then Nyxar woke.”, “The serpent saw it.”, “Slowly, night by night, the real moon healed.”) control the pace. The ending links back to the opening: the full moon of paragraph 1 becomes the changing moon of today.
  • Structure — the owl’s advice (“Strength will not win this. Stillness will.”) is set up in paragraph 6 and pays off in the climax: wisdom beats strength, the key feature of a strong myth.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — long, flowing complex sentences for the storytelling voice (“Long ago, when the mountains were still warm… the moon shone full and round…”) contrast with very short ones for shock. Punctuation as taught: a semicolon joining two related sentences (“never feared the dark; they rowed out under its silver light”). A pair of dashes for extra information (“— the messenger of wise Athena —”). A single dash for emphasis (“— until it glimpses the reflection”). A colon for explanation (“a second moon: its reflection, perfect and round”). An ellipsis for a suspenseful pause (“waited… and waited”). Speech punctuated correctly.
  • Spelling 3/3 — ambitious words correct: mountains, shadow, serpent, goddess, messenger, generations, reflection, vanished, destroyed. Capital letters for all gods and places.

6. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
Naming many gods and monstersNo real plot. Reads like a listOne god, one hero, one monster — developed properly.
Getting real gods wrong (Poseidon as god of fire)Weakens Creation. The examiner noticesCheck the quick reference, or invent your own god.
A long battle scene with blood and weaponsAction without tension. The climax feels emptyLet the hero win by wisdom, a trick or a sacrifice.
The god solves the problemThe hero does nothing. Weak climaxThe god gives advice. The HERO acts.
No explanation or lasting truth at the endThe story misses the purpose of a mythFinish with “And that is why, to this very day…” or what the people never forgot.
Modern language (“OK”, “awesome”, “he was like…”)Breaks the grand storytelling voiceKeep the language solemn and timeless.
Semicolons in a list of trialsGrammar & punctuationSeparate sentences with First / Next / Finally, or commas. A semicolon only joins two complete, related sentences.
Checklist before you hand in
  • My story answers the theme and EVERY bullet prompt.
  • I opened in a distant, mystical past with a grand storyteller’s voice.
  • I have one hero, one god or helper, and one monster or danger.
  • There is a call to adventure and a quest with (about) three trials.
  • My hero wins through wisdom or courage, not just strength or a god’s help.
  • I used exaggerated imagery and at least one strong simile or personification.
  • My ending explains something in nature, a tradition or a lasting truth.
  • I used a colon, a semicolon and a dash correctly. Gods and places have capital letters.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: In the extract you have read, a character gives up something precious. Write a story about a sacrifice that helps other people. You should: introduce the character and their world, describe the sacrifice and why it is made, and show what changes as a result. Plan it as a myth that explains something in nature.

    Show answer
    Explanation first: why the desert has oases. G: myth. C: Amira, a shepherd girl with the gift of finding water. The Sun-King (a proud god who burns the land). A desert fox as guide. S: a golden desert kingdom long ago. P: third person, past. Plot — 1 introduction: once the desert was green. 2 the call: the Sun-King, angry that people forgot to honour him, burns the land. The wells die. 3 quest: Amira follows the fox to the Sun-King’s palace. Trials: the sand that swallows, the mirage city, the wind of knives. 4 climax: the Sun-King demands her gift in return for mercy. She gives it — and her tears of sacrifice fall into the sand. 5 resolution: wherever a tear fell, a spring rose. “And that is why, to this very day, green islands of water lie hidden in the desert.”

  2. Practice task 2: Write a legend (a great human hero, no gods needed) about a person whose courage saved a city. List the five stages in note form.

    Show answer
    1 Introduction: a walled city by the sea, long ago. Yara, the lighthouse keeper’s daughter. 2 The call: an enemy fleet will attack in a storm. The lighthouse fire has gone out and the men are on the walls. 3 Quest: she climbs the broken tower in the storm — the locked door, the missing steps, the wind trying to throw her off. 4 Climax: she lights the fire with her own cloak. Seeing the beacon blaze, the enemy fleet knows the city is awake and turns back into the storm. 5 Resolution: the city is saved. Every year a fire is lit on the tower in her name, and the tower is still called Yara’s Light.

  3. Make this sentence larger than life using exaggeration and imagery: “The monster was big and scary.”

    Show answer
    Example: The monster was taller than the city walls, and when it breathed, the clouds above the mountain turned black and the river ran backwards in fear. (Exaggeration + personification of the river.)

  4. Why is “Strength will not win this. Stillness will.” a good line to give the hero before the quest?

    Show answer
    It is short and memorable, it creates a question in the reader’s mind (what does “stillness” mean?), and it sets up the climax, where the hero wins by waiting for the sea to become still. This is called foreshadowing, and it improves both Structure and Creation marks.

  5. Correct the punctuation: “He faced three trials; the rocks, the strait and the storm.”

    Show answer
    He faced three trials: the rocks, the strait and the storm. — A colon introduces a list. A semicolon is never used before a list. It only joins two complete, related sentences.
