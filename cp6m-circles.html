Circles: Parts and Constructing Circles

Learn the centre, radius, diameter and circumference of a circle, and how to construct a circle of any radius or diameter with compasses.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Know and name the parts of a circle: centre, radius, diameter and circumference. [6Gg.02]
  • Use a pair of compasses to construct (draw accurately) a circle with a given radius or a given diameter. [6Gg.11]

A circle is the set of all points that are the same distance from one fixed point, the centre. That one idea explains everything on this page: why every radius is the same length, why the diameter is twice the radius, and how a pair of compasses draws a perfect circle.

1. Key words

Word / phraseMeaning / use
circleدائرة — a round 2D shape; every point on it is the same distance from the centre
centreالمركز — the point in the exact middle of the circle
radius (plural radii)نصف القطر — a straight line from the centre to the circumference; also its length
diameterالقطر — a straight line from one side of the circle to the other, passing through the centre
circumferenceالمحيط — the curved line all the way round the circle; also its length (the perimeter of the circle)
pair of compassesالبرجل — the instrument used to draw circles
constructيرسم بدقة — draw accurately with a ruler and compasses
chordوتر — a straight line joining two points on the circumference (a diameter is the longest chord)

2. The parts of a circle

PartWhat it isHow to recognise it
centrethe middle pointoften a dot, labelled O or C
radiuscentre → circumferencestarts at the centre, stops at the edge
diameteredge → centre → edgegoes right across and passes through the centre
circumferencethe distance aroundthe curved outline itself, not a straight line

Two facts that follow from the definition:

  • All the radii of one circle are the same length, and all its diameters are the same length.
  • A diameter is made of two radii in a straight line, so diameter = 2 × radius and radius = diameter ÷ 2.

A straight line that joins two points on the circle but does NOT pass through the centre is a chord, not a diameter. It is always shorter than the diameter. A line through the centre that does not reach the circumference at both ends is not a diameter either.

افتكر
  • نصف القطر (radius): من المركز للمحيط. القطر (diameter): من المحيط للمحيط ويعدّي بالمركز.
  • القطر = 2 × نصف القطر، ونصف القطر = القطر ÷ 2.
  • المحيط (circumference): الخط المنحني اللي حوالين الدائرة كلها، مش خط مستقيم.

3. Constructing a circle with a pair of compasses

To construct a circle with a given radius (for example 3 cm):

  1. Check the pencil in the compasses is sharp and its tip is level with the point (needle).
  2. Put the point exactly on the 0 mark of your ruler and open the compasses until the pencil tip is exactly on 3 cm.
  3. Mark the centre with a small dot (or use the centre given in the question). Put the point on it and press gently so it does not slip.
  4. Hold the compasses by the top handle only and turn them all the way round in one smooth movement. Do not change the width while you turn.
  5. Check: measure from the centre to the circle in two or three places — each should be 3 cm.

To construct a circle with a given diameter (for example 7 cm): first halve the diameter to find the radius: 7 ÷ 2 = 3.5 cm. Then set the compasses to 3.5 cm and follow the same steps. Check by drawing a straight line through the centre from one side to the other — it should measure 7 cm.

Drawing circle patterns. Many questions ask you to copy a pattern of circles, for example two circles of radius 3 cm whose centres are 3 cm apart, so each circle passes through the other's centre. Draw a line, mark the two centres with the ruler, then draw each circle with the compasses set to the radius.

Accuracy tips for constructions
  • Use a sharp HB pencil — thick lines make your radius inaccurate.
  • Never rub out the centre point; examiners look for it.
  • If the question says "diameter", halve it before setting your compasses.
  • Keep construction marks; they show your method.

4. Worked examples

Worked example 1 (1 mark) A circle has a radius of 8.5 cm. What is its diameter?

  1. diameter = 2 × radius
  2. 2 × 8.5 = 17
  3. Answer: 17 cm.

Common mistake: halving instead of doubling (4.25 cm). Ask yourself: is the diameter longer or shorter than the radius? Longer — so multiply.

Worked example 2 (1 mark) The diameter of a coin is 2.6 cm. What is its radius?

  1. radius = diameter ÷ 2
  2. 2.6 ÷ 2 = 1.3
  3. Answer: 1.3 cm.

Worked example 3 (1 mark) Two identical circles fit exactly inside a rectangle 20 cm long and 10 cm wide. Each circle touches the other circle and the sides of the rectangle. Find the radius of one circle.

  1. Across the length there are 2 diameters: 20 ÷ 2 = 10 cm, so each diameter is 10 cm.
  2. Check with the width: one diameter fits the 10 cm width. ✓
  3. radius = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 cm.
  4. Answer: 5 cm.

Common mistake: dividing 20 by 4 straight away without thinking, or giving the diameter (10 cm) when the question asks for the radius. Underline the word radius or diameter in the question.

Worked example 4 (2 marks) Construct a circle with diameter 9 cm. Draw and label one radius.

  1. Radius = 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 cm.
  2. Set the compasses to 4.5 cm on a ruler, mark a centre, and draw the circle.
  3. Draw a straight line from the centre to the circle and label it "radius".
  4. Marking: 1 mark for a circle with diameter 9 cm (drawn accurately), 1 mark for a correctly drawn and labelled radius.

Worked example 5 (1 mark) Circle P has a radius of 4 cm. Circle Q has a diameter that is 3 times the diameter of circle P. What is the radius of circle Q?

  1. Diameter of P = 2 × 4 = 8 cm.
  2. Diameter of Q = 3 × 8 = 24 cm.
  3. Radius of Q = 24 ÷ 2 = 12 cm.
  4. Answer: 12 cm. (Shortcut: if the diameter is 3 times bigger, the radius is also 3 times bigger: 3 × 4 = 12 cm.)

Worked example 6 (2 marks) Two circles have the same centre. The large circle has a diameter of 18 cm. The small circle has a radius of 5 cm. How wide is the ring between the two circles (measured along a radius)?

  1. Change everything to radii: large radius = 18 ÷ 2 = 9 cm.
  2. Small radius = 5 cm.
  3. Width of the ring = 9 − 5 = 4 cm.
  4. Answer: 4 cm. Marks: 1 mark for finding the radius 9 cm (or a method 18 ÷ 2 − 5), 1 mark for 4 cm.

Common mistake: working out 18 − 5 = 13 cm, which mixes a diameter with a radius. Always compare like with like — radius with radius, or diameter with diameter.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up radius and diameter. Radius → from the centre; diameter → right across through the centre.
  • Setting the compasses to the diameter when the question gives a diameter. Halve it first.
  • Calling any line across a circle a diameter. It must pass through the centre.
  • Thinking the circumference is a straight line or a point. It is the whole curved edge (the distance around).
  • Letting the compasses slip or open wider while turning: hold them by the top only.
  • Forgetting units (cm) or giving the radius when the diameter was asked for.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Label the parts: draw lines from the words centre, radius, diameter, circumference to a diagram, or name a line marked on a circle.
  • Find a radius or diameter from a picture of circles inside a rectangle or square, or touching each other in a row. Count how many diameters fit across.
  • Construct a circle of a given radius or diameter with compasses — sometimes on a given centre or inside a box. Accuracy matters.
  • Complete a sentence: "The diameter is ___ times the length of the radius."
  • "Explain" or "Tick the correct statement" questions on the relationship between radius and diameter.

Practice

Remember
  • Centre = the middle point. Radius = centre to edge. Diameter = edge to edge through the centre. Circumference = the distance all the way round.
  • diameter = 2 × radius; radius = diameter ÷ 2.
  • All radii of a circle are equal; all diameters are equal.
  • Compasses are always set to the RADIUS — halve a diameter first.
  • For circles in a row or inside a square, count how many diameters fit across.
