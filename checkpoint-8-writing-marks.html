How Your Writing Is Marked

The five writing categories (25 marks) explained in plain English, what top marks need in each, the rules that cap marks, and the G.A.A.P. and G.C.S.P.P. planning techniques with a 35-minute plan and checking routine.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Section B of each paper is worth 25 marks — half the paper. The examiner does not give one overall impression mark. Instead, your writing is marked against five separate categories, and the marks are added together. When you know exactly what each category rewards, you can write to collect every mark. This page explains the categories, the special rules that limit marks, and the two planning techniques used throughout this course: G.A.A.P. for Paper 1 and G.C.S.P.P. for Paper 2.

The five categories at a glance

The same five categories are used for Paper 1 (non-fiction) and Paper 2 (fiction), and for both the Stage 8 Progression Test and Checkpoint 0861.

Category (official name)CodeMarksIn one question
Creation of textsWc5Is your content relevant, developed and right for the text type, purpose and reader?
Vocabulary and languageWv3Did you choose words carefully and for effect?
Structure of texts (Text structure)Ws7Is the whole piece well organised, paragraphed and linked?
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)Wg7Are your sentences accurate, varied and punctuated well — and used for effect?
Word structure (spelling)Ww3Can you spell accurately, including ambitious words?
Total25

Look at the numbers. Structure (7) and Grammar and punctuation (7) together are worth 14 marks — more than half. A well-organised piece with varied, accurate sentences will always score well, even if the ideas are simple. Creation of texts (5) is almost as valuable as Vocabulary and Spelling put together (3 + 3 = 6), so content and text type matter a lot too.

How the examiner uses the bands

Each category has bands (for example 1, 2–3, 4–5, 6–7). The examiner starts at the lowest band and moves up while your writing meets the description: this is called best fit. If you meet only some of a band's description, you get the lower mark in that band. If you do not meet even the lowest description, that category scores 0. So the aim is to show every feature of the top band clearly, not just one or two.

Special rules that limit your mark

RuleWhat happensHow to stay safe
Very short answer20 words or fewer = 0 marks in total. 21–60 words = maximum 7 marks in total.Sixty words is only about eight handwritten lines. Aim for 350+ words.
Wrong text typeIf you write the wrong kind of text (e.g. a story instead of an article), Creation of texts is capped at 1, and Structure is also capped (at about 3, or lower if the content is also off-task).Underline the text type in the task. Use its layout features from the first line.
PlanningThe planning space is not marked.Plan in notes, not sentences. Spend 4–5 minutes, no more.

1. Creation of texts (Wc) — 5 marks

This category is about content and purpose: does your writing answer the actual task, does it read like the text type you were asked for, and does it keep the reader in mind from start to finish?

BandWhat it looks like (paraphrased)
1Some content linked to the topic with a little development, or a few recognisable features of the text type. Wrong text type = this band at most.
2Content is partly relevant. Features of the text type appear here and there, not consistently. A simple viewpoint that mostly fits the purpose.
3Content is mostly relevant with a range of developed ideas. Features of the text type are used throughout. The viewpoint is consistent, and the writer's own style starts to show.
4–5Content is fully relevant and well developed, with specific details. A wide range of text-type features is used successfully. The writer keeps a clear relationship with the reader all the way through and sustains interest.
How to get 5/5 in Creation of texts
  • Cover every point or bullet in the task — and develop each one with details, examples and explanation, not one line each.
  • Use the features of the text type throughout, not only in the opening (e.g. a persuasive article keeps using direct address, rhetorical questions and emotive language to the end).
  • Keep one clear viewpoint or narrative voice. Do not change your opinion or your narrator half-way.
  • Talk to your reader: in non-fiction, remind them why it matters to them. In fiction, keep them curious about what happens next.
  • Be specific. 'A small café by the harbour that smelt of cardamom' is worth more than 'a nice place'.

2. Vocabulary and language (Wv) — 3 marks

This category rewards word choice. Correct words are not enough for the top band: your words must be chosen for an effect — to persuade, to describe, to create a mood or to sound expert.

BandWhat it looks like (paraphrased)
1Vocabulary is correct and on topic, but plain.
2Vocabulary suits the text type, with some descriptive, emotive or topic-specific words. The tone fits the purpose.
3Ambitious, specialised vocabulary is used well across the whole piece, clearly chosen for effect and to create the right mood or tone.
Plain (band 1)Chosen for effect (band 3)
The market was busy and loud.The market heaved with shoppers, and the air rang with the cries of traders.
Plastic is bad for the sea.Discarded plastic is quietly choking our coastline.
He was scared.His stomach tightened, and a cold dread crept up his spine.
The film was good.The film is gripping from its first scene to its final, unexpected twist.
How to get 3/3 in Vocabulary
  • Learn 10–15 topic words for each genre pack in this course and use several in every piece.
  • Replace weak words: good, bad, nice, big, very, a lot, thing, stuff, said, went.
  • Choose verbs with power (crept, seized, soared) — strong verbs often do more than adjectives.
  • Match the register: formal words for a report or formal letter, warm and lively words for a blog or story.
  • Keep it up to the end. Many students use their best words in the first paragraph and then stop.

3. Structure of texts (Ws) — 7 marks

This category looks at the whole piece: the opening, the order of ideas, the paragraphs, the links between them, and the ending.

BandWhat it looks like (paraphrased)
1Separate points can be seen. Very basic organisation.
2–3Related ideas are grouped. Some logical order, with simple connectives (and, also, because, then). There may be an opening and/or ending.
4–5Ideas develop logically through the whole piece. Paragraphs are used properly and linked so the writing flows.
6–7Well-organised paragraphs, clearly linked within and between paragraphs with a wide range of connectives, building to an effective whole with a strong opening and ending.
How to get 7/7 in Structure
  • Plan the order before you write: opening → 2–4 developed body paragraphs → ending.
  • One main idea per paragraph. Start each with a topic sentence (non-fiction) or a change of time, place or action (fiction).
  • Link paragraphs: use connectives (Furthermore, However, As a result, Later that evening, By the time…) or echo a word from the previous paragraph.
  • Make the opening hook the reader and the ending complete the piece (a call to action, a recommendation, a reflection, a resolution or a twist).
  • In stories, vary the pace: a one-line paragraph at the moment of tension can be very effective.

4. Grammar and punctuation (Wg) — 7 marks

This category looks at your sentences: their accuracy, their variety and whether you use them for effect. It also checks punctuation — accurate basics first, then a wider range.

BandWhat it looks like (paraphrased)
1Simple sentences mostly correct. Full stops and capital letters mostly accurate.
2–3Simple and compound sentences, with some attempts at complex ones. Verb tenses usually consistent. Basic punctuation mostly correct.
4–5Grammar accurate almost throughout. A confident mix of simple, compound and complex sentences. Expanded noun phrases. Correct verb forms and modal verbs. Commas used correctly, including to mark clauses.
6–7Sentence types and lengths are chosen deliberately for effect. Clauses are reordered for emphasis. A wide range of punctuation (e.g. colons, semicolons, dashes, brackets) is used accurately and with purpose.
How to get 7/7 in Grammar and punctuation
  • Use all four sentence types: simple, compound, complex and compound-complex — and place a short sentence where you want impact.
  • Open some sentences with a subordinate clause ('Although the rain had stopped, …'), a fronted adverbial ('Without warning, …') or an -ing/-ed phrase ('Exhausted from the climb, …').
  • Use at least one each of: a colon (to introduce a list or explanation), a semicolon (to join two related main clauses), a pair of dashes or brackets (for extra information), and accurate apostrophes.
  • Keep your tense consistent: past for stories and recounts, present for reviews and most articles.
  • Use modal verbs in non-fiction for force or politeness: must, should, could, might.

Examples of sentence-level effects you can copy:

  • Short sentence for impact: 'For three weeks, we had trained every evening on the dusty pitch behind the school. Then the storm came.'
  • Reordered clause for emphasis: 'Because every bottle we throw away lasts for centuries, we must act today.'
  • Colon for explanation: 'The answer was simple: we needed more volunteers.'
  • Semicolon joining two related sentences: 'The old lighthouse stood empty; nobody had climbed its stairs for years.'
  • Pair of dashes for extra information: 'My grandfather — who had never left his village before — gripped the railing of the ferry.'

5. Word structure (spelling) (Ww) — 3 marks

This category is about spelling, from everyday words to ambitious ones.

BandWhat it looks like (paraphrased)
1Common and high-frequency words, regular plurals and common prefixes and suffixes are mostly correct.
2Words with double consonants, silent letters, common exception words and a wider range of prefixes and suffixes are mostly correct.
3Ambitious and topic-specific words, commonly misspelt words, tricky endings and easily confused homophones are spelt correctly.
Spelling areaLearn these
Commonly misspeltnecessary, definitely, separate, environment, recommend, beginning, believe, government, embarrassed, accommodation
Double consonantsstopped, travelling (British), occurred, beginning, committee, address
Silent lettersknowledge, island, doubt, rhythm, muscle, wrist
Tricky endings-tion/-cian (information, musician) · -able/-ible (comfortable, responsible) · -ance/-ence (appearance, independence)
Homophonestheir/there/they're · your/you're · aloud/allowed · whether/weather · affect/effect · desert/dessert · practise (verb)/practice (noun)
How to get 3/3 in Spelling
  • Use British spelling consistently: colour, favourite, organise, centre, travelled.
  • Learn the spelling of every vocabulary word you plan to use — an ambitious word spelt wrongly loses its value.
  • Save three minutes at the end to check homophones and the words you know you often get wrong.

Paper 1: the G.A.A.P. technique

Before you write a single sentence of a non-fiction answer, spend the first few minutes breaking the task down with G.A.A.P. Each letter is a decision you must make, and each one is linked to marks.

LetterAsk yourselfWhat it decidesMarks it protects
G — GenreWhat layout (text type) is it: article, review, letter, email, report, speech, diary? And what exact topic?Headline, greeting, sub-headings, sign-off. Staying precisely on the topic (not 'the environment' in general if the task says 'litter in the park').Creation of texts and Structure (wrong text type caps both)
A — AudienceWho will read it, and where? A school magazine for students? A letter to a head teacher? The local council?Register: formal or informal, how you address the reader, the vocabulary you choose.Vocabulary and language, Creation of texts
A — Area of contentWhat points does the task tell me to include?Underline every point. Use all of them and develop each in its own paragraph.Creation of texts (relevance and development)
P — PurposeIs it to inform, explain, describe, recount, argue, persuade or review?The toolkit: e.g. persuade → rhetorical questions, direct address, emotive words, call to action. Inform → clear facts, neutral tone, sub-headings.Creation of texts, Vocabulary

Worked example. Task: *Your school website is running a feature called 'Our City, Our Future'. Write an article encouraging students to use public transport, cycle or walk to school. You should explain the problems caused by so many car journeys, what students can do, and how the school could help.*

G.A.A.P.My decisions
Genre — layoutArticle: headline, strong opening, 3–4 paragraphs, possibly sub-headings, a closing call to action.
Genre — topicGetting to school without a car — not pollution in general.
AudienceStudents at my school reading the website → semi-formal, lively, 'we' and 'you', no slang.
Area of content(1) problems caused by car journeys (traffic at the gate, pollution, lack of exercise). (2) what students can do (walk in groups, cycle, school bus). (3) how the school could help (bike racks, safe crossing, a 'Walk on Wednesday' challenge).
PurposePersuade + inform → rhetorical questions, statistics-style facts, emotive language, modal verbs (must, could), call to action.
G.A.A.P. in the exam

Write G, A, A, P down the side of the planning space and fill each line in a few words. This takes 2–3 minutes and stops the three biggest mistakes: wrong text type, wrong tone and a missing bullet point.

Paper 2: the G.C.S.P.P. technique

For a story, make every big decision before you write, so you do not change direction half-way. Use G.C.S.P.P.

LetterAsk yourselfWhat to decide
G — GenreWhat kind of story suits the task: realistic, adventure, mystery, horror, fantasy, science fiction?Genre sets the mood, vocabulary and atmosphere (e.g. mystery → clues and questions, adventure → risk, movement and a goal).
C — CharactersWho is in the story, what is each one's role, and how are they related?Keep it to 2–4 characters. Know your main character's personality and what he or she wants.
S — SettingWhere and when does it happen? Is the setting given in the task?If the setting is given, use it and stay consistent. If not, choose one that fits the genre and helps the mood.
P — Point of viewFirst person ('I') or third person ('he/she')?First person feels personal; third person lets you describe more. If you continue a given story, keep its point of view exactly.
P — PlotWhich part am I writing: a complete story, an opening, an ending or a continuation?For a complete story: opening → build-up → problem/climax → resolution. Follow every bullet prompt in the task.

Worked example. Task: *Write a story about someone who has to finish an important task before time runs out. You should describe the setting, the main character and what happens.*

G.C.S.P.P.My decisions
GenreRealistic fiction with suspense.
CharactersYassin (14, careful, worried about letting people down) · his younger sister Salma · Mr Hany, the bakery owner.
SettingA small family bakery in Alexandria at dawn on the morning of a wedding; the oven has broken.
Point of viewThird person, past tense, close to Yassin's thoughts.
PlotComplete story: (1) dawn, smell of bread, order for 200 pastries. (2) the oven dies. (3) race across the city to borrow a neighbour's oven, with the clock ticking. (4) the last tray arrives as the wedding begins. (5) Yassin realises he is calmer under pressure than he thought.
Continuation tasks

If a task asks you to continue the story from the extract, re-read the extract first and lock in the characters, setting, point of view and tone. Your continuation must feel like the same story — same names, same personalities, same narrator. Switching from 'I' to 'he' is a serious and avoidable error.

Using your 35 minutes

Most Year 8 students can produce 350–450 words of careful writing in 35 minutes with this plan:

TimeStepWhat you do
0–4 minDecode and planUnderline the text type, audience, purpose and every bullet. Fill in G.A.A.P. or G.C.S.P.P. in notes. Write a 4–5 line paragraph plan, and jot 5–6 strong words and one or two punctuation features you will use.
4–8 minOpeningWrite the headline/greeting (non-fiction) or a hooking first line (fiction), then the opening paragraph.
8–28 minBodyOne paragraph per point or story stage, about 5 minutes each. Link every paragraph. Check you are covering every bullet.
28–32 minEndingA clear conclusion: call to action, recommendation, reflection, or a satisfying resolution.
32–35 minCheckRun the checking routine below. Correct neatly with a single line through any mistake.

Draw this quickly in the planning space. It works for both papers.

The 3-minute checking routine

  1. Task: Have I written the right text type and covered every bullet point?
  2. Paragraphs: Is there a clear opening, body and ending, with each paragraph linked?
  3. Sentences: Is there at least one simple, one compound and one complex sentence, and a short sentence for effect?
  4. Punctuation: Full stops and capitals · commas after opening clauses · apostrophes (it's/its) · speech marks in stories · my planned colon, semicolon and dashes.
  5. Tense: Have I stayed in the same tense?
  6. Spelling: Check homophones (their/there, your/you're, whether/weather) and every ambitious word.

خطة الـ35 دقيقة في الكتابة

أول 4 دقائق: اقرأ السؤال وحدد نوع النص والقارئ والهدف وكل النقاط المطلوبة باستخدام G.A.A.P. في الورقة الأولى أو G.C.S.P.P. في القصة، واكتب خطة سريعة في كلمات وليس جملًا. من الدقيقة 4 إلى 28: اكتب المقدمة ثم فقرة لكل نقطة. من 28 إلى 32: الخاتمة. آخر 3 دقائق: راجع الفقرات وعلامات الترقيم والأزمنة والإملاء. الخطة نفسها لا تُصحح، لكنها تحميك من أكبر الأخطاء.

Practice: decode these tasks

For each task, write a G.A.A.P. or G.C.S.P.P. breakdown before you look at the answer.

  1. Paper 1 task: 'Your town's youth centre may close. Write a letter to the local council explaining what the centre offers young people, why it matters, and what could be done to keep it open.' — Break it down with G.A.A.P.

    Show answer
    G (layout): formal letter — addresses, date, 'Dear Sir or Madam,', paragraphs, 'Yours faithfully,' + full name. G (topic): keeping the youth centre open. A (audience): council officials → formal, polite, no contractions. A (area of content): (1) what the centre offers. (2) why it matters to young people. (3) ways to keep it open (volunteers, fundraising, shared use). P (purpose): persuade + explain → reasons with examples, modal verbs (should, could), a polite but firm request at the end.

  2. Paper 1 task: 'Write a review of a place to eat in your area for a website read by families. Describe the place, the food and the service, and say who you would recommend it to.' — Break it down with G.A.A.P.

    Show answer
    G (layout): review — title naming the place, star rating (optional), opening that sets the scene, a paragraph each on place, food, service, and a recommendation. G (topic): one specific local restaurant or café. A (audience): parents and families online → friendly, semi-formal, helpful. A (area of content): description of the place · the food · the service · who it suits. P (purpose): inform + give opinion → descriptive vocabulary, balanced positives and a negative, a clear final verdict.

  3. Paper 2 task: 'Write a story about a character who discovers that a place they know well is hiding a secret.' — Break it down with G.C.S.P.P.

    Show answer
    G: mystery. C: Nour (13, curious, observant) and her grandfather, plus perhaps one minor character (the caretaker). S: grandfather's old flat in a quiet Cairo street, with a locked cupboard behind the bookcase. P (point of view): first person, past tense, to share Nour's curiosity. P (plot): complete story — normal visit → strange detail noticed → search and tension → the secret revealed (old letters showing the grandfather's past) → what Nour now understands about him.

  4. Paper 2 task: 'Continue the story from the extract, describing what happens when the storm reaches the village.' The extract is told in third person about a fisherman named Karim. What two things must you NOT change, and why?

    Show answer
    You must keep the third-person point of view and keep Karim's character (name, personality, relationships) and the village setting exactly as in the extract. A continuation is marked on how naturally it follows the original. Switching to 'I' or changing who Karim is would be a serious consistency error and would weaken Creation of texts and Structure.

  5. A student wrote 55 excellent words before running out of time. What is the highest writing mark possible, and what lesson does this teach?

    Show answer
    The maximum is 7 out of 25, because answers of 21–60 words are capped at 7. Lesson: manage your time — reading must stop at about 35 minutes so that you have enough time to write a full answer.

  6. Which category is worth more: Vocabulary and language plus Word structure (spelling) together, or Creation of texts on its own?

    Show answer
    Vocabulary (3) + Spelling (3) = 6, while Creation of texts = 5, so Vocabulary and Spelling together are worth one mark more. But notice that Creation of texts alone is almost as valuable as both of them, and the two biggest categories are Structure (7) and Grammar and punctuation (7).
