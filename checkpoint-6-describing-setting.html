Checkpoint 6 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Describing a Setting

How to describe a place so the reader can see, hear, smell and feel it — one clear mood, wide-to-detail zoom, the senses, strong verbs and one strong comparison — with building-block sentences, short model paragraphs and a full sample story opening.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is a setting, and why does it matter in Paper 2?

The setting is where and when a story happens: the place, the time of day, the season and the weather. A good setting description does more than tell the reader where we are. It creates an atmosphere (a mood), it prepares the reader for what will happen, and it makes the story feel real.

In Paper 2 (Fiction) you will often need to describe a setting. In a story opening, the setting is usually one of the first things the examiner looks for. In a story about a special place, the place is the heart of the task. In a continuation, you must keep the setting the extract has already described — and you can add new details to it. Setting description mainly earns marks in Creation of texts (engaging the reader) and Vocabulary and language (imaginative word choice).

Remember the balance

Your task is still a story. A setting description that goes on for the whole answer, with no character and no events, will not score well for Structure. Describe the setting in one or two strong paragraphs, then let the story move — and bring the setting back at key moments. In the exam, about 30 minutes is suggested for the whole writing task (Paper 2, Section B, 25 marks). Many strong answers are about 250-350 words.

2. Features of a strong setting description

FeatureWhat it doesExample
One clear moodEvery detail supports one feeling: calm, busy, eerie, joyful, lonely…Eerie: an empty playground, a swing creaking by itself.
Zoom from wide to detailStart with the big picture, then focus on one or two small, telling details.The whole beach → the crowded umbrellas → one red kite tugging at its string.
Three or four sensesNot only sight: sound, smell, touch (and sometimes taste).The air smelled of salt and sunscreen. Sand burned under my feet.
Strong, precise verbsVerbs make the place move and feel alive.Waves smashed against the rocks. Smoke curled from the chimney.
One strong simile or metaphorA comparison helps the reader picture it. One good one beats five weak ones.The fortress spread out like a giant sandcastle.
PersonificationGives the place human actions, which creates mood.The old house groaned in the wind. The shadows crept closer.
OnomatopoeiaLets the reader hear the place.Drip. Drip. The tap would not stop.
Light, weather and time of dayThey change the mood instantly.Golden morning light = hope. A grey, drizzly evening = sadness.
Prepositional phrase startersGuide the reader around the place.Above the door… Beneath the bridge… In the far corner…
The character reacts to the placeShows the setting through someone’s eyes, so the story keeps moving.Mariam could touch both walls at once.
An echo at the endFinish with a small, specific detail instead of explaining your feelings.Only the blue door stayed in her mind.

3. How to describe a setting — six steps

  1. Choose ONE mood before you write. Ask: should this place feel warm, tense, eerie, busy or calm? Then choose only the details that build that mood. A ‘scary’ forest with happy birds singing sends a mixed message.
  2. Zoom from wide to detail. Begin with the whole view (the town from the hill), then move closer (a narrow path), then closer still (salt glittering in a crack, a polished handle).
  3. Use three or four senses. After sight, add one sound, one smell and one thing the character touches or feels (heat, cold, wind, rough walls).
  4. Use one strong comparison. A single fresh simile or metaphor that suits the place. Avoid tired ones like ‘as white as snow’ in a desert story.
  5. Let the verbs do the work. ‘The wind hissed through the empty windows’ is stronger than ‘there was a very strong, loud wind’.
  6. End with an echo of the mood. Close on one small, specific detail (the warm handle, the last streetlight, a kite in the sky) rather than ‘It was a very nice place.’
Avoid the ‘shopping list’ description

‘There was a big tree. There was a small house. There was a blue sky. There was a dog.’ This is a list, not a description. Join details together, use verbs, and show how they make the character feel: ‘A huge fig tree spread its branches over the little house, and in its shade an old dog dozed, too hot to bark.’

4. Building blocks: mix-and-match sentences

These sentence frames help you build a description quickly in the exam. Pick three or four categories, not all of them, and change them to first person (I) or third person (he/she) to match your story.

CategoryFrameFilled in
Orientation (where we are)[Character] stepped into [place], and it hit [him/her] all at once.Karim stepped into the market, and it hit him all at once.
Wide viewEverywhere [I/she] looked, [visual detail] stretched out.Everywhere she looked, golden sand stretched out to the horizon.
Close detailIn the far corner, [small object] [verb].In the far corner, a cracked lantern swayed on its hook.
SoundThe only sound was [sound 1], broken now and then by [sound 2].The only sound was the tick of the clock, broken now and then by a cough upstairs.
SmellThe air smelled of [smell], [description].The air smelled of cumin and fresh bread, warm and welcoming.
Touch / temperatureWhen [small event], [I/he] felt [feeling] against [skin/clothes].When the bus doors opened, I felt the sticky heat against my face.
ComparisonIn the [light], the [object] looked like [comparison].In the moonlight, the minaret looked like a silver pencil.
Closing echoAs [I/she] turned away, [final detail] stayed in [my/her] mind.As I turned away, the empty swing stayed in my mind.

Worked example (third person, busy mood): Karim stepped into the Friday market, and it hit him all at once. Everywhere he looked, stalls overflowed with oranges, mint and shining aubergines. The air smelled of cumin and fresh bread, warm and welcoming. A seller banged a pan and sang out his prices. In the bright sunshine, the piles of spices looked like tiny hills of red, yellow and brown. As Karim pushed deeper into the crowd, the song of the seller followed him.

5. One place, two moods

The same place can feel completely different. The writer’s choices of light, sound, verbs and comparisons create the mood. Study these two descriptions of the same school playground.

Happy mood (morning)Eerie mood (after dark)
LightSunlight splashed across the yard.One orange streetlight flickered over the yard.
SoundShouts and laughter bounced off the walls.The only sound was a swing creaking by itself.
VerbsChildren raced, skipped and tumbled.Leaves scraped across the concrete.
ComparisonThe yard buzzed like a beehive.The climbing frame stood like a skeleton against the sky.
Closing detailA football rolled to a stop at my feet.A forgotten football lay in the shadows, as if waiting for someone.
Pathetic fallacy (weather that matches feelings)

Writers often make the weather match the character’s mood: a storm when a character is angry or afraid, bright sunshine when a problem is solved. You do not need to name the technique in your writing. Just use it: ‘As Laila walked home with her test result, grey clouds pressed down on the rooftops.’

6. How to structure a setting-led story opening

When the task asks for the opening of a story, or for a story about a special place, this plan works well. It mixes setting with character and action, so it never becomes a list.

ParagraphWhat to writeTip
1. HookOne short, surprising sentence about the place or the character.‘Mariam had never seen a town made of mud.’
2. Wide viewThe big picture: landscape, buildings, light, colour. One strong comparison.Use a prepositional starter: ‘From the top of the hill,…’
3. Move in closerThe character walks into the place. Add sound, smell and touch.Show the character reacting: touching, noticing, listening.
4. Change of moodSomething shifts: it gets quieter, darker, stranger — or brighter.Light and sound are the easiest ways to change mood.
5. The detail that mattersZoom in on one object or event that starts the problem.A short, one-line paragraph adds suspense.
6. Hook endingEnd on a question, a surprise or a hint.Do not solve the mystery in an opening.

In a complete story, use the setting at three points: describe it in the opening, change it at the climax (the light goes, the storm arrives, the crowd goes silent), and bring one detail back in the ending (the circular ending). In a continuation, re-read the extract and keep its setting details: if the extract says it is night and raining, your continuation cannot suddenly be sunny.

7. Short model paragraphs to study

Each paragraph builds a different mood. Notice the senses, the verbs and the one comparison in each. Learn the phrases you like.

  • Grandmother’s kitchen (warm, safe): Teta’s kitchen was always the warmest room in the house. Steam rose from a pot of lentil soup, and the smell of onions frying in butter wrapped around me like a blanket. Jars of pickles lined the windowsill, glowing green and gold in the afternoon light. The radio crackled with an old song, and Teta hummed along, slightly out of tune. Nothing bad could happen in that kitchen.
  • The Friday market (busy, noisy): By nine o’clock, the market was already roaring. Sellers shouted over each other, a donkey cart rattled past, and somewhere a radio played at full volume. Pyramids of tomatoes and oranges glowed under faded umbrellas. The air was thick with the smell of mint, fish and frying falafel. People squeezed between the stalls like water finding its way through rocks.
  • The school corridor at night (eerie): The corridor stretched ahead of Adam, longer than it had ever looked in daylight. Moonlight fell through the tall windows in pale stripes. Every footstep echoed, as if someone was walking behind him. A classroom door stood half open. Inside, the chairs were upside down on the desks, their legs pointing at the ceiling like the bones of some strange animal.
  • The Corniche in a winter storm (wild): The sea had gone mad. Grey waves hurled themselves against the wall of the Corniche and burst into white spray that soaked the pavement. The wind screamed between the buildings and tore at my jacket. Fishing boats tossed in the harbour. Only one café was open, its yellow windows shining through the rain like a lighthouse.
  • The desert at night (calm, vast): When the engine stopped, the silence was enormous. Above us, the sky was crowded with more stars than I had ever imagined, so many that they looked like spilled sugar. The sand, so hot at midday, was cool and soft between my toes. Far away, a fox barked once. Then the desert held its breath again.

9. Strong sample — a setting-led story opening

The Blue Door (story opening, special place)271 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Write the opening of a story set in a place that the main character is visiting for the first time. Describe the place so that the reader can picture it, and end in a way that makes the reader want to read on.

Mariam had never seen a town made of mud.

From the top of the hill, the old fortress of Shali spread out beneath her like a giant sandcastle that the sea had forgotten to wash away. Hundreds of crooked walls leaned against each other, the colour of honey in the late afternoon sun. Beyond them, palm trees crowded around the green pools of the oasis, and beyond the palms, the desert rolled on and on until it melted into the sky.

“Ten minutes,” Baba called from the car park below. “Then we go back for dinner.”

Mariam nodded, but she was already walking. The narrow path twisted between the houses, and the walls were so close that she could touch both sides at once. They felt rough and surprisingly cool. Salt glittered in the cracks. Somewhere above her, a dove cooed, and a dry wind hissed through the empty windows.

The further she went, the quieter it became. The voices of the other visitors faded behind her. The sun slipped lower, and the shadows crept across the path like long, dark fingers. A smell of dust and warm stone hung in the air.

Then the path ended at a small wooden door.

It was the only door she had seen in the whole fortress. Every other doorway was an empty hole, but this one was painted bright blue, and its iron handle was polished, as if someone used it every day.

Mariam glanced back. Nobody was watching.

She reached for the handle. It was warm.

From the other side of the door, very softly, someone was humming her grandmother’s favourite song.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: a clear story opening — the setting is described in detail, the character is introduced, and the mystery begins, but nothing is solved. The mood moves from wonder to quiet, then to mystery, which keeps the reader engaged.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: one strong, original simile for the whole place (‘like a giant sandcastle that the sea had forgotten to wash away’) · personification (‘the shadows crept… like long, dark fingers’, the wind ‘hissed’) · precise verbs (twisted, glittered, faded, slipped) · four senses — sight (honey-coloured walls), sound (dove, wind), touch (rough, cool walls · the warm handle) and smell (dust and warm stone).
  • Structure of texts 7/7: follows the setting-led plan — hook sentence → wide view from the hill → moving closer with the senses → change of mood (quieter, darker) → the detail that matters (the blue door) → hook ending. Short paragraphs (‘Then the path ended…’, ‘She reached for the handle. It was warm.’) slow the pace and build suspense.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: varied starters — prepositional (‘From the top of the hill,’ ‘Beyond them,’), adverbial (‘Somewhere above her,’), comparative structure (‘The further she went, the quieter it became.’) · simple, compound and complex sentences · speech punctuated correctly, with the reporting clause placed between two spoken sentences · past tense and third person kept throughout.
  • Spelling 3/3: fortress, crooked, surprisingly, glittered, polished, grandmother’s — all correct, including the apostrophe of possession.
  • Setting through the character: we see the place with Mariam — she can ‘touch both sides at once’, she glances back, she reaches for the handle — so the description never becomes a list.

10. Common mistakes and checklist

MistakeBetter
Only describing what you can seeAdd one sound, one smell and one thing to touch or feel.
Mixed moods (a ‘scary’ cave with cheerful birdsong)Choose one mood and keep every detail on that side.
Too many adjectives: ‘the big, old, dark, scary, creepy house’Two well-chosen adjectives and a strong verb: ‘The dark house sagged against its neighbour.’
Tired comparisons: ‘as cold as ice’, ‘as quiet as a mouse’Make a fresh one that fits your setting: ‘as quiet as an empty mosque at midnight’.
Describing for the whole answer with no storyOne or two setting paragraphs, then move the story on.
Changing the setting of an extract in a continuationKeep the time, place and weather already given.
Ending with ‘It was a lovely place.’End on a small, specific detail that echoes the mood.
Setting checklist
  • I chose one mood before writing.
  • I zoomed from the wide view to a small detail.
  • I used at least three senses.
  • I used one strong simile or metaphor, and some personification.
  • My verbs are precise (crept, glittered, roared, hissed).
  • I used prepositional phrase starters to move the reader around the place.
  • My character reacts to the place, so the story keeps moving.
  • I ended with a detail that echoes the mood, or with a hook.

11. Practice

  1. Why does the author use the simile ‘like a giant sandcastle that the sea had forgotten to wash away’ in paragraph 2 of the sample?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to help the reader picture the shape and colour of the fortress (sandy, lumpy, crumbly towers) · to show that the town is made of mud and looks fragile, as if it could disappear · to suggest that it is very old and has survived by chance · to create a sense of wonder, as Mariam sees it for the first time · to make the description memorable and original.

  2. Why does the author write ‘Then the path ended at a small wooden door.’ as a paragraph on its own?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to make the door stand out as the most important detail · to slow the pace and create a pause · to build suspense, because the reader wonders what is behind the door · to mark the change from description to the start of the mystery.

  3. Why does the author use personification in ‘the shadows crept across the path like long, dark fingers’?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to make the shadows seem alive and threatening · to show that time is passing and night is coming · to change the mood from wonder to unease · to create suspense, as if something is reaching for Mariam.

  4. Turn this ‘shopping list’ into a strong description: ‘There was a park. There were trees. There was a lake. There were ducks.’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: Tall eucalyptus trees circled the little park, their leaves whispering in the breeze. In the middle, the lake shone like a dropped mirror, and a family of ducks paddled across it, quacking crossly at a boy with a bag of bread.

  5. Write two sentences about a hospital waiting room: one with a calm mood and one with a tense mood.

    Show answer
    Calm: Soft music drifted from a speaker, and sunlight warmed the rows of blue chairs. Tense: Under the buzzing white lights, nobody spoke, and every time the doors swung open, every head turned at once.

  6. Add a sound, a smell and a touch detail to: ‘Salma walked onto the beach.’

    Show answer
    Possible answer: Salma walked onto the beach, where gulls screamed above the waves (sound). The air tasted of salt and suncream (smell/taste), and the hot sand stung the soles of her feet (touch).

  7. Practice task: ‘Write the opening of a story set in an old building that has been empty for many years.’ Write a short plan using the setting-led structure.

    Show answer
    Mood: eerie. P1 hook: ‘The cinema on Talaat Harb Street had been closed for thirty years.’ P2 wide view: faded posters, broken sign, pigeons on the roof · simile — the entrance gaping like a toothless mouth. P3 moving in: Hamza squeezes through a gap · dust, smell of old popcorn and damp, sticky carpet. P4 mood change: rows of red seats in the dark, one beam of light from a hole in the roof. P5 the detail: the old projector is warm. P6 hook: on the torn screen, a flickering picture begins to play.
