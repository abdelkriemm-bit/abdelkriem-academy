Rhetorical Synthesis (Student Notes)

Student-notes questions made easy: read the goal first, check every part of it, and avoid the true-but-off-goal trap — goal types, four worked examples and a 10-question practice quiz.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Rhetorical synthesis questions belong to Expression of Ideas, about 20% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (usually 8–12, shared with transitions). They are usually the last questions in each module, so you meet them when time is short — good news, because with the right method they are some of the fastest points on the test.

Instead of a normal passage, you see a list of bullet-point notes that a student has taken while researching a topic. The question names a goal, for example: "The student wants to emphasize a similarity between the two dams" or "The student wants to introduce the qanat to an audience unfamiliar with it." Then comes the fixed stem: "Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?" The four choices are sentences built from the notes.

Here is the secret: almost every choice is true according to the notes. The question is not "Which sentence is correct?" but "Which sentence does this exact job?" This is a writing-purpose question, not a reading-comprehension question.

The method: goal first, notes last

  1. Skip the notes. Go straight to the question and read the goal. The goal is the only thing that decides the answer.
  2. Break the goal into parts. "Emphasize / the difference / in length / between the two dams" has four parts. "Introduce / qanats / to an audience unfamiliar with them" means: name it + say what it is.
  3. Predict what the right sentence must contain. For a difference: both things + a contrast word (while, whereas, unlike, but) + the feature named in the goal.
  4. Check every choice against every part of the goal. Cross out any choice that misses even one part: only one of the two things, the wrong feature, a similarity instead of a difference, no explanation for a new audience.
  5. Use the notes only to break a tie or check accuracy. If two choices seem to fit, look back at the notes to see which one uses the information correctly.
السر في السؤال ده
  • ما تقراش الملاحظات (notes) في الأول خالص. اقرأ الهدف (The student wants to…) وقسّمه أجزاء.
  • كل الاختيارات تقريبًا صح من ناحية المعلومات، بس واحد بس اللي بيحقق الهدف بالظبط. الاختيار «الصح بس مش في الموضوع» هو الفخ.
  • لو الهدف «تشابه» لازم تلاقي الحاجتين + كلمة زي both أو like. لو «اختلاف» لازم الحاجتين + while أو unlike.

Worked examples

Worked example 1: emphasize a similarity

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • The Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2560 BCE.
  • It was built mainly from limestone blocks.
  • El Castillo is a pyramid in Mexico built by the Maya more than 3,000 years later.
  • El Castillo is also built mainly from limestone.
  • The Great Pyramid originally had smooth sides, while El Castillo has a staircase on each of its four sides.

The student wants to emphasize a similarity between the two pyramids. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

  • A) The Great Pyramid of Giza was built around 2560 BCE, while El Castillo was built more than 3,000 years later.
  • B) Both the Great Pyramid of Giza and El Castillo were built mainly from limestone.
  • C) El Castillo, a Maya pyramid in Mexico, has a staircase on each of its four sides.
  • D) Unlike the Great Pyramid, which originally had smooth sides, El Castillo has four staircases.
Answer: B
  • Goal parts: similarity + both pyramids. Only B names both pyramids AND a shared feature (limestone), with the signal word "Both".
  • A is wrong: true, but it shows a difference in dates (true but off-goal).
  • C is wrong: it describes only one pyramid (half the goal).
  • D is wrong: it is a difference, signalled by "Unlike" (opposite of the goal).

Worked example 2: introduce to an unfamiliar audience

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Tahtib is a traditional Egyptian martial art performed with a long wooden stick.
  • Scenes resembling tahtib appear in ancient Egyptian tomb art.
  • Today it is often performed at weddings and festivals in Upper Egypt, usually accompanied by music.
  • In 2016, UNESCO added tahtib to its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The student wants to introduce tahtib to an audience unfamiliar with it. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

  • A) Tahtib, which appears in ancient Egyptian tomb art, was added to a UNESCO list in 2016.
  • B) In 2016, tahtib joined UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
  • C) Tahtib, a traditional Egyptian martial art performed with a long wooden stick, is often seen at weddings and festivals in Upper Egypt.
  • D) Tahtib is often performed with music at weddings and festivals.
Answer: C
  • An unfamiliar reader first needs to know WHAT tahtib is. Only C explains it ("a traditional Egyptian martial art performed with a long wooden stick").
  • A is wrong: it gives history and an award but never says what tahtib is — the reader could think it is a painting or a song.
  • B is wrong: same problem; the name is used with no explanation.
  • D is wrong: "performed with music" could describe a dance or a song; it does not say it is a martial art.

Worked example 3: present a study and its findings

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • In 2023, a team led by ecologist Mai Ibrahim studied 40 school gardens in Cairo.
  • The team counted the butterfly species that visited each garden over one year.
  • Gardens planted with native flowering plants attracted an average of 14 butterfly species.
  • Gardens planted mostly with imported ornamental plants attracted an average of 6 species.

The student wants to present the study and its findings. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

  • A) In a 2023 study of 40 Cairo school gardens, Mai Ibrahim's team found that gardens with native flowering plants attracted more than twice as many butterfly species (14 on average) as gardens with mostly imported plants (6).
  • B) Mai Ibrahim's team counted the butterfly species that visited 40 school gardens in Cairo over the course of one year.
  • C) Butterflies visit school gardens in Cairo that contain native plants and gardens that contain imported plants.
  • D) Gardens planted mostly with imported ornamental plants attracted an average of six butterfly species.
Answer: A
  • The goal has two parts: the study (who, what) AND its findings (the result). Only A gives both, and its comparison (14 vs 6 = more than twice) matches the notes.
  • B is wrong: it presents the study's method but not the findings (half the goal).
  • C is wrong: it hides the finding; it says nothing about the difference between the gardens.
  • D is wrong: it gives one number from the results but no study and no comparison (half the goal).

Worked example 4: a difference in one specific feature

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

  • Emperor penguins and Adélie penguins both live in Antarctica.
  • Emperor penguins can grow to more than one meter tall.
  • Adélie penguins grow to about 70 centimeters tall.
  • Emperor penguins breed during the Antarctic winter.
  • Adélie penguins breed during the Antarctic summer.

The student wants to emphasize a difference in the breeding seasons of the two species. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

  • A) Emperor penguins, which can grow to more than one meter tall, are much larger than Adélie penguins.
  • B) Both emperor penguins and Adélie penguins live and breed in Antarctica.
  • C) Emperor penguins breed during the Antarctic winter.
  • D) While emperor penguins breed during the harsh Antarctic winter, Adélie penguins breed in the summer.
Answer: D
  • Goal parts: difference + breeding season + both species. D has all three, with the contrast word "While".
  • A is the classic trap: it is a difference between the two species, but in SIZE, not breeding season (true but off-goal).
  • B is wrong: it shows a similarity.
  • C is wrong: it mentions only one species, so no difference is shown (half the goal).
Classic traps
  • True but off-goal: the sentence is accurate and well written but does a different job (a difference in size when the goal asks about breeding seasons).
  • Half the goal: only one of two things, or the study without its findings, or the method without the results.
  • Reversed goal: a similarity when the goal asks for a difference, or the opposite.
  • The name with no explanation: for an unfamiliar audience, a choice that only uses the name fails, however interesting its facts.
  • Over-explaining for a familiar audience: a choice that defines something the audience already knows is wasting the sentence.
  • The biggest-sentence trap: the longest choice with the most facts is not automatically right. Relevance beats quantity.
  • Reading the notes first: you waste time and start judging choices by interest, not by the goal.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Jump to the question line and read the goal. Underline (in your head) the key words: similarity, difference, introduce, unfamiliar, findings, method, aim, when and where.
  • Predict the shape of the answer: both + both/like; both + while/unlike; name + "a/an…" description; study + found.
  • Eliminate choices by the goal alone. You will often answer without reading the notes at all.
  • Check the notes only if two choices are left.
  • These questions usually come last in the module: keep 4–5 minutes for them, roughly 40–60 seconds each.

Practice

Remember
  • Read the goal before the notes; the goal alone decides the answer.
  • Check every part of the goal: both items, the right feature, the right audience, study + findings.
  • Most choices are true — the trap is a true sentence that does a different job.
  • Unfamiliar audience → name + explanation. Familiar audience → no basic definition needed.
  • Similarity → both / like. Difference → while / unlike / than.
