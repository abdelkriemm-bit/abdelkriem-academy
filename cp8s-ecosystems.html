Ecosystems, Bioaccumulation and Invasive Species

Ecosystems around the Earth and the habitats inside them, how toxic substances build up along food chains, and how invasive species such as water hyacinth change whole ecosystems — with food-web practice in Checkpoint style.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 Science — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Identify different ecosystems on the Earth and recognise that one ecosystem contains many different habitats (8Be.01)
  • Describe how toxic substances build up in living things (bioaccumulation) and the effect this has on an ecosystem (8Be.02)
  • Describe how a new or invasive species can affect other organisms and the whole ecosystem (8Be.03)

An ecosystem is all the living organisms in an area together with the non-living (physical) environment they live in, and all the ways they interact. The organisms depend on each other (for food, shelter, pollination) and on the non-living parts (water, light, temperature, soil, air). Because everything is linked, a change to one part — a poison in the water, or a new animal arriving — can affect the whole ecosystem.

Ecosystems on the Earth

Ecosystems come in all sizes. The Red Sea coral reef is a huge ecosystem; a pond, a rotting log or even a school garden is a small one. Each ecosystem has its own conditions, and the organisms living there are adapted to them.

Microhabitats are tiny habitats: under a stone in the desert (cool and damp for a scorpion), inside a crack in a coral, under the bark of a tree. Exam tip: an ecosystem includes the non-living environment; a habitat is just the place where an organism lives.

النظام البيئي والموطن

النظام البيئي (ecosystem) = كل الكائنات الحية في منطقة + البيئة غير الحية (مياه، ضوء، حرارة، تربة) والعلاقة بينهم. الموطن (habitat) = المكان اللي الكائن عايش فيه. النظام البيئي الواحد فيه مواطن كتير: الشعاب المرجانية في البحر الأحمر فيها حشائش البحر، والكهوف والشقوق، والمية المفتوحة، والقاع الرملي.

Food chains and food webs: a quick reminder

Feeding links join the organisms of an ecosystem. In a food chain, the arrow means "is eaten by" and shows the direction energy (and anything in the food) moves. A producer (a plant or alga) makes its own food; consumers eat other organisms. The animal at the end of the chain is the top predator. A food web shows many food chains linked together. Because of these links, anything that changes one population affects others — and anything that is in the food (including poisons) is passed along the chain.

Bioaccumulation of toxic substances

Some toxic substances get into ecosystems from human activities: pesticides (such as DDT, used in the past to kill insects), heavy metals such as mercury and lead from factories and mining, and some industrial chemicals. The dangerous ones share two properties:

  • They are not broken down easily in the environment — they last for many years.
  • When an organism takes them in, its body cannot break them down or get rid of them, so they are stored (often in fat) and build up over its life. This is bioaccumulation.
  1. Tiny amounts of the pesticide wash into the water, and plankton (tiny organisms) take it in. It stays in their bodies.
  2. Each small fish eats many plankton over its life. It keeps all the pesticide from all of them, so its concentration is higher.
  3. Each large fish eats many small fish, and each fish-eating bird eats many large fish. At every step the pesticide becomes more concentrated.
  4. So top predators have the highest concentration — high enough to harm or kill them, even though the water seemed only slightly polluted.

The impact on an ecosystem

EffectExample
Top predators are poisoned — they become ill, die, or cannot reproduceIn the 1950s–60s, DDT built up in birds of prey such as peregrine falcons and eagles. It made them lay eggs with thin shells that broke when the parents sat on them, so few chicks hatched and their populations fell sharply. Many countries then banned DDT, and the birds slowly recovered.
Humans can be harmed — we are often top consumersIn Minamata, Japan (1950s), a factory released mercury compounds into the bay. Mercury built up in fish and shellfish, and people and cats that ate them were badly poisoned. Today pregnant women are often advised to limit large predatory fish such as swordfish, which can contain more mercury.
Knock-on effects through the food webIf the top predator population falls, the animals it ate may increase in number; they then eat more of their own food, so that population may fall. The balance of the whole ecosystem changes and biodiversity can fall.

How to reduce bioaccumulation: ban or limit long-lasting pesticides; use pesticides that break down quickly or biological pest control; treat factory waste before it reaches rivers and seas; test fish for mercury.

Exam technique — calculations: "How many times greater?" means divide the bigger concentration by the smaller one. For example, bird ÷ small fish = 25 ÷ 0.5 = 50 times.

New and invasive species

A native species lives naturally in an area. Sometimes a new species arrives that has never lived there. Humans often bring them — on purpose (as pets, farm animals, garden plants, fish for fishing, or to control pests) or by accident (in ships' ballast water, on cargo, through canals). Many new species die out or fit in without harm. But some become invasive: they spread quickly and harm native species and the ecosystem.

Why invasive species spread so quicklyHow they affect other organisms and the ecosystem
No natural predators or diseases in the new area (these were left behind in their home area)Competition: they compete with native species for food, space, light or nesting sites, and often win, so native populations fall
They reproduce quickly and in large numbersPredation: they eat native species that have no defences against them
They are good competitors and can live in many conditionsDisease: they can bring diseases that kill native species
Native animals may not eat them (they may be poisonous or unfamiliar)Habitat change: they can change the habitat itself (e.g. covering water, blocking light)
Food web effects: changes in one population pass along the web; biodiversity falls; some native species may become extinct

Case studies

Invasive speciesHow it arrivedEffects
Water hyacinth in the Nile and Egyptian canalsA floating plant from South America, spread as a garden pond plantGrows extremely fast and covers the water surface → blocks light, so water plants and algae below die → when they rot, microorganisms use up the oxygen, so fish die. It also blocks irrigation canals and boats, and loses a lot of water from its leaves.
Nile perch in Lake Victoria (East Africa)Put into the lake in the 1950s to give fishermen a large fish to catchA large predator with no predators of its own in the lake; it ate huge numbers of small native cichlid fish, and many of the hundreds of cichlid species disappeared.
Cane toad in AustraliaBrought in 1935 to eat beetles that damaged sugar caneIt did not control the beetles, but bred quickly and spread over a huge area. Its skin is poisonous, so native predators (snakes, lizards, quolls) that eat it die.
Lionfish in the Caribbean and western AtlanticNative to the Red Sea and Indian Ocean; released from home aquariumsNo predators there; it eats huge numbers of small reef fish, so reef fish populations fall.
Grey squirrel in BritainBrought from North AmericaOut-competes the native red squirrel for food and carries a disease that kills red squirrels; red squirrels have disappeared from most of England.

Controlling invasive species: prevention is best (checking ships and cargo, never releasing pets or aquarium fish into the wild). Others are removed by hand or machine (water hyacinth is cut and lifted out of canals), or controlled by a carefully tested natural enemy — for example, small weevils that eat only water hyacinth have been used in some lakes.

How to answer food web questions about a new species: (1) find what the new species eats — those populations will decrease; (2) find which animals ate the same food — they now face competition and may decrease; (3) follow the arrows one more step — the food of the animals that decreased may now increase. Always give the reason for each change.

التراكم الحيوي والأنواع الغازية

التراكم الحيوي: مادة سامة (زي مبيد DDT أو الزئبق) ما بتتكسرش وما بتخرجش من الجسم، فبتتخزن. وكل حيوان بياكل كتير من اللي قبله في السلسلة الغذائية، فالتركيز بيزيد في كل خطوة، وأعلى تركيز بيكون في المفترس الأخير (زي الطيور اللي بتاكل سمك). النوع الغازي: كائن جديد اتنقل لمكان مش مكانه، ومالوش أعداء طبيعيين هناك، فبيتكاثر بسرعة وينافس أو ياكل الأنواع المحلية — زي ورد النيل اللي بيغطي المية ويمنع الضوء فالسمك يموت.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 (ecosystem or habitat?) (a) Explain the difference between an ecosystem and a habitat. [2] (b) Name two different habitats within a coral reef ecosystem. [1]

  • (a) An ecosystem is all the organisms in an area and their physical (non-living) environment, interacting [1]; a habitat is the place where an organism lives [1].
  • (b) Any two: seagrass bed, reef crest, caves / cracks in the coral, open water, sandy sea floor, reef slope.
  • Common mistake: naming another ecosystem ("desert") when the question asks for a habitat within the reef.

Worked example 2 (bioaccumulation calculation) Use the pesticide diagram above. (a) How many times greater is the concentration in the large fish than in the plankton? [1] (b) Explain why the fish-eating bird has the highest concentration. [2]

  • (a) 2 ÷ 0.04 = 50 times
  • (b) The pesticide is not broken down / not excreted, so it stays in the body [1]; each bird eats many fish over its life, so it takes in the pesticide from all of them and it builds up to a high concentration [1].
  • Common mistake: "The bird drinks the polluted water." The water concentration is tiny; nearly all the pesticide comes from food.

Worked example 3 (impact on an ecosystem) A farmer sprays a long-lasting pesticide near a river. Twenty years later there are far fewer kingfishers (fish-eating birds) along the river. Suggest how the pesticide caused this and one other effect on the river ecosystem. [3]

  • The pesticide washed into the river and built up along the food chain (bioaccumulation) [1].
  • Kingfishers, as top predators, got the highest concentration, so many died or could not breed (e.g. thin eggshells) [1].
  • Other effect: with fewer kingfishers, the number of small fish may increase, so the animals they eat (e.g. insect larvae) may decrease / the balance of the food web changes [1].

Worked example 4 (invasive species in a food web) Use the lake food web above. The introduced fish spreads quickly. (a) Suggest what happens to the number of small fish. Explain your answer. [2] (b) Explain why the number of herons may fall. [2] (c) Suggest what may happen to the algae. Explain your answer. [2]

  • (a) Small fish decrease [1], because the introduced fish eats them (and it has no predators in the lake) [1].
  • (b) The heron also eats small fish, so there is less food for it / it competes with the introduced fish [1]; herons may eat more frogs, so frogs decrease too, and some herons starve or move away [1].
  • (c) Algae may increase [1], because fewer small fish and insect larvae are left to eat the algae [1].
  • Exam technique: use the word because, and name the organisms from the web.

Worked example 5 (water hyacinth) Water hyacinth covers the surface of a canal. Explain why fish in the canal die. [3]

  1. The plant blocks light from reaching water plants and algae below. [1]
  2. They cannot photosynthesise, so they die and rot; the microorganisms that decompose them use up oxygen (and less oxygen is made by photosynthesis). [1]
  3. The fish do not get enough oxygen for respiration, so they die. [1]

Worked example 6 (why invasive species spread) Suggest two reasons why cane toads spread so quickly across Australia. [2]

  • Any two: no natural predators in Australia (predators that eat them die because the toads are poisonous); no diseases to control them; they reproduce quickly / lay many eggs; plenty of food; they can live in many habitats.
  • Common mistake: "because they are invasive" — that just repeats the question.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up ecosystem (living + non-living, interacting) and habitat (the place an organism lives).
  • Saying the top predator gets poisoned because it "drinks the water". It gets the poison from the many organisms it eats.
  • Forgetting to say the substance is not broken down or excreted — that is why it builds up.
  • Writing "the population will change" without saying increase or decrease and why.
  • Thinking every new species is invasive. A species is invasive only if it spreads and causes harm.
  • Reading food web arrows backwards. The arrow points to the eater.
  • In "how many times" questions, subtracting instead of dividing.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Food web questions: "An invasive species is added. Describe and explain what happens to population X." Give the change (increase/decrease) and the reason from the web.
  • Bioaccumulation data: a diagram or table of concentrations along a food chain. Calculate "how many times greater" and explain why the top predator has the most.
  • Match ecosystems to their conditions, or identify habitats within one ecosystem from a picture.
  • Suggest reasons: why an invasive species spreads (no predators, fast reproduction) or why a toxic substance is dangerous (not broken down).
  • Evaluate: "Was introducing species X a good idea?" Give an advantage and a disadvantage and reach a conclusion.

Practice

Remember
  • Ecosystem = living organisms + their non-living environment, interacting. Habitat = the place where an organism lives. One ecosystem has many habitats.
  • Examples of ecosystems: desert, coral reef, river or lake, rainforest, grassland, polar, deep ocean.
  • Bioaccumulation: a toxic substance that is not broken down or excreted builds up in an organism; its concentration increases at each step of a food chain, so top predators are harmed most.
  • Invasive species spread fast (no predators or diseases, fast breeding) and harm natives by competition, predation and disease; effects pass through the food web and biodiversity falls.
  • In food web questions, always state increase or decrease AND give the reason using the feeding links.
