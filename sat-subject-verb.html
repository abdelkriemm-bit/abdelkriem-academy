Subject–Verb Agreement

Find the true subject every time — even when it is hidden behind phrases, inverted, collective or Latin — and choose the verb form the SAT expects.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

Subject–verb agreement is the most frequently tested grammar rule in the Form, Structure, and Sense part of the Digital SAT. The rule itself is short — a singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb — but the test hides the subject so well that strong students still lose points. This page teaches you how to find the true subject every time, lists every tricky subject the test likes, and then trains you with worked examples and a 12-question quiz.

What this question type tests

Subject–verb agreement belongs to the Standard English Conventions domain, which is about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions (about 11–15 questions across the two modules). Inside that domain, the skill is called Form, Structure, and Sense, together with pronouns, verb tense, finiteness, modifiers and plural/possessive nouns. These questions usually come after the Information and Ideas questions in each module.

Every question has one short passage (25–150 words) with a blank, and the stem is always the same: "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?" You can recognise an agreement question in two seconds by looking at the choices: they are the same verb in different forms, for example:

  • is / are / was / were
  • show / shows / have shown / has shown
  • provides / provide / are providing / has provided

When two choices are singular and two are plural, the question is mainly about agreement. When the choices also change tense (has / had / will), you must check the tense as well — see the page on Verb Tense, Aspect and Finiteness. Often one step removes two choices (wrong number) and the second step removes one more (wrong tense).

The basic rule — and the "-s" confusion

In the present tense, a singular subject (he, she, it, one thing) takes a verb with -s; a plural subject (they, two or more things) takes a verb without -s. This is the opposite of nouns, where -s usually means plural. Students who are not native speakers often mix this up, so learn the table.

SubjectPresentPresent perfectPast of bePresent of be
Singular: the scientist / itstudieshas studiedwasis
Plural: the scientists / theystudyhave studiedwereare

Only these forms change with number: present simple (-s), is/are, was/were, has/have, does/do. The simple past of normal verbs (studied, showed) and modal verbs (can, will, must) are the same for singular and plural — so a past-tense or modal choice is never wrong because of agreement alone.

Step 1: Find the true subject

Ask: "Who or what does the action of this verb?" Then remove everything that is not the subject. The test usually puts one of these between the subject and the verb:

What to cross outSignal words✓ Correct✗ Wrong (the trap)
Prepositional phraseof, in, on, at, with, for, from, among, between, near, aboutThe list of new rules is on the board.The list of new rules are on the board.
Appositive / extra information between commas, dashes or brackets, a … , / — … —The pharaoh, together with his advisers, was buried nearby.The pharaoh, together with his advisers, were buried nearby.
Relative clausewho, which, that, whose, whereThe students who joined the club meet on Mondays.The students who joined the club meets on Mondays.
Participle phrase-ing / -ed phrase after the nounThe samples collected from the lake contain algae.The samples collected from the lake contains algae.
"Fake" joining phrasesalong with, as well as, together with, in addition to, including, likeRania, as well as her sisters, plays the piano.Rania, as well as her sisters, play the piano.

After crossing out, read the skeleton sentence: subject + verb + rest. "The collection [of rare coins found in the tomb] is …" The skeleton must sound right on its own.

Only "and" makes a plural

A and B = plural (The teacher and the student are ready). Phrases like as well as, along with, together with, in addition to do NOT add to the subject; they are extra information, so the verb follows the first noun only.

Step 2: Know the tricky subjects

Once you have the subject, check whether it is one of the special cases below. Each one appears regularly on the test.

Subject typeRule✓ Example✗ Example
each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, someone, nobody, anythingAlways singular — even with "of + plural" after themEach of the samples was tested.Each of the samples were tested.
every / each + A and BSingularEvery student and teacher has a badge.Every student and teacher have a badge.
both, few, many, severalAlways pluralSeveral of the vases are cracked.Several of the vases is cracked.
all, most, some, none, any, half + ofLook at the noun after "of"Most of the water is salty. / Most of the wells are dry.Most of the wells is dry.
either … or / neither … nor / not only … but also / … or …The verb agrees with the subject closer to itNeither the coach nor the players were told. Neither the players nor the coach was told.Neither the coach nor the players was told.
Collective nouns: team, committee, jury, family, class, staff, audience, government, company, band, public, population, flock, colonySingular in American English (the SAT standard)The committee has approved the plan.The committee have approved the plan.
the number of + plural nounSingular (the subject is "number")The number of visitors has doubled.The number of visitors have doubled.
a number of + plural nounPlural (it means "many")A number of visitors have complained.A number of visitors has complained.
-ing / to-verb / clause as subjectSingularCollecting fossils requires patience. Whether the drug works remains unclear.Collecting fossils require patience.
Words that end in -s but are singular: news, mathematics, physics, economics, measlesSingularThe news was surprising.The news were surprising.
British school students: collective nouns

At British schools you may have learned "The team are playing well." British English allows this. The SAT tests American Standard English, where a collective noun is one unit: "The team is playing well" and "The team won its match." Follow the American rule on this test.

Step 3: Plural words from Latin and Greek

Science passages love these words. Their plural does not end in -s, so students think they are singular. Learn both columns.

Singular (one)Plural (more than one)✓ Example
phenomenonphenomenaThese phenomena are rare.
criterioncriteriaThe criteria were published in advance.
bacteriumbacteriaSome bacteria survive boiling water.
mediummediaThe new media have changed how news spreads.
analysis / hypothesis / thesisanalyses / hypotheses / thesesBoth hypotheses predict the same result.
nucleus / fungus / stimulusnuclei / fungi / stimuliThe fungi grow in damp soil.
datum (rare)dataIn formal scientific writing: These data show a clear pattern.

A note on data: in everyday English many people say "the data is", but formal scientific writing treats data as plural. A well-made question will not depend on "data is" versus "data are" alone, but if a passage already treats data as plural ("these data"), keep it plural. Words like phenomena, criteria and bacteria are always plural.

Step 4: Inverted sentences — the subject comes after the verb

Sometimes the verb comes before its subject. The SAT uses three patterns. In each one, find the noun after the verb and turn the sentence around in your head.

Pattern✓ CorrectTurned around✗ Trap
There is / There are (there is never the subject)There are three reasons for the delay.Three reasons are …There is three reasons …
Among / Of / Inside / On + place + verb + subjectAmong the findings was a carved ivory comb.A carved ivory comb was among the findings.Among the findings were a carved ivory comb.
Here + verb + subject, Only then / Not only + verb + subjectHere are the results.The results are here.Here is the results.

لما الجملة تبدأ بـ There أو Among أو On the wall أو Inside the box، الفاعل بييجي بعد الفعل. اقلب الجملة في دماغك: Among the findings was a comb ← A comb was among the findings. وخلّي بالك: findings اسم جمع بس هو مش الفاعل.

Step 5: Relative clauses — agree with the noun before "who / which / that"

The verb inside a relative clause agrees with the noun the pronoun refers to. "The wells that supply the village are old." Here that supply agrees with *wells*, and the main verb are also agrees with *wells*. Check both verbs separately.

The 6-step method for test day

  1. Read the choices first. Same verb, singular vs plural? It is an agreement question.
  2. Find the verb's subject. Ask "Who or what ___?" Do not trust the nearest noun.
  3. Cross out prepositional phrases, extra information between commas or dashes, relative clauses, and "as well as" phrases.
  4. Check the special list: each/every/neither, either…or, collective nouns, the number of / a number of, Latin plurals, -ing subjects, inverted sentences.
  5. Eliminate the two choices with the wrong number. Then choose between the remaining two by tense: match the time clues and the other verbs in the passage.
  6. Read the skeleton sentence (subject + chosen verb) once to confirm.

Worked examples

Worked example 1: the long subject

Passage: "The ancient trade routes of the Sahara, which connected salt mines in the north with gold-producing regions in the south, ______ for far more than the exchange of goods. Historians note that ideas, languages, and religious practices also traveled along them."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) was responsible
  • B) is responsible
  • C) were responsible
  • D) has been responsible

Answer: C) were responsible. Cross out the prepositional phrase "of the Sahara" and the relative clause "which connected … in the south". The skeleton is "The routes … were responsible" — plural. The routes are historical and the passage uses past tense ("traveled"), so the plural past form is correct.

  • A) was responsible — Nearest-noun trap: singular, as if the subject were "the south" or "the Sahara".
  • B) is responsible — Singular, and present tense does not fit the historical context.
  • D) has been responsible — Singular "has"; the subject "routes" needs "have".

Worked example 2: neither … nor

Passage: "Nobody knows why the eastern arch of the old stone bridge is lower than the western one. Neither the original architect nor the engineers who later restored the bridge ______ any written record explaining the difference."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) has left
  • B) have left
  • C) leaves
  • D) was leaving

Answer: B) have left. With neither … nor, the verb agrees with the subject closer to it. The closer subject is "the engineers" (plural), so the verb must be plural: "have left". (Cross out "who later restored the bridge" first.)

  • A) has left — Agrees with "architect", the farther subject. Neither…nor follows the closer subject.
  • C) leaves — Singular, and present simple does not fit a finished action in the past.
  • D) was leaving — Singular "was", and the progressive form makes no sense for leaving a record.

Worked example 3: the number of

Passage: "In the Fayoum Oasis, a small team of bird researchers has counted migratory birds at Lake Qarun every winter since 2015. The number of species recorded at the lake ______ grown steadily, a trend the researchers link to cleaner water."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) have
  • B) has
  • C) are
  • D) were

Answer: B) has. The number of + plural noun is singular: the subject is "number", not "species". The time clue "since 2015" and the word "grown" need the present perfect: "has grown".

  • A) have — Plural — the classic trap of agreeing with "species".
  • C) are — "Are grown" is a passive form that changes the meaning, and it is plural.
  • D) were — Plural, and "were grown" is passive past — it does not fit "since 2015".

Worked example 4: inverted sentence

Passage: "Divers surveying coral reefs in the Red Sea found that many colonies had lost their color after a hot summer. Among the most surprising results of the survey ______ several healthy colonies near the Gulf of Aqaba that seemed to resist the heat."

Question: Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) was
  • B) is
  • C) has been
  • D) were

Answer: D) were. The sentence is inverted. Turn it around: "Several healthy colonies … were among the most surprising results." The subject is "colonies" (plural), and the passage is in the past ("found", "had lost"), so "were" is correct.

  • A) was — Singular. The trap is treating "the survey" (the nearest noun, singular) as the subject.
  • B) is — Singular and present tense.
  • C) has been — Singular "has".
Classic traps
  • Nearest-noun trap: the noun right before the blank is in a prepositional phrase or relative clause. Always cross out and find the real subject.
  • "As well as" trap: along with / as well as / together with do not make a plural subject. Only "and" does.
  • Collective-noun trap: team, committee, jury, company and government are singular on the SAT, even if the members are many.
  • Number trap: "the number of" = singular (has, is); "a number of" = plural (have, are).
  • Latin-plural trap: phenomena, criteria, bacteria, hypotheses are plural even without -s.
  • Inversion trap: in "There are …" and "Among the … was …", the subject is after the verb.
  • "Each of the" trap: each, every, either, neither and one stay singular even when followed by a plural noun.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Look at the choices first; if singular and plural forms are mixed, put your finger on the subject before reading anything else.
  • Treat everything between two commas or two dashes as invisible.
  • Say the skeleton in your head: "The number … has", "Each … was", "The routes … were".
  • If two choices have the correct number, the tense decides — look at the other verbs and time words in the passage.
  • These questions should take 30–45 seconds. Save the extra time for reading questions.

Practice

Twelve original test-style questions. Use the 6-step method on each one. At the end you will see your score, the correct answers and an explanation for every question.

Remember
  • The verb agrees with the true subject, not the nearest noun: cross out prepositional phrases, extra information and relative clauses.
  • Only and makes a plural subject; as well as / along with do not. With either…or / neither…nor, follow the closer subject.
  • Singular: each, every, either, neither, collective nouns (team, committee), the number of, -ing subjects. Plural: a number of, both, several, phenomena, criteria, bacteria.
  • In There is/are and Among the … was/were sentences, the subject comes after the verb.
  • Fix the number first, then let the passage's time clues decide the tense.
