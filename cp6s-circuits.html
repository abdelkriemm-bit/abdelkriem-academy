Electric Circuits, Symbols, Series and Parallel

Draw and read circuit diagrams with the standard symbols, and compare how bright lamps are in series and parallel circuits.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 Science — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Use circuit diagrams and the standard symbols to show, build and compare circuits with cells, switches, lamps and buzzers (6Pe.01).
  • Build simple circuits and compare how bright the lamps are in series and parallel circuits (6Pe.02).

1. What makes a circuit work?

An electric circuit is a path that electricity flows around. A circuit only works when it is a complete loop with no gaps: from one end of the cell, through the wires and components, and back to the other end of the cell. The flow of electricity around the circuit is called the current.

  • A cell pushes the current around the circuit. Two or more cells joined together make a battery, which gives a bigger push.
  • Wires are made of a metal such as copper, which is a good electrical conductor. They are covered in plastic, an insulator, to keep us safe.
  • Components such as lamps and buzzers use the electricity: a lamp gives out light, a buzzer makes a sound.
  • A switch opens or closes a gap in the circuit. Closed switch = complete circuit = current flows. Open switch = gap = no current.

Safety

Only do circuit experiments with cells. Never use mains electricity (wall sockets) — it can kill. Do not connect a wire straight from one end of a cell to the other with nothing in between: the wire and cell get hot.

2. Circuit symbols

Scientists all over the world draw circuits with the same simple symbols instead of pictures. Learn these eight — you must be able to draw them and recognise them.

SymbolWhat to remember
CellTwo lines: a long thin line and a short thick line. The long line is the positive (+) end.
Battery of cellsTwo or more cell symbols joined in a row, all facing the same way. Count the pairs of lines to count the cells.
LampA circle with a cross (×) inside.
BuzzerA dome shape on two short legs.
Open switchA gap: the lever is lifted up, so no current flows.
Closed switchNo gap: the lever is down, so current flows.
WireA straight line drawn with a ruler.
JunctionA dot where three or more wires meet.

Rules for drawing circuit diagrams

  1. Use a ruler and a pencil. Draw wires as straight lines with square corners.
  2. Use the correct symbol for every component — never a picture.
  3. Leave no gaps in the wires (except an open switch, which is meant to be a gap).
  4. Do not draw a wire through the middle of a symbol.
  5. Put a dot at every junction where wires join.
  6. Label components if the question names them (lamp A, switch S1…).

3. Series circuits

In a series circuit, all the components are joined one after another in a single loop. There is only one path for the current, so the same current flows through every component.

  • Adding more lamps in series makes every lamp dimmer. The push from the cell has to be shared between the lamps.
  • If one lamp breaks or is unscrewed, there is a gap in the only path, so all the lamps go out.
  • One switch anywhere in the loop turns everything on and off together.
  • Adding more cells (in the same direction) gives a bigger push, so lamps get brighter and buzzers get louder. Too many cells can make a lamp blow (its thin wire breaks).

4. Parallel circuits

In a parallel circuit, the components are on separate branches. At each junction the current splits up and then joins again. There is more than one path for the current.

  • Each lamp in parallel gets the full push of the cell, so each lamp is as bright as one lamp on its own with the same cell.
  • If one lamp breaks, the other branch is still a complete loop, so the other lamp stays on (at the same brightness).
  • A switch on a branch controls only the lamp on that branch. A switch on the main part of the circuit (next to the cell) controls everything.
  • The lights in your home are wired in parallel: you can switch each light on and off on its own, and one broken bulb does not turn off the whole house.

5. Comparing brightness

Circuit (identical cells and lamps)Brightness of each lamp
1 cell, 1 lampnormal brightness (this is our standard)
1 cell, 2 lamps in seriesboth dimmer than normal (both the same)
1 cell, 3 lamps in seriesall even dimmer
1 cell, 2 lamps in parallelboth at normal brightness
2 cells, 1 lampbrighter than normal
2 cells, 2 lamps in seriesabout normal brightness (double push shared by two lamps)
Quick rule

More cells → brighter. More lamps in series → dimmer. Lamps in parallel → each one as bright as a single lamp. The same rules work for buzzers: more cells → louder; more components in series → quieter.

Why might a circuit not work? Check: Is a switch open? Is there a gap or a loose connection? Is a lamp broken (blown)? Is the cell used up (flat)? Has an insulator (plastic, wood, rubber) been placed in the circuit? Are two cells in a battery facing opposite ways so their pushes cancel?

7. Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Look at the circuit below. (a) How many cells are there? [1] (b) Name the two components on the bottom wire. [1] (c) Is the switch open or closed? [1]

Answer: (a) 3 cells (count the pairs of long and short lines). (b) a lamp and a buzzer. (c) closed — the line joins both terminals, so there is no gap. It is a series circuit, so the lamp lights and the buzzer sounds.

Worked example 2 — Draw a circuit diagram for: one cell, an open switch, a lamp and a buzzer, all in series. [2]

  1. Draw a rectangle loop with a ruler.
  2. Put the cell symbol on the top side, the open switch on one side, and the lamp and buzzer on the bottom.
  3. Check: one single loop, correct symbols, no extra gaps, no wire through a symbol.

Mark scheme style: 1 mark for all four correct symbols; 1 mark for everything in one complete loop (series). Common mistake: drawing the cell as two equal lines, or drawing a picture of a bulb.

Worked example 3 — Circuits A, B and C in section 5 all use identical cells and lamps. (a) In which circuit(s) are the lamps dimmest? [1] (b) Explain why. [1]

Answer: (a) B. (b) The two lamps are in series, so the push of one cell is shared between two lamps. (In C, each lamp in parallel gets the full push of the cell, so each is as bright as the lamp in A.)

Worked example 4 — Mariam's circuit has two lamps in parallel (like the parallel circuit in section 4). Lamp A breaks. What happens to lamp B? Explain. [2]

Answer: Lamp B stays on at the same brightness (1 mark), because it is on a separate branch that is still a complete loop with the cell (1 mark). Common mistake: saying lamp B gets brighter — in a parallel circuit it stays the same.

Worked example 5 — Look at the circuit with switches S1, S2 and S3. Which switches must be closed to light lamp P but not lamp Q? [2]

Answer: close S3 (the main switch, so current can leave the cell) and S1 (to complete lamp P's branch); keep S2 open so lamp Q stays off.

Worked example 6 (investigation) — Ali investigates how the number of cells affects the loudness of a buzzer. (a) Name the independent variable. [1] (b) Give one control variable. [1] (c) Predict the result. [1]

Answer: (a) the number of cells. (b) the same buzzer / the same wires / the same distance from the buzzer to the ear or sound meter. (c) The more cells, the louder the buzzer, because more cells give a bigger push to the current.

Common mistakes
  • Drawing pictures of bulbs and batteries instead of symbols, or drawing wires freehand with gaps.
  • Drawing a cell with two lines of the same length. One is long and thin, the other short and thick.
  • Thinking lamps in parallel are dimmer. They are each as bright as a single lamp.
  • Thinking the lamp nearest the cell is brightest in a series circuit. All identical lamps in series are equally bright.
  • Forgetting that an open switch anywhere in a series loop turns everything off.
  • Saying "the current is used up" by the first lamp. The current flows all the way round the loop.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Match or name symbols: draw lines from names to symbols, or write the name under a symbol.
  • Draw a circuit diagram from a picture or description — use a ruler and the correct symbols.
  • Compare brightness: "Which circuit has the brightest lamp?" — count cells and count lamps in series.
  • Predict and explain what happens when a lamp breaks or a switch opens, in series and in parallel.
  • Switch puzzles: which switches to close to make only one lamp or buzzer work.
  • Investigations: variables, predictions and conclusions about the number of cells or lamps.

Practice

Remember
  • A circuit must be a complete loop; an open switch or a gap stops the current.
  • Learn the eight symbols: cell, battery, lamp, buzzer, open switch, closed switch, wire, junction.
  • Series: one path; more lamps → dimmer; one breaks → all go out.
  • Parallel: branches; each lamp as bright as one alone; one breaks → the others stay on.
  • More cells → brighter lamps and louder buzzers (too many can blow a lamp).
  • Draw with a ruler, use symbols, no gaps, dots at junctions.
