Checkpoint 6 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Newspaper Report — Writing Pack

How to write a full-mark news report for Paper 1 — a task type set in a recent paper: features, the 5 Ws lead, structure, useful phrases and two strong samples for a school and a local newspaper.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

A newspaper report (also called a news report) tells readers about a real event quickly, clearly and fairly. It is one of the most important genres to learn for Paper 1, because a news report for a school newspaper about a recent school or local event has appeared in a recent Checkpoint 6 paper. If you can write a strong report, you are ready for one of the most likely tasks in Section B.

Why this page matters
  • A news report task has come up recently in Paper 1 (non-fiction). It may say 'Write a report for your school newspaper…' or 'Write a news report about…'.
  • The event is usually something ordinary that you can imagine easily: a sports day, a school trip, a concert, a competition, a visitor, a storm, a charity day or a local festival.
  • You are allowed to invent the details: names, times, numbers and quotations. The examiner wants to see that you know the features and can organise a report well.

1. What is a newspaper report?

A newspaper report is a type of recount written for the public. The first paragraph gives a short summary of the whole event. The next paragraphs give more details and the words of people who were there. The last paragraph explains how the story ended or what will happen next.

QuestionAnswer for a newspaper report
PurposeTo inform readers about something that has happened. It does not try to persuade.
AudienceReaders of a school newspaper, a local newspaper or a news website: pupils, parents, teachers, people in your town.
ToneFormal and objective (fair, not showing the writer's own feelings). The reporter does not say 'I think'. Other people's opinions appear only inside quotations.
Person and tenseThird person (he, she, they, the pupils). Past tense for what happened. Present tense for how things are now. Future forms (will, is going to) for what happens next.
Where it appearsPaper 1 Non-fiction, Section B Writing.

CategoryMarksWhat a top answer needs in a news report

Vocabulary and language3Formal news words (announced, confirmed, eyewitness) and different reporting verbs instead of 'said' every time.

Marking rules you must know
  • Each category is marked separately using 'best fit'.
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. Between 21 and 60 words can score no more than 7 marks altogether, so always finish your report.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example a diary or a story instead of a report), Creation can be capped at 1 and Structure can be capped at 3 (or 2). Always check the genre in the task.

Newspaper report or story? Report or article?

Many Year 6 pupils lose marks because their report turns into a story or a diary. Look at the differences.

Newspaper reportStory / diaryMagazine article
Starts withThe 5 Ws: the whole event in one or two sentencesSetting the scene slowly: 'It was a sunny morning…'A hook: a question or a surprising fact
PersonThird person: the pupils, theyOften first person: I, weOften first person and 'you'
OpinionsOnly in quotations from other peopleThe writer's feelings everywhereThe writer's opinion is welcome

2. Features of a newspaper report

FeatureWhy it is usedExample
HeadlineGrabs attention and sums up the story in a few words (about 4–6)Pupils Plant Five Hundred Trees
BylineShows who wrote the reportBy Hana Mostafa, Year 6 Reporter
Lead (orientation) paragraphAnswers Who? What? When? Where? (and often Why?) at onceOn Monday morning, sixty Year 6 pupils planted five hundred trees in Al-Azhar Park to make the city greener.
Third person, past tenseKeeps the report fair and factualThe visitors arrived at nine o'clock.
Facts and numbersMake the report sound accurateThe fair raised 12,000 pounds for the children's hospital.
Direct speech (quotations)Lets eyewitnesses and experts speak, adds human interest“I couldn't believe my eyes,” said Ali Hamdy, aged 11.
Reported speechTells what someone said without their exact wordsThe coach said that the team had trained every day.
Reporting verbsAvoids repeating 'said'explained, added, announced, admitted, praised, warned
Passive voicePuts the event first and sounds formalThe match was cancelled. Two pupils were given first aid.

Concluding paragraphExplains the result or what will happen nextThe school plans to hold the event again next spring.

Headline tricks
  • Alliteration (same first sound): Brave Boys Beat the Blaze
  • Rhyme: Not Too Cool for School
  • A short question: Who Took the Trophy?
  • A play on words: Pupils Raise the Roof (about a loud concert)
  • Headlines often leave out small words (a, the) and use the present tense even for past events: School Choir Wins National Prize. Capitalise the main words.

3. How to structure a newspaper report

Reporters use the inverted pyramid: the most important information comes first and the smaller details come later. A reader who reads only the first paragraph should still know the main story.

PartWhat to writeTips
HeadlineA short, catchy title that sums up the storyAbout 4–6 words. Try alliteration or rhyme
BylineBy + reporter's name (+ role)By Adam Sherif, Year 6 Reporter
Paragraph 1 — LeadWho? What? When? Where? (Why?) — the whole story in brief1–2 sentences. Put the most exciting fact first
Paragraph 2 — What happenedThe events in more detail, in time orderUse time connectives: at first, shortly after, within minutes
Paragraph 3 — EyewitnessA quotation from someone who was there: what they saw and feltQuotation + reporting verb + name, age or role
Paragraph 4 — Official / expertA quotation from a teacher, head teacher, coach, doctor or police officerExplains why or how it happened, or praises people
Paragraph 5 — ConclusionHow the story ended, or what will happen nextLook to the future. End with a calm, clear sentence

Always cover the task bullets. If the task says 'include what happened, how people reacted and what will happen next', give each bullet its own paragraph. A report that misses a bullet cannot reach the top band in Creation of texts.

The 5 Ws plan (2 minutes)
  • Who? the people involved (give names, ages or roles)
  • What? the event in one sentence
  • When? day, date or time
  • Where? the exact place
  • Why / How? the reason, or how it happened
  • Then add: 1 eyewitness quotation · 1 official quotation · 1 number or fact · 1 'what happens next'.

How to start: lead paragraphs you can adapt

  • The big fact first: More than three hundred pupils cheered on Thursday afternoon when Nile Star School won the city football cup for the first time.
  • An ordinary day that changed: What began as a normal school day on Monday quickly became one to remember when a family of ducks marched into the playground at Green Hills School.
  • The community is talking: In an event that has left the whole school talking, Year 6 pupils raised a record 15,000 pounds at their charity fair last Saturday.
  • Numbers that surprise: Five hundred books, two hundred volunteers and one very busy weekend: that is the story of the new library at Sunrise Primary School, which opened yesterday.
  • Short and dramatic: A sudden storm brought Sports Day at Oasis International School to an early end yesterday morning, but nobody was hurt.

How to end: closing paragraphs you can adapt

  • What happens next: The school has announced that the competition will become a yearly event, and pupils are already planning their next designs.
  • The result: By the end of the afternoon, the playground was clean again and the ducks had been safely returned to the nearby lake.
  • A quotation that closes: “We will never forget this day,” said one proud parent as the families left the hall.
  • Still developing: The cause of the fire is still being investigated, and the school will update parents later this week.
  • Looking back: It was a day that the whole community will remember for a long time.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Opening the report (the 5 Ws)

  • On [day], [who] [did what] at [where].
  • Residents of [place] woke up to the news that…
  • What began as an ordinary [day/event] quickly became…
  • In an event that has left the whole school talking, …

Developing the story

  • According to those who were there, …
  • Shortly after [time], …
  • Within minutes, …
  • Officials confirmed that…
  • The event, which lasted [time], …
  • Nobody was hurt, but…

Introducing quotations

  • “…,” explained [name], who saw everything.
  • “…,” added [name], aged [age].
  • Head teacher [name] praised the pupils: “…”
  • One parent described the moment as “…”

Background and effects

  • This is not the first time…
  • Experts believe that…
  • As a result, …
  • The event raised / caused / attracted…

Ending the report

  • The school has announced that…
  • Organisers hope to…
  • It is expected that…
  • The event will be remembered for a long time.
Word / phraseMeaning / use
announcedtold everyone officially: The head teacher announced the winners.
confirmedsaid that something is definitely true: Police confirmed that nobody was injured.
eyewitnessa person who saw the event happen
according toshows where information comes from: According to the coach, …
organisedplanned and arranged an event
incidentan event, often a surprising or unpleasant one
immediatelyat once, with no delay
delightedvery pleased
disappointedsad because something did not happen as hoped
postponedmoved to a later time: The match was postponed until Friday.
evacuatedmoved people away from a dangerous place to safety
spectatorspeople who watch an event
restoredbrought back to normal: Power was restored an hour later.
successfulachieving what was planned
reportedlyaccording to what people say (not yet proved)
Reporting verbs instead of 'said'

explained · added · announced · admitted · praised · warned · described · confirmed · replied · laughed. Choose a verb that fits the speaker: a head teacher praises, a nervous winner admits, a police officer warns.

5. Strong sample answers

Here are two strong reports: one for a school newspaper about a school event, and one for a local newspaper about an event in the town. Read the notes to see where each mark is earned.

Sample 1 — School newspaper: a concert in the dark300 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Last week something unexpected happened at a school event. Write a report for your school newspaper. In your report you should include: • what happened and when • how pupils, teachers and parents reacted • what will happen next.

The Show Goes On in the Dark

By Laila Hassan, Year 6 Reporter

More than two hundred parents cheered in the dark on Tuesday evening when a power cut hit the Year 6 spring concert at Green Valley International School in Sheikh Zayed. Instead of stopping the show, the performers carried on by torchlight.

The concert began at six o'clock in the school hall. The choir had just finished its first song when, at about twenty past six, every light in the building went out. For a few seconds, the hall was completely silent. Then a small child in the front row began to cry, and some parents stood up, unsure what to do.

Music teacher Mr Karim Adel acted quickly. He asked the audience to switch on the torches on their phones and point them towards the stage. Within a minute, hundreds of tiny lights were shining like stars, and the pianist, eleven-year-old Youssef Samir, started to play again from memory.

“I was really nervous at first because I couldn't see the keys,” admitted Youssef after the show. “When I heard everyone clapping along, I forgot about the dark completely.”

Head teacher Mrs Nadia Fawzy praised the pupils for staying calm. “Our students showed real courage tonight,” she said. “They turned a problem into something magical.”

According to the electricity company, the power cut was caused by a damaged cable near the school. Power was restored at a quarter past seven, just as the choir was singing the school song — the last item of the evening.

The school has announced that the concert will be performed again next month, so that families who missed parts of it can enjoy the whole show. Many parents, however, are already calling Tuesday's performance the most memorable concert the school has ever held.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation 5/5: all three bullets are covered: what happened and when (paragraphs 1–2), reactions of pupils, teachers and parents (paragraphs 2–5), what happens next (last paragraph). The reporter never says 'I think'; opinions appear only in quotations or are given to 'many parents'.
  • Vocabulary 3/3: news vocabulary (announced, restored, according to, performers, audience) and varied reporting verbs (admitted, praised, said). A simile adds colour without becoming a story: 'shining like stars'.
  • Structure 7/7: a short, clear headline with a play on 'the show must go on', byline, a lead paragraph with the 5 Ws (who: parents and performers · what: a power cut, but the show continued · when: Tuesday evening · where: Green Valley School), events in time order with time connectives (at about twenty past six, within a minute), eyewitness quotation, official quotation, cause, and a 'what next' ending.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7: past tense used accurately, present perfect where needed (has announced, has ever held), passive voice (was caused, was restored, will be performed), complex sentences with 'when', 'because' and 'so that', speech punctuated correctly with the comma inside the closing speech mark, and a single dash for emphasis on the final item.
  • Spelling 3/3: ambitious words spelt correctly: electricity, performers, completely, memorable, courage, announced.
Sample 2 — Local newspaper: young volunteers clean the river bank266 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Last weekend, children from your area took part in a big clean-up event. Write a news report for your local newspaper. Include: • what happened, who took part and where • what people said about the event • what will happen next.

Young Volunteers Make the Nile Shine

By Omar Nabil, Junior Reporter

Over one hundred children, some as young as eight, collected more than two thousand plastic bottles from the bank of the River Nile in Maadi last Saturday. The clean-up was organised by the Green Hands Club to celebrate World Environment Day.

The volunteers met at the Corniche at eight o'clock in the morning. Each team was given gloves, rubbish bags and a section of the river bank to clean. By midday, the teams had filled ninety bags with bottles, cans and old fishing nets. Every bag was weighed and sorted before it was taken to a local recycling centre.

Eleven-year-old Mariam Tarek, who joined with her two brothers, was shocked by what she found. “We even pulled out an old bicycle wheel!” she exclaimed. “I didn't know so much rubbish was hiding in the grass.”

Club leader Dr Hesham Ali explained why the work is so important. “Plastic in the river harms fish and birds, and it ends up in the sea,” he said. “These children have shown that small hands can make a big difference.”

Local shopkeepers also supported the event. A nearby bakery provided free breakfast for every volunteer, and a juice shop donated cold drinks during the hot afternoon.

The Green Hands Club has announced that it will hold a clean-up on the first Saturday of every month. Families who would like to take part can register at the club's office in Road 9. The next event, which will take place in the Maadi Garden, is expected to attract even more volunteers.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation 5/5: every bullet is covered: what, who and where (paragraphs 1–2), what people said (paragraphs 3–4), what next (last paragraph). Numbers (one hundred, two thousand, ninety bags) make the report believable, and the tone stays factual throughout.
  • Vocabulary 3/3: precise words (volunteers, organised, recycling centre, donated, register) and varied reporting verbs (exclaimed, explained, said).
  • Structure 7/7: headline, byline, lead paragraph with Who/What/When/Where/Why in two sentences, events in time order (at eight o'clock, by midday), eyewitness, expert, extra detail about the community, then the future. Paragraphs move from most to least important — the inverted pyramid.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7: passive voice (was organised, was given, was weighed, is expected), relative clauses (who joined with her two brothers / which will take place in the Maadi Garden), a pair of commas for extra information (some as young as eight) and an exclamation mark used correctly inside speech marks. Lists of items (gloves, rubbish bags and a section of the river bank) use commas correctly.
  • Spelling 3/3: volunteers, environment, recycling, celebrate, shopkeepers, attract — all correct.

MistakeWhy it costs marksFix
Starting like a story: 'It was a sunny morning and I woke up early…'Wrong text type — Creation and Structure can be cappedStart with the 5 Ws, not with the beginning of the day
Writing 'I think it was amazing' as the reporterBreaks the objective tonePut feelings inside quotations from other people
No headline or bylineLoses Structure marksWrite them first, before paragraph 1
Quotations with no speaker, or wrong punctuationLoses Grammar and punctuation marks“…,” said [name], [age/role]. Put the comma inside the speech marks
Using 'said' in every quotationWeak vocabularyUse explained, added, admitted, praised
Forgetting one of the task bulletsContent is not completeGive each bullet its own paragraph and tick it in your plan
Silly or impossible facts (a million spectators at a school match)The report is no longer believableKeep numbers realistic and the same all the way through
Mixing tenses: 'The bus arrives and the pupils got on'Grammar errorsPast tense for the events
The report just stopsNo clear endingFinish with the result or what happens next
Checklist before you finish
  • Headline (short, catchy) and byline.
  • Lead paragraph answers Who, What, When, Where (and Why) in 1–2 sentences.
  • Third person and fair — no 'I think' from the reporter.
  • Two quotations (eyewitness + official), correctly punctuated, with different reporting verbs.
  • Realistic names, times and numbers.
  • At least two passive verbs and a mix of short and long sentences.
  • Every bullet in the task has its own paragraph.
  • A final paragraph about the result or what happens next.

Practice tasks

  1. Write a report for your school newspaper about a visit from a famous author to your school. Include: • what happened during the visit • what pupils and teachers said • what will happen next. (Plan only.)

    Show answer
    Headline: Story Star Visits Sunrise School. Byline: By [name], Year 6 Reporter. P1 lead: On Wednesday morning, children's author [invented name] spent three hours with 150 Year 6 pupils in the school library, talking about her new adventure book. P2: events in order — reading aloud, question time, writing workshop, book signing. P3: eyewitness quotation from a pupil (admitted / exclaimed). P4: quotation from the librarian or head teacher (praised / explained). P5 what next: the school announces a new writing competition, and the winners' stories will be printed in the school magazine.

  2. Turn this weak opening into a strong lead paragraph: 'It was Thursday. We went to the zoo. I was very excited. A monkey took my hat.'

    Show answer
    Example: 'Sixty Year 6 pupils from Palm Tree School had an unexpected surprise at Giza Zoo on Thursday morning when a cheeky monkey snatched a pupil's hat and refused to give it back.' It is third person, gives Who, What, When, Where in one sentence and starts with the most exciting fact.

  3. Rewrite as a correctly punctuated direct quotation with a better reporting verb: the coach said we trained every day and we deserved to win

    Show answer
    “We trained every day, and we deserved to win,” explained coach Samy Fouad. (Speech marks around the exact words, comma inside the closing speech mark, capital letter at the start of the spoken words, reporting verb + name and role.)

  4. Change these active sentences into the passive to make them sound more formal: (a) The firefighters rescued the cat. (b) The head teacher cancelled the trip.

    Show answer
    (a) The cat was rescued by the firefighters. (b) The trip was cancelled (by the head teacher). The passive puts the event first, which is useful in news reports.

  5. Write three headlines for a report about a Year 6 class that won a national science competition. Use alliteration, rhyme and a question.

    Show answer
    Alliteration: Super Scientists Scoop Top Prize. Rhyme: Class Shines with Science Designs. Question: Who Built the Best Robot? (Keep each one short and capitalise the main words.)

  6. Find two problems in this sentence from a news report and fix them: 'I think the match was brilliant and the crowd goes wild when Ahmed scored.'

    Show answer
    Problem 1: 'I think … brilliant' is the reporter's opinion. Problem 2: the tense changes from past to present (goes). Fix: 'The crowd went wild when Ahmed scored the winning goal. “It was brilliant,” said one fan.'

  7. Write a report for your local newspaper about a new park that opened in your area. Include what the park has, what visitors said and what is planned for the future. (Plan only.)

    Show answer
    Headline: New Park Brings Green Joy to [area]. Lead: The new [name] Park opened its gates to hundreds of families on Friday afternoon in [area]. P2: what it has — playground, cycle path, 300 trees, a small lake (numbers). P3: eyewitness quotation from a child or parent. P4: official quotation from the park manager about why it was built. P5 future: a café and an outdoor library will be added next year, plus the opening times. Keep third person and past tense.
