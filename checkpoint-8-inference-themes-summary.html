Checkpoint 8 · Reading: understanding texts

Inference, Main Ideas, Themes and Summary

Explicit and implicit information, a step-by-step inference method with STEAL, main ideas and themes, the 40-50-word summary with a model answer, and table-completion questions.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Most reading marks in Checkpoint 8 come from understanding what a text says and what it suggests. This page covers four connected skills: finding explicit information, making inferences (reading between the lines), working out main ideas and themes, and writing the short summary (40-50 words) and table-completion answers. Each skill has a step-by-step method, worked examples and practice.

How these skills are tested
  • Retrieval (1 mark): “Give one reason why…”, “Which country did the writer visit first?”, “Find a phrase that shows…”.
  • Inference: “What does this suggest about how Laila feels?”, “What impression do we get of the grandfather?”, “Why do you think the boy hid the letter?”
  • Own words + quotation (up to 4 marks): “Explain, in your own words, why the village changed. Support your answer with a quotation.”
  • Main ideas: “Match each paragraph to its main idea” (one idea in the list is a distractor that matches no paragraph).
  • Table completion (2-4 marks): retrieve facts and organise them into a table.
  • Summary (2 marks): “Summarise… in 40-50 words”, sometimes from notes rather than from a full text.

1. Explicit and implicit information

Explicit informationImplicit information
MeaningStated directly in the textSuggested but not stated: you must infer it
Your jobFind it and copy or report it accuratelyWork it out from clues and explain your reasoning
Question wordsfind, give, state, name, list, which, when, wheresuggest, imply, impression, why do you think, what does this tell us
Example text“The museum closes at 5 p.m. on Fridays.”“When he opened the envelope, his face went pale and he sat down slowly.”
AnswerIt closes at 5 p.m. on Fridays.He has received bad or shocking news. (Clues: “face went pale”, “sat down slowly”.)
Retrieval rules (explicit questions)
  • Go to the exact paragraph or lines named in the question. An answer from the wrong place scores 0 even if it is true.
  • Give exactly what is asked: “one word” means one word; “two reasons” means two separate reasons.
  • Keep it short. A precise phrase beats a long copied sentence that hides the answer.
  • If the question says “in your own words”, do not copy. Change the key words into your own (see the summary section for how).

2. Inference: reading between the lines

An inference is a sensible conclusion based on evidence. You are a detective: the writer gives clues, and you work out what they mean. A good inference is not a guess — you must be able to point to the words that led you to it.

The inference method: Clue → Meaning → Because
  • 1. Find the clue: a word, action, piece of speech or detail in the lines named.
  • 2. Ask what it suggests: what feeling, relationship, reason or situation would explain this clue?
  • 3. Write it: “This suggests that… because the writer says ‘…’.” Point + evidence + explanation.
  • 4. Check: does anything else in the text contradict your idea? If yes, change it.

Inferring character with STEAL. When a question asks for an impression of a character, look at five kinds of clues. These are the same tools used to build characters in realistic fiction.

STEALLook at…Example clueInference
S — SpeechWhat the character says and how“Fine,” she snapped, without looking up.She is annoyed and does not want to talk.
T — ThoughtsWhat they think or wonderHe wondered whether anyone would notice if he left.He feels unimportant or lonely.
E — Effect on othersHow other people react to themThe younger children went quiet when Karim walked in.Karim is respected or feared.
A — ActionsWhat they doShe quietly put half her sandwich on her friend’s plate.She is kind and generous.
L — LooksAppearance, clothes, body languageHis shirt was ironed, his shoes shining.He is careful, proud or trying to impress.

Use precise feeling words like these. “Happy” and “sad” are acceptable, but a precise word shows deeper understanding.

Worked example. Text: “Grandad’s hands shook as he unwrapped the old photograph. He touched the face of the young soldier in it, then turned towards the window and said nothing for a long time.” Question: What does this suggest about Grandad’s feelings? Answer: It suggests he is moved and perhaps sad, remembering someone from his past. His hands “shook”, which shows strong emotion, and he “said nothing for a long time”, which suggests he is lost in painful memories.

3. Explain in your own words + a quotation

Some questions (up to 4 marks) ask you to explain in your own words and also to support with a quotation. These are two different jobs: the explanation must be your own language, and the quotation must be copied exactly in inverted commas.

Example. Text: “Rami had trained every morning before school for a year, running along the canal in the dark while his friends were still asleep.” Question: Explain in your own words what this tells us about Rami. Support your answer with a quotation. Answer: Rami is extremely dedicated and is willing to make sacrifices to improve. He gives up sleep and comfort to practise, as shown by “running along the canal in the dark while his friends were still asleep”.

Quoting rules
  • Copy the words exactly, including spelling, inside inverted commas.
  • Keep it short: the few words that prove the point, not a whole paragraph.
  • Never let a quotation replace your explanation. Quotation + “This shows…”.
  • Embed quotations in your sentence when you can: He is “running along the canal in the dark”, which shows…

4. Main ideas

The main idea of a paragraph is what the whole paragraph is mostly about, in one short statement. It is narrower and more concrete than a theme. Often the first sentence (the topic sentence) states it, and the other sentences give details, examples or evidence.

  1. Read the paragraph and cover the details (numbers, names, examples).
  2. Ask: what do all the sentences have in common?
  3. Say it in 5-10 words: “The problems caused by the rising salt in the lake.”
  4. Check it is not too narrow (one detail only) or too wide (could describe the whole text).

Matching paragraphs to main ideas. You may get a list of main ideas and be asked to match each paragraph. There is usually one extra idea that matches no paragraph. Match the easiest paragraphs first, then use elimination. Beware of an idea that repeats a word from a paragraph but describes only a small detail of it.

Worked example. Paragraph: “The school garden began as a small corner of weeds. Year 7 students cleared it, planted tomatoes and beans, and built a simple watering system from old bottles. By June, the canteen was serving salad grown just metres away.” Which is the main idea? (a) how to build a watering system (b) how students turned waste ground into a productive garden (c) why the canteen serves salad. Answer: (b). Option (a) is only one detail and (c) is only the final result.

5. Themes

A theme is a big idea that runs through a whole text and could apply to many other stories. You can express it as a topic (one word or phrase) or, for a deeper answer, as a message (a full sentence about life).

Theme as a topicTheme as a message
FriendshipTrue friends support each other even when it costs them something.
CourageCourage means acting even when you are afraid.
FamilyFamily ties can survive distance and disagreement.
AmbitionAmbition can bring success, but it can also make people lose what matters most.
Change / growing upGrowing up often means seeing people and places differently.
LossMemories keep people we have lost close to us.
Nature vs humansHuman carelessness can damage nature, and nature’s loss harms people too.
Identity / belongingPeople feel at home where they are accepted, not only where they were born.
PerseveranceHard work and patience can make a dream come true.
Justice / fairnessStanding up for what is right can be difficult but worthwhile.

How to find a theme: look at what the main character learns or how they change, what problem keeps returning, and what the ending suggests. Then name the topic and, if you can, turn it into a message.

Main ideaTheme
ScopeOne paragraph or sectionThe whole text
TypeConcrete, about this textAbstract, about life in general
Example (story of a girl who saves for a year to buy her mother a gift)Paragraph 2: Mona takes a job sorting books at the library.Love and sacrifice: people we love are worth working hard for.

6. The summary task (40-50 words)

The summary question asks you to give the main points about one focus (for example, “why the oasis attracts visitors” or “the problems the oasis faces”) in a fixed number of words, usually 40-50. It tests two skills at once: selecting the important points and writing concisely in your own words. Sometimes you are given notes instead of a full text, and you turn the notes into a short paragraph.

Summary method: 6 steps
  • 1. Underline the focus in the question. Only points about that focus count.
  • 2. Highlight the relevant points in the text. Usually 4-6 points. Ignore examples, quotations, descriptions and the writer’s opinions unless the question asks for them.
  • 3. Write each point as a short note in your own words (2-6 words each).
  • 4. Join the notes into full sentences with linking words (and, also, as well as, because, however, as a result).
  • 5. Count every word. Aim for about 45 so you are safely inside the limit.
  • 6. Check: full sentences, no quotations, no “I think”, no introduction such as “This text is about…”.
Too many words? Cut by…Too few words? Add by…
Removing examples and numbers that are not neededAdding a missing main point you skipped
Removing adjectives and adverbs that add no meaningAdding a short reason with “because” or “so”
Combining points into a list: “pottery, fossils and the lake”Making a vague word more precise: “problems” → “rising salt levels”
Replacing a phrase with one word: “in spite of the fact that” → “although”Joining two points with a connective that shows a link

Changing words into your own (paraphrasing). Use synonyms (“famous” → “well known”), change the word class (“the number of fish has been reduced” → “fewer fish”), or change the sentence pattern. You may keep words that have no simple synonym, such as names, “fossils” or “pottery”.

Source text for the model below (original): Fifty years ago, the Fayoum Oasis was famous for one thing: its lake. Today, Lake Qarun is still there, but visitors come for much more. In the desert to the west lies Wadi El Hitan, the Valley of the Whales, where the fossil skeletons of ancient whales lie in the sand. Millions of years ago, this dry valley was the bottom of a sea, and scientists still travel from across the world to study the bones. Closer to the villages, the village of Tunis has become known for its pottery. Families who once farmed now run small workshops, and children learn to shape clay almost as soon as they can walk. However, the oasis faces problems. The lake is becoming saltier every year, which has reduced the number of fish, and some fishermen have had to leave for jobs in Cairo. Local groups are now teaching visitors how to protect the shoreline.

Model summary (40-50 words)46 words · 2/2 (all main points, own words, inside 40-50 words)
Task: Summarise the reasons why visitors come to the Fayoum Oasis and the problems the oasis faces. Write 40-50 words.

Visitors are drawn to the oasis by its lake, ancient whale fossils in a former seabed, and the pottery made by local families. However, rising salt in the lake has reduced fish, forcing some fishermen to leave, so locals now teach visitors to protect the shore.

Why this gets the marks
  • Covers both parts of the focus: reasons to visit (three points) and problems (salt → fewer fish → fishermen leave) plus the response.
  • Own words: “drawn to” for “come for”, “former seabed” for “the bottom of a sea”, “forcing… to leave” for “have had to leave”.
  • Leaves out details that are not main points: “fifty years ago”, “scientists from across the world”, “children learn to shape clay”, the names Wadi El Hitan and Tunis.
  • Joined with connectives (“However”, “so”) into full sentences, not notes or bullet points.
  • Word count checked and inside the 40-50 limit.

Summary from notes. If the question gives you notes, such as “cycle lane — 5 km — opened May / fewer cars near schools / some shop owners unhappy: deliveries harder”, turn them into connected sentences: “A new five-kilometre cycle lane opened in May. It has reduced traffic near schools, although some shop owners are unhappy because deliveries have become more difficult.” Do not simply copy the notes with dashes.

  • اكتب بكلامك أنت (own words)، ومن غير اقتباسات، ومن غير أمثلة وأرقام مش مهمة.
  • اربط النقط بـ and, also, however, because، واكتب جمل كاملة مش نقط.

7. Table-completion questions

These questions (2-4 marks) give you a table with headings and ask you to fill the empty boxes from the text. They test careful retrieval and organisation.

  1. Read the headings first: they tell you exactly what to look for (place, reason, problem, solution…).
  2. Check if one row is already completed as an example. Copy its style and length.
  3. Scan the text for each item. Usually the information appears in the text in the same order as the rows.
  4. Write short answers (a phrase, not a sentence). Do not repeat the example row.
  5. Check that each box answers its own column heading.

Worked example, using the Fayoum text above.

8. Common mistakes

  • Answering from the wrong paragraph or lines.
  • Copying a whole sentence when “in your own words” is required.
  • Inferences with no evidence (“He is sad.” — why?).
  • Choosing a detail as the main idea of a paragraph.
  • Summary: including examples, quotations or opinions · going over or under the word range · writing notes instead of sentences.
  • Table: writing long sentences in the boxes, or repeating the example row.

9. Practice questions

Use this short original text for questions 1-8.

“The bell had rung ten minutes ago, but Hana was still standing at the gate. She watched the other girls run towards the school building in pairs and groups, laughing. Her new uniform felt stiff. She checked the timetable in her hand for the fourth time, folded it into a tiny square, and took one step forward. Then another. A tall girl with a basketball under her arm stopped beside her. ‘You must be the new girl,’ she said. ‘I’m Salma. Come on — I’ll show you where 8B is.’ For the first time that morning, Hana smiled.”

  1. 1. How long ago had the bell rung? (explicit)

    Show answer
    Ten minutes ago.

  2. 2. What does “She checked the timetable in her hand for the fourth time” suggest about Hana?

    Show answer
    She is nervous and anxious. Checking it again and again shows she is worried about getting lost or being late, not that she has forgotten it.

  3. 3. Why do you think the writer mentions the other girls running “in pairs and groups, laughing”?

    Show answer
    To contrast them with Hana, who is alone. It suggests she feels lonely and left out because everyone else already has friends.

  4. 4. What impression do you get of Salma? Use STEAL and a quotation.

    Show answer
    She is friendly and confident. Her speech is welcoming (“Come on — I’ll show you where 8B is”) and her action of stopping beside a stranger shows she is kind.

  5. 5. How do Hana’s feelings change in the extract? Support with two quotations.

    Show answer
    At first she is nervous and isolated (“still standing at the gate”), but at the end she feels relieved and happier when someone welcomes her (“For the first time that morning, Hana smiled”).

  6. 6. What is the main idea of the extract in one sentence?

    Show answer
    A nervous new student is welcomed by a kind classmate on her first day.

  7. 7. Name a theme of the extract, as a topic and as a message.

    Show answer
    Topic: belonging / friendship. Message: a small act of kindness can make a stranger feel she belongs.

  8. 8. Explain in your own words why Hana takes “one step forward. Then another.” and support with a quotation.

    Show answer
    She is slowly finding the courage to go in, even though she is afraid. The very short fragment “Then another.” shows that each step needs a new effort.

  9. 9. Paragraph: “Plastic bags take hundreds of years to break down. In the sea, turtles mistake them for jellyfish and swallow them. On land, they block drains and cause floods.” Choose the main idea: (a) turtles eat jellyfish (b) the harm caused by plastic bags (c) how drains work.

    Show answer
    (b). Every sentence describes a type of harm. (a) and (c) are only small details.

  10. 10. Rewrite in your own words, 12 words or fewer: “The festival attracted an unprecedented number of visitors, far more than the organisers had anticipated.”

    Show answer
    Example: The festival drew a record crowd, much bigger than organisers expected. (11 words)

  11. 11. Turn these notes into a summary sentence: “beach clean-up — Saturday — 60 volunteers / 200 kg rubbish / mostly bottles”.

    Show answer
    Example: On Saturday, sixty volunteers cleared 200 kilograms of rubbish from the beach, most of it bottles.

  12. 12. A student’s summary begins: “This text is about a town and I think it is very interesting because…”. Give two problems.

    Show answer
    It wastes words on an introduction (“This text is about…”) and it includes a personal opinion (“I think”), which a summary must not include.

  13. 13. Explicit or implicit? “The shop’s shelves were almost empty, and a sign on the door read ‘Closing down — everything half price’.” Question: Is the shop doing well?

    Show answer
    Implicit: the text does not say it directly, but the empty shelves and “Closing down” sign suggest the shop is failing.

  14. 14. Fill the table from this text: “Rania, the eldest, cooks dinner. Omar, the youngest, feeds the cat. Their father does the shopping on Fridays.” Columns: Person | Job.

    Show answer
    Rania | cooks dinner. Omar | feeds the cat. Father | does the shopping (on Fridays).

  15. 15. Why is “He was sad” a weak answer to “What does the extract suggest about how he feels?”

    Show answer
    It gives no evidence or explanation, and “sad” is vague. A strong answer uses a precise word (e.g. “heartbroken”) + a quotation + “This suggests… because…”.
