Checkpoint 6 · Reading: understanding texts

Inference, Main Ideas and Themes

Explicit and implicit information, retrieving facts, inference with evidence, main ideas and themes, putting events in order and writing a short summary, with practice on two original texts.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

Many reading marks come from two simple questions: What does the text say? and What does the text suggest? This page teaches you how to find information that is stated (explicit), how to work out information that is hinted (implicit), how to find the main idea of a paragraph and the theme of a whole story, how to put events in order, and how to write a short summary. At the end there are two short practice texts with full answers.

How these skills are tested
  • Retrieval (usually 1 or 2 marks): “Give one reason why…”, “Where did the family go first?”, “Find two things the girl took with her.”
  • Inference: “What does this tell us about Laila?”, “Why do you think the boy hid the letter?” Some inference questions ask for a reason AND a quotation (often 2 marks).
  • Explain in your own words, or give two reasons each with a quotation (can be worth 4 marks).
  • Tick-one questions on feelings, on the meaning of a line, or on what a paragraph is mainly about.
  • Main idea and theme questions: “What is this paragraph mainly about?”, “What lesson does the story teach?”
  • Order and summary skills help you answer questions about how the text is organised and what happens.

1. Explicit and implicit information

Explicit informationImplicit information
MeaningIt is stated directly in the text. You can point to it.It is hinted. You must work it out from clues (read between the lines).
Your jobFind it and copy it or say it correctlyFind the clues, work out what they suggest, explain
Question wordsfind, give, name, list, which, when, where, how manysuggest, show, tell us about, why do you think, what impression
Example text“The shop opens at eight o’clock every morning.”“As soon as the teacher said ‘surprise test’, the whole class groaned.”
AnswerIt opens at eight o’clock.The pupils are unhappy about the test (they groaned).

2. Retrieving information (explicit questions)

  1. Find the key word in the question (for example “first”, “two things”, “why”).
  2. Go to the right lines if the question gives line numbers or a paragraph. Do not search the whole text.
  3. Scan for the key word or a word with a similar meaning.
  4. Read the sentence before and after to be sure you have the full answer.
  5. Answer only what is asked. If it says “give two”, give exactly two different things.
  6. Copy names, numbers and spellings carefully. Short answers are fine, full sentences are not always needed.

Worked example. “On Saturday morning the Year 6 pupils planted twelve lemon trees in the school garden. Mr Fathy, the science teacher, brought the spades, and the pupils carried water from the tap near the gate.” Question: Who brought the spades? (1 mark) Answer: Mr Fathy (the science teacher). Question: Give two jobs the pupils did. (2 marks) Answer: They planted lemon trees. They carried water.

Retrieval traps
  • Giving one idea twice in different words when the question asks for two different things.
  • Taking the answer from the wrong paragraph.
  • Adding your own ideas to a “find” question. If it is a retrieval question, the answer is in the text.

3. Inference: reading between the lines

To infer means to work something out from clues, like a detective. Writers do not always tell you everything. They give you clues and trust you to understand. An inference is not a guess: you must be able to point to the clue that proves it.

The inference method: Clue → Meaning → Because
  • Clue: find the words that hint at something (a quotation).
  • Meaning: say what the clue suggests (a feeling, a reason, a type of person, what will happen).
  • Because: explain how the clue shows this.
  • Example: “Sami’s hands were shaking” (clue) → he is frightened (meaning) → because shaking hands are a sign of fear (because).

Inferring about characters. Use the tools of characterisation to collect clues about a character: Speech (what they say and how), Thoughts (what they think), Effect on others (how other characters react to them), Actions (what they do), Looks (how they look, their face and body). Each one can be a clue.

Clue typeClue from a textWhat we can infer
Speech“Hurry up! I haven’t got all day!” snapped the shopkeeper.He is impatient and rude.
ThoughtsHana wondered if anyone would notice she was gone.She feels lonely or unimportant.
Effect on othersWhen Coach Waleed walked in, the players stopped joking at once.The players respect him (or are a little afraid of him).
ActionsHe counted his coins twice and put the expensive juice back.He does not have much money.
LooksHer eyes were red and puffy.She has been crying, so she is upset.
SettingThe windows were broken and weeds grew through the steps.Nobody has lived in the house for a long time.
Word / phraseMeaning / use
suggestsThis suggests that he is nervous.
showsThe word “snapped” shows she is angry.
impliesThe writer implies that the town has changed.

Worked example 1. “Laila looked at the empty seat beside her and pushed her lunch away. It was the third day Noor had not come to school.” What can we infer about Laila? She misses Noor and is worried or sad. Evidence: she “pushed her lunch away”, which shows she has lost her appetite because she is upset.

Worked example 2. “Grandad’s farm had no electricity. In the evenings we read by the light of an oil lamp, and the only sound was the frogs by the canal.” What does this suggest about the farm? It is far from the city, quiet and old-fashioned. Evidence: “no electricity”, “read by the light of an oil lamp”, and the only sound is frogs, so there are no cars or crowds.

Two reasons, each with a quotation (up to 4 marks). Question: Why do you think Laila is worried? Give two reasons and a quotation for each. Answer: First, her friend is absent: “the third day Noor had not come to school”. Second, she cannot eat: she “pushed her lunch away”. Each reason is different and each has its own short quotation.

4. Explain in your own words

When a question says in your own words, you must not just copy the text. Show you understand by changing the words. You can keep names and small words, but change the key words.

Example. Text: “The villagers were terrified that the river would burst its banks.” Copied (weak): The villagers were terrified that the river would burst its banks. Own words (strong): The people in the village were very frightened that the river would flood over its sides.

5. Main ideas

The main idea is what a paragraph or a text is mostly about. It is close to the surface of the text: you can find it by looking at what most of the sentences are about. The first sentence of a non-fiction paragraph (the topic sentence) often gives it.

  1. Read the whole paragraph.
  2. Ask: what is this paragraph mostly about? Which word or idea comes up again and again?
  3. Ignore small details (one example, one number).
  4. Say it in one short sentence or a short heading.

Worked example. “Dates are a very healthy food. They contain lots of fibre, which helps digestion. They also give quick energy, so many people eat them when they break their fast. Dates are rich in vitamins and minerals too.” Main idea: Dates are good for your health. (Not “people eat dates when they break their fast”, which is only one detail.)

6. Themes

A theme is the big idea that runs through the whole story. It can be said in two ways: as a topic (one word or a short phrase, such as friendship, courage, honesty) or as a message (a full sentence about life, such as “True friends help each other in hard times”). A moral in a fable is one kind of theme message.

Main ideaTheme
How much of the text?One paragraph, one section or the whole text’s contentThe whole story
What is it?What happens or what it is aboutThe big idea or lesson about life
How specific?Specific to this text (names, events)General: it could apply to many stories
Example (story of a girl who practises the piano every day and wins a competition)Nada practises hard and wins the competition.Topic: hard work. Message: Hard work and patience lead to success.
Common theme (topic)Possible messageThe kind of story clue that shows it
FriendshipA true friend stays loyal when things are difficult.A character helps a friend even when it costs them something
CourageBeing brave means acting even when you are afraid.A frightened character still does the right thing
HonestyTelling the truth is better than hiding a mistake.A lie causes trouble, then the truth makes things better
FamilyFamily members support each other.A family works together to solve a problem
KindnessSmall acts of kindness can change someone’s life.A small kind action has a big result
Perseverance / not giving upIf you keep trying, you can succeed.A character fails many times before succeeding
GreedWanting too much can make you lose what you have.A greedy character ends up with nothing
PrideBeing too proud can lead to a fall.A boastful character loses (like the hare in the race)
Belonging / fitting inEveryone wants to be accepted, and differences can be a strength.A new pupil or an outsider finally finds a place
Change / growing upGrowing up means learning from your mistakes.A character is different at the end from the start
  1. Look at the problem: what goes wrong in the story?
  2. Look at the change: how is the main character different at the end?
  3. Ask what the character learns, or what the reader learns.
  4. Turn it into a general sentence with no names: not “Kareem learns to keep trying” but “We should not give up after one failure”.

7. Sequencing: putting events in order

Some questions ask you to put events in the order they happen (for example, by numbering them 1 to 5). Use these clues: time connectives (first, then, later, after that, finally, the next morning), paragraph order, and tense (had + past participle often shows something that happened earlier, as in “she had already left”).

Flashbacks

A story can jump back in time. “As he stood at the door, Kareem remembered the day he had first arrived.” The arrival happened FIRST, even though it is mentioned later in the text. Always order events by when they happened, not by where they appear on the page.

8. Writing a short summary

A summary gives only the most important points of a text, in a few short sentences and in your own words. It leaves out details, examples, description and speech.

  1. Read the whole text and find the main idea of each paragraph.
  2. Note the key events or points (who, what, why, how it ends).
  3. Cross out details, examples, description and dialogue.
  4. Write the points in order, in your own words, using linking words (but, so, then).
  5. Check you have not added your own opinion.

Short story to summarise: Kareem’s football team lost the school final by one goal. He had missed the last shot, and on the bus home he would not speak to anyone. That evening his grandfather sat beside him and showed him a video of the match. “Look,” he said, “you scored two goals before that miss.” Kareem watched quietly. The next morning he was the first one at practice, and he no longer felt ashamed.

Model summary34 words · Strong answer: every key point, in order, in own words
Task: Summarise the story about Kareem in three sentences.

Kareem’s team lost the final, and he blamed himself for missing the last shot. His grandfather reminded him of the earlier goals he had scored. Kareem decided to keep practising and stopped feeling ashamed.

Why this gets the marks
  • Sentence 1 → the problem (lost the final, missed the last shot) and Kareem’s feeling.
  • Sentence 2 → the turning point: the grandfather’s help, without copying his speech.
  • Sentence 3 → the ending and the change in Kareem.
  • Left out on purpose: the bus, the video details, the exact words of the grandfather.
  • Own words: “blamed himself” sums up his silence on the bus, and “stopped feeling ashamed” rewords “no longer felt ashamed”.

9. Practice on short texts

Text A (fiction). Yara had begged her parents for a kitten for two whole years. When at last they brought home a tiny grey cat, she named him Pepper and promised to feed him every day. For the first week she kept her promise. Then the school drama show began, and Yara stayed late every afternoon to practise. One evening she came home to find Pepper waiting by his empty bowl, crying softly. Her little brother Ziad was sitting next to him, holding the bag of cat food. “I gave him some yesterday,” Ziad said quietly, “but I didn’t know how much.” Yara’s face grew hot. She knelt down, filled the bowl and stroked Pepper’s soft back until he purred. Then she wrote a feeding timetable and stuck it on the fridge, with her own name at the top of every single day.

  1. A1. How long had Yara wanted a kitten? (explicit)

    Show answer
    For two whole years.

  2. A2. What made Yara stop feeding Pepper every day? (retrieval)

    Show answer
    Because the school drama show began and she stayed late every afternoon to practise.

  3. A3. “Yara’s face grew hot.” What does this suggest about how she feels?

    Show answer
    She feels guilty / ashamed. Her face growing hot shows embarrassment because she realises she broke her promise and her little brother had to look after the cat.

  4. A4. What impression do we get of Ziad? Give a reason and a quotation.

    Show answer
    He is kind and caring: he tried to feed the cat even though it was not his job (“I gave him some yesterday”). Also accept: he is young / unsure (“I didn’t know how much”).

  5. A5. Put these events in order: (a) Yara writes a timetable (b) Pepper arrives (c) the drama show starts (d) Yara finds Pepper by his empty bowl.

    Show answer
    (b), (c), (d), (a).

  6. A6. Why do you think Yara put her own name at the top of every day?

    Show answer
    To show she is taking full responsibility again and will keep her promise from now on (and does not want Ziad to have to do it).

  7. A7. What is the theme of the story? Give it as a topic and as a message.

    Show answer
    Topic: responsibility / keeping promises. Message: when we promise to look after something, we must keep our promise even when we are busy.

  8. A8. Write a two-sentence summary of Text A.

    Show answer
    Yara got the kitten she wanted and promised to feed him, but she forgot when she became busy with the school show. When she saw her hungry cat and her worried brother, she felt guilty and made a timetable to make sure she fed him every day.

Text B (non-fiction). Every spring, our school holds a Book Swap Day. The idea is simple: pupils bring books they have already read and swap them for books they have not. Last year more than 400 books changed hands in a single afternoon. Mrs Dina, the librarian, says the event is popular because it costs nothing. “Some families cannot buy many new books,” she explains, “but everyone can bring one old book and leave with a new adventure.” The swap also helps the environment, because books are used again instead of being thrown away. Any books left at the end are given to a children’s hospital nearby. In my opinion, it is the best afternoon of the whole school year. This year, the Student Council hopes to beat last year’s record, so start looking on your shelves now!

  1. B1. How many books were swapped last year? (explicit)

    Show answer
    More than 400.

  2. B2. Give two reasons why Book Swap Day is a good idea, according to the text.

    Show answer
    Any two: it costs nothing, so all families can join. It helps the environment because books are used again. Leftover books go to a children’s hospital.

  3. B3. What is the main idea of the text?

    Show answer
    The school’s Book Swap Day is a free and useful event that lets pupils exchange books.

  4. B4. Why has the writer included the words of Mrs Dina?

    Show answer
    To show the information comes from a real person who knows about the event (the librarian), which makes it reliable, and to explain why the event matters to families.

  5. B5. Find one fact and one opinion in the text.

    Show answer
    Fact: last year more than 400 books changed hands / leftover books are given to a children’s hospital. Opinion: “it is the best afternoon of the whole school year” (it shows what the writer thinks, and others could disagree).

  6. B6. What does the last sentence suggest about the purpose of the text?

    Show answer
    That it also wants to persuade pupils to take part: the imperative “start looking on your shelves now!” speaks directly to the reader.

  7. B7. Explain in your own words why some families like the event.

    Show answer
    Because some families do not have enough money to buy lots of new books, but at Book Swap Day children can get a different book for free.
الاستنتاج (inference) ليس تخمينًا

كل إجابة استنتاج يجب أن يكون معها دليل من النص. اكتب: الإجابة + because + الكلمات الموجودة في النص. مثال: She feels guilty because “her face grew hot”.

10. Common mistakes

  • Answering an inference question with a fact copied from the text and no explanation.
  • Guessing with no evidence (“Maybe she has a headache”) when the text gives a clearer clue.
  • Confusing the main idea (what happens) with the theme (the lesson about life).
  • Writing a theme with names in it. A theme is general.
  • Putting a flashback event in the wrong place when sequencing.
  • Writing a summary that is too long, copies whole sentences or adds your own opinion.
