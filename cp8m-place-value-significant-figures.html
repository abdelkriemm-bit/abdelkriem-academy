Cambridge Lower Secondary · Mathematics 0862 · Stage 8 · Number

Multiplying and Dividing by 0.1 and 0.01, and Significant Figures

Use place value to multiply and divide by 0.1 and 0.01, round any number to a given number of significant figures, and estimate answers by rounding to 1 significant figure.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 › Checkpoint 8 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Use place value to multiply and divide whole numbers and decimals by 0.1 and 0.01, for example 46 × 0.01 = 0.46 and 3.2 ÷ 0.1 = 32 (8Np.01)
  • Round numbers to a given number of significant figures, for example 34 567 to 2 significant figures is 35 000 (8Np.02)

You already know how to multiply and divide by 10, 100 and 1000 by moving digits across the place-value columns. In Stage 8 you use the same idea for 0.1 and 0.01, and you learn a new way of rounding — to significant figures — which works for very big and very small numbers. Both skills are used all the time for estimating answers on the non-calculator paper and checking calculator answers.

1. Multiplying by 0.1 and 0.01

0.1 is the same as 1/10. Finding 1/10 of a number means dividing it by 10. So:

  • × 0.1 is the same as ÷ 10 (digits move 1 place to the right)
  • × 0.01 is the same as ÷ 100 (0.01 = 1/100; digits move 2 places to the right)

Think about money: 0.1 of $35.20 is one tenth of it, $3.52 — less than you started with. Multiplying a positive number by 0.1 or 0.01 makes it smaller.

2. Dividing by 0.1 and 0.01

Ask: "How many tenths are there in 1?" The answer is 10. So 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10, and 5 ÷ 0.1 = 50. In the same way there are 100 hundredths in 1, so 1 ÷ 0.01 = 100.

  • ÷ 0.1 is the same as × 10 (digits move 1 place to the left)
  • ÷ 0.01 is the same as × 100 (digits move 2 places to the left)

Dividing a positive number by 0.1 or 0.01 makes it bigger. This surprises many students, so always do a sense check: "Is my answer bigger or smaller than the number I started with?"

Two steps. 0.4 × 0.1 ÷ 0.01: first × 0.1 gives 0.04, then ÷ 0.01 gives 4. (Overall: ÷ 10 then × 100 is the same as × 10.)

Units. 1 cm = 0.01 m, so 250 cm = 250 × 0.01 m = 2.5 m. 1 mm = 0.1 cm, so 37 mm = 37 × 0.1 cm = 3.7 cm.

3. What is a significant figure?

The first significant figure of a number is its first non-zero digit, reading from the left. It is the digit with the biggest place value, so it tells you most about the size of the number. Every digit after it is also significant (including zeros in between).

4. Rounding to significant figures

  1. Find the first significant figure (the first non-zero digit).
  2. Count along to the number of significant figures you need. Draw a line after the last one.
  3. Look at the next digit after the line. If it is 5 or more, round the last digit up. If it is 4 or less, leave it.
  4. Keep the size of the number. For a big number, fill the places up to the decimal point with zeros (34 567 → 35 000, not 35). For a small decimal, keep the zeros in front (0.004 528 → 0.0045). Do not write extra digits after the line.

Look at the last row. 2.996 to 3 s.f.: the digits 2.99 then a 6, so round up: 2.99 → 3.00. You must write 3.00 — the two zeros are the 2nd and 3rd significant figures. Writing just 3 would only be 1 s.f.

Significant figures or decimal places? 0.004 528 to 2 decimal places is 0.00 (useless!), but to 2 significant figures it is 0.0045. That is why scientists and engineers use significant figures for very small and very large numbers. Read the question carefully to see which one it asks for.

5. Estimating with significant figures

To estimate an answer, round every number to 1 significant figure, then work it out in your head. Combine this with the 0.1 and 0.01 facts:

  • 48.7 × 0.0312 ≈ 50 × 0.03 = 50 × 3 × 0.01 = 150 × 0.01 = 1.5 (calculator: 1.519…)
  • 4.13 × 289 ÷ 0.098 ≈ 4 × 300 ÷ 0.1 = 1200 ÷ 0.1 = 12 000 (calculator: 12 179.3…)
  • (39.2 + 61.4) ÷ 0.48 ≈ (40 + 60) ÷ 0.5 = 100 ÷ 0.5 = 200 (calculator: 209.5…)

Use an estimate to check a calculator answer. If your calculator says 151.9 for 48.7 × 0.0312, the estimate 1.5 shows that you pressed a wrong key.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Work out (a) 72 × 0.1 (b) 72 × 0.01 (c) 72 ÷ 0.1 (d) 0.72 ÷ 0.01 [4 marks]

  • (a) × 0.1 = ÷ 10: 7.2
  • (b) × 0.01 = ÷ 100: 0.72
  • (c) ÷ 0.1 = × 10: 720
  • (d) ÷ 0.01 = × 100: 72

Common mistake: answering 7.2 for part (c). Dividing by a number less than 1 makes the answer bigger.

Worked example 2 — Write the missing numbers. (a) 4.5 × ☐ = 0.045 (b) 0.6 ÷ ☐ = 60 [2 marks]

  • (a) 4.5 → 0.045: the digits moved 2 places right, which is ÷ 100. ÷ 100 is the same as × 0.01, so ☐ = 0.01.
  • (b) 0.6 → 60: the digits moved 2 places left, which is × 100. × 100 is the same as ÷ 0.01, so ☐ = 0.01.

Worked example 3 — Round 0.070 62 to 2 significant figures. [1 mark]

First significant figure: 7 (the zeros before it do not count). Second: 0. Next digit: 6, which is 5 or more, so round up: 0.070|62 → 0.071. Common mistake: writing 0.07 (that is 1 s.f.) or 0.0706 (3 s.f.).

Worked example 4 — Round 489 512 to (a) 1 significant figure (b) 2 significant figures (c) 3 significant figures. [3 marks]

  • (a) 4|89 512: next digit 8, round up → 500 000.
  • (b) 48|9 512: next digit 9, round up 48 → 49 → 490 000.
  • (c) 489|512: next digit 5, round up 489 → 490 → 490 000. (Yes, the same as part (b): here the 3rd significant figure is a 0.)

Common mistake: writing 49 or 490. Rounding must not change the size of the number — fill with zeros to keep the place value.

Worked example 5 — By rounding each number to 1 significant figure, estimate the value of (9.87 × 0.0412) ÷ 0.198. Show your working. [2 marks]

  1. Round: 9.87 ≈ 10, 0.0412 ≈ 0.04, 0.198 ≈ 0.2. (1 mark for all three seen.)
  2. 10 × 0.04 = 0.4.
  3. 0.4 ÷ 0.2 = 2 (how many 0.2s in 0.4?). Answer: ≈ 2. (Exact value: 2.05…)

Worked example 6 — A rectangular garden is 12.6 m long and 8.35 m wide. Work out its area. Give your answer correct to 3 significant figures. [2 marks]

Calculator: 12.6 × 8.35 = 105.21. To 3 s.f.: 105|.21, next digit 2 → round down → 105 m². Write the full calculator value first (105.21) — a method mark is often given for it even if the rounding goes wrong. Check with an estimate: 10 × 8 = 80 and 13 × 8 = 104, so 105 is sensible.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking × 0.1 makes the number bigger because you are "multiplying". For positive numbers, × 0.1 and × 0.01 make it smaller; ÷ 0.1 and ÷ 0.01 make it bigger.
  • Mixing up × 0.01 and × 0.1: 0.01 is a hundredth, so the digits move two places.
  • Counting leading zeros as significant: 0.0368 to 2 s.f. is 0.037, not 0.04.
  • Losing the size: 58 349 to 2 s.f. is 58 000, not 58.
  • Dropping needed zeros at the end of a decimal: 2.996 to 3 s.f. is 3.00, not 3.
  • Rounding in stages (0.0446 → 0.045 → 0.05). Always look only at the one digit after the cut: 0.0446 to 1 s.f. is 0.04.
  • Rounding to decimal places when the question says significant figures (or the other way round).
How Checkpoint asks this
  • "Work out" and "Complete the calculations" with 0.1 and 0.01 on the non-calculator paper — often several small parts worth 1 mark each.
  • Missing-number boxes: 3.2 × ☐ = 0.032. Decide how many places the digits moved and in which direction.
  • "Draw a ring around" the calculation that gives the same answer, e.g. 470 × 0.01 = 470 ÷ 100.
  • "Round … to 2 significant figures" — check your answer has exactly the right number of significant figures and the same size as the original.
  • "Estimate … Show your working" — you must show each number rounded (usually to 1 s.f.); an answer with no rounding shown may score 0.
  • Calculator questions that say "Give your answer to 3 significant figures": write the full display first, then round.

Practice

Remember
  • × 0.1 = ÷ 10 and × 0.01 = ÷ 100 (smaller). ÷ 0.1 = × 10 and ÷ 0.01 = × 100 (bigger).
  • Move the digits, not the decimal point, and always do a bigger-or-smaller check.
  • The first significant figure is the first non-zero digit; zeros in between count, zeros in front do not.
  • When rounding, look at the next digit only (5 or more → round up), and keep the size of the number with zeros.
  • Estimate by rounding every number to 1 significant figure, and show the rounded numbers.
