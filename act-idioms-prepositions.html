Idioms and Prepositions

The right small word after capable, interested, consist, prefer and dozens more — a grouped table of 50 ACT idioms, meaning-dependent prepositions and gerund vs infinitive, with a 10-question ACT-style quiz.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

An idiom is a fixed way of saying something. English decides that we are *interested in* something but *capable of* something, that we *decide to go* but *avoid going*. There is no logic rule that tells you which small word is right — native speakers just know it. For students who learned English as a second language, this makes idiom questions some of the hardest on the ACT. The good news: the same few dozen combinations appear again and again. This lesson gives you the method, a large grouped table to learn, and the gerund-vs-infinitive rules.

What this question type tests

Idiom questions are usage and word-choice questions. They belong to the ACT English categories Conventions of Standard English (38–43% of the 40 scored English questions) and Knowledge of Language (18–23%), which is about precise and appropriate wording. On the page they look like any other ACT English item: a short underlined portion and four choices, usually with NO CHANGE first, often with no stem.

You can recognise them with a vertical scan: the choices are almost identical and only one small word changes (of / for / in / to / with / on), or the verb form after a word changes (to design / designing / of designing).

The method

  1. Find the "anchor" word just before the preposition: the adjective, verb or noun that controls it (capable, consist, responsible, interest).
  2. Say each choice silently with the anchor word, ignoring the rest of the sentence: "capable of", "capable to", "capable for". The standard pair usually stands out.
  3. Check what follows the preposition. After a preposition, a verb must be in the -ing form: capable of designing, interested in learning, insist on paying.
  4. If two choices both sound fine, check the meaning. Some anchors take different prepositions for different meanings (agree with a person / agree to a plan).
  5. If you still cannot decide, eliminate any choice that is longer or adds words — idiom questions rarely reward extra words.

Safe ACT choices for a few disputed idioms: *different from* is always correct (avoid *different than* in ACT essays); *regard X as Y* (not *regard X to be Y*); *independent of*.

When the preposition changes the meaning

For some anchor words, more than one preposition is standard, but each one means something different. Here the method is meaning first.

AnchorPreposition → meaning✓ Example
agreewith a person / an opinion · to a proposal · on a decision both sides makeI agree with you. They agreed to the plan. We agreed on a date.
compareto = say one thing is like another · with = examine similarities and differences (careful writing keeps this difference)She compared the city to a beehive. We compared this year's results with last year's.
differfrom = be unlike · with = disagree with a personMy answer differs from yours. I differ with you on this point.
angrywith / at a person · about a situationangry with his brother about the noise
resultin = cause · from = be caused bySmoking results in illness. Illness results from smoking.

Gerund or infinitive?

A gerund is verb + -ing used as a noun (swimming, designing). An infinitive is *to* + verb (to swim, to design). Some verbs must be followed by one form, some by the other. The ACT tests the most common ones.

Verb + to + verb (infinitive)Verb + -ing (gerund)
decide, plan, hope, expect, want, intendenjoy, avoid, finish, keep, mind, quit
agree, refuse, promise, offer, threatenconsider, suggest, recommend, risk, imagine
manage, fail, attempt, try, tend, seemadmit, deny, delay, postpone, practise, miss
afford, deserve, learn, need, prepareappreciate, resist, discuss, involve, mention
✓ They decided to build a bridge. ✗ decided building✓ She avoided answering. ✗ avoided to answer
  • After any preposition → -ing: ✓ interested in learning, ✓ capable of designing, ✓ before leaving, ✓ instead of waiting. ✗ interested in to learn.
  • "to" can be a preposition, not part of an infinitive. Then it is followed by -ing: ✓ look forward to seeing you, ✓ be used to working late, ✓ object to paying, ✓ committed / dedicated to protecting, ✓ the key to solving. ✗ look forward to see you. Test: if you can put a noun after *to* (look forward to the holiday), *to* is a preposition → use -ing.
  • Verbs that change meaning: *stop* — ✓ He stopped smoking (quit) / He stopped to smoke (paused in order to smoke). *remember / forget* — ✓ I remember locking the door (I did it earlier) / Remember to lock the door (a task for later).
  • Parallelism after these verbs: keep both items in the same form: ✓ She enjoys reading and writing. ✗ She enjoys reading and to write.

Worked examples

Each example is a sentence from an ACT-style essay. The underlined portion is shown in bold.

Worked example 1 — adjective + preposition. "Many young architects in Egypt are capable to design buildings that stay cool without air conditioning."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) capable in designing
  • C) capable of designing
  • D) capable for design

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. The idiom is *capable of*, and a verb after a preposition takes -ing: capable of designing.
  • A uses *to* + verb. Trap: *able to design* is correct, so *capable to design* sounds possible — but *capable* needs *of*.
  • B uses the wrong preposition.
  • D uses the wrong preposition and the bare verb *design*.

Worked example 2 — verb + preposition. "Traditional Nubian houses consist from thick mud-brick walls, domed roofs and a wide inner courtyard."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) of
  • C) in
  • D) with

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. The idiom is *consist of* (= be made of).
  • A *consist from* is a common translation error.
  • C *consist in* is a rare formal idiom meaning *have as its main quality* ("Happiness consists in…"); it does not mean *be made of*. Trap: a real phrase with the wrong meaning.
  • D *consist with* is not standard.

Worked example 3 — gerund after a verb. "After weeks of debate, the council finally agreed building a footbridge across the canal."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) on building
  • C) to build
  • D) for building

Answer: C.

  • C is correct. *Agree* is followed by the infinitive when you agree to do an action: agreed to build.
  • A uses a gerund after *agree*, which is not standard.
  • B *agree on* is used with a noun for a shared decision (agreed on a date), not with an action they will do.
  • D *agree for* is not standard.

Worked example 4 — "to" as a preposition. "The students are looking forward to visit the science museum in Alexandria next month."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) visiting
  • C) have visited
  • D) be visiting

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. In *look forward to*, *to* is a preposition (you can say *look forward to the trip*), so the verb takes -ing: looking forward to visiting.
  • A treats *to* as part of an infinitive. Trap: this is the most common error with *look forward to*.
  • C and D are verb phrases that cannot follow a preposition.

Worked example 5 — meaning-dependent idiom. "At the public meeting, most residents agreed to the mayor that the old market should be restored rather than demolished."

Question: Choose the best version of the underlined portion. If the original is best, choose NO CHANGE.

  • A) NO CHANGE
  • B) with
  • C) on
  • D) for

Answer: B.

  • B is correct. You agree with a person or an opinion. Here the residents share the mayor's opinion, so *agreed with the mayor*.
  • A *agree to* is used for accepting a proposal or a request (agreed to the plan), not a person. Trap: a real idiom with the wrong meaning.
  • C *agree on* is used when people reach a shared decision about something (agreed on a date).
  • D *agree for* is not standard.
Classic traps
  • The translation trap: consist from, interested about, responsible of, depend of — these follow Arabic patterns, not English.
  • capable to / succeed to / insist to: these three need a preposition + -ing (capable of doing, succeed in doing, insist on doing).
  • prefer … than: the standard is prefer X to Y.
  • to + base verb after a preposition-to: look forward to see, used to work (meaning accustomed) → look forward to seeing, used to working.
  • Gerund vs infinitive: decided building, avoided to answer → decided to build, avoided answering.
  • Right word, wrong meaning: consist in, compare to, agree on — real idioms that do not fit the sentence.
  • Broken pairs: not only … and also, neither … or, between … to.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Only the small word changes? Say the anchor word + each preposition alone.
  • A verb after a preposition is always -ing.
  • If *to* can be followed by a noun (look forward to the trip), it is a preposition → -ing.
  • Check pairs: not only / but also, either / or, neither / nor, between / and, as / as.
  • Two choices both sound right? Decide by meaning (agree with a person, to a plan).
  • Before test day, read the four tables on this page aloud three times — hearing the pairs is the fastest way to learn them.

Practice

Two short ACT-style essays with 5 questions each. The numbers in brackets match the question numbers. Every question tests idioms, prepositions or gerund/infinitive choices.

Remember
  • Find the anchor word and say it with each preposition: capable of, interested in, consist of, responsible for, depend on, different from.
  • prefer X to Y; regard X as Y; not only … but also; neither … nor.
  • After a preposition, use -ing — including *look forward to*, *be used to*, *object to*.
  • decide/agree/plan/refuse/manage to do; enjoy/avoid/finish/consider/suggest doing.
  • When two prepositions are both real, the meaning decides (agree with / to / on; compare to / with).
