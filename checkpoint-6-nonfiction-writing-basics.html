Checkpoint 6 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Paper 1 Writing Basics: Purpose, Format, Register and G.A.A.P.

Everything you need before any non-fiction genre: purpose vs format, formal vs informal language, reading the task, the G.A.A.P. technique, a 5-minute plan, paragraphing and a strong sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

In Checkpoint 6 Paper 1, the writing task is worth 25 marks: half of the whole paper. Before you learn any single genre (autobiography, recount, diary, letter, review…), you need the skills on this page. You will learn why you are writing (purpose), what shape the writing must take (format), whether to sound formal or informal (register), how to read the task with the G.A.A.P. technique, how to plan in five minutes and how to build strong paragraphs. Every genre page in this course is built on these foundations, so study this page first.

1. Where the writing task sits in Paper 1

Section B: Writing25about 30 minutesWrite ONE non-fiction piece. There is space to plan before you write.

Paper 2 (Fiction) has exactly the same shape (Reading 25 + Writing 25 in 1 hour), but its writing task is a story. Recent Paper 1 writing tasks have included a review of a toy for a children's magazine, a news report for a school newspaper and a balanced argument about where a new recycling centre should go. Other non-fiction types from the curriculum can also appear: letters, persuasive writing, information texts, instructions, recounts, diaries, biographies and articles. That is why this course gives you a pack for every genre.

Rules that can cost you marks before your writing is even judged
  • 20 words or fewer = 0 marks for the whole task.
  • 21 to 60 words = no more than 7 marks out of 25, however good the writing is. Always write a full answer.
  • Writing the wrong text type (for example, a story when a report was asked for) means Creation of texts can get only 1 mark and Structure is limited too.
  • Your plan is not marked, but a good plan raises the marks of everything else.
How long should my answer be?

Aim for about 250–350 words. With about 30 minutes for writing, that is long enough to develop every point, short enough to finish and check. Quality matters more than length.

2. Writing purposes vs writing formats

Almost every writing task asks you to do two separate jobs at once. Purpose is *why* you are writing: the effect you want on the reader (to inform, explain, describe, persuade, argue, recount, instruct or entertain). Format is *how the writing is packaged*: its layout, such as a letter, an email, a diary entry, a report, a leaflet or a review.

Students lose marks because they mix these two up. Some think a format always has one fixed purpose ("a report can only inform"). Others think every task must have a format, even when none is named. In fact, one purpose can be written in many formats, and one format can serve many purposes.

PURPOSEFORMAT (layout)
Question it answersWhy am I writing this? What do I want my reader to think, feel or do?What should this look like on the page?
Decided byThe intention in the task (convince, explain, describe, recount…)Only what the task names directly. Never guessed.
If the task does not sayYou still work it out from the wordingWrite normal, well-paragraphed writing with no special layout
How many in one piece?Can be more than one (a main purpose and a second one)Only ONE format per piece
The Golden Rule (from the teacher's book)

A piece of writing can have more than one purpose, but it can only ever have ONE format. A format's special features (a greeting, an address, a headline, a date line, a sign-off) are used ONLY when the task names that format. If no format is named, do not invent one. Write clear, organised paragraphs that do the job the task asks for.

Example 1: *"The old playground in your town is going to be closed. Write to convince people that it should stay open."* The purpose is to persuade (the word *convince* tells you). No format is named: there is no "letter", "article" or "speech". So you write normal persuasive paragraphs with a title, and no greeting, address or headline.

Example 2: *"Write a letter to your local council explaining why the playground should stay open."* The purpose is the same (persuade), but now the format is named: a letter. Now, and only now, you use letter layout: greeting, organised paragraphs and a proper sign-off.

Main purpose first

Many tasks mix purposes. A review informs AND persuades. A diary recounts AND describes feelings. Always ask: "What is the MAIN effect I want on my reader?" That is your main purpose, and it decides most of your language choices.

الهدف (Purpose) هو لماذا تكتب: تقنع؟ تخبر؟ تحكي ما حدث؟ أما الشكل (Format) فهو شكل الكتابة على الورقة: خطاب، إيميل، مذكرات، تقرير. إذا لم يذكر السؤال الشكل بالاسم، لا تخترع شكلاً: لا تكتب Dear ولا عنواناً صحفياً، بل اكتب فقرات منظمة بعنوان بسيط تحقق الهدف. وإذا ذكره، فالتزم بكل ملامحه.

  1. "Your school is having a 'Kindness Week'. Write to encourage other pupils to take part." Purpose? Format?

    Show answer
    Purpose: persuade (the word 'encourage'). Format: none named, so write normal persuasive paragraphs with a title. No greeting, no headline.

  2. "Write a diary entry about the first day of the summer holidays." Purpose? Format?

    Show answer
    Purpose: recount and express feelings. Format: diary entry (named), so use the date, Dear Diary, first person and an informal, personal tone.

  3. "Write a review of a book you enjoyed for the school library's website." Purpose? Format?

    Show answer
    Main purpose: inform AND persuade (tell readers about the book and recommend it). Format: review (named), so name the book, describe it without spoiling the ending, give your opinion and a recommendation or rating.

  4. "Write about a time when you helped someone." Purpose? Format?

    Show answer
    Purpose: recount. Format: none named, so write a normal recount: title, events in time order, feelings and a reflection. Do not add Dear Diary or a date line.

  5. "Explain to a younger pupil how to look after a class pet." Purpose? Format?

    Show answer
    Purpose: explain / instruct. Format: none named, so write clear, organised paragraphs (you may use steps). Do not turn it into a letter.

3. Formal vs informal language (register)

Register means how formal or informal your language is. You choose it by asking three questions: Who is reading? Why am I writing? What format has the task named? A letter to a headteacher needs a different register from a diary entry. Choosing the wrong register is one of the easiest ways to lose marks in Creation of texts and Vocabulary, even when your ideas are excellent.

FeatureFormalInformal
ContractionsNot used: cannot, do not, I amCommon: can't, don't, I'm
Slang and chatty wordsAvoidedAllowed (but no text-message spelling)
SentencesComplete, often longerCan be short, chatty, with questions and exclamations
TonePolite, respectful, seriousFriendly, personal, relaxed
VocabularyPrecise: assist, receive, numerousEveryday: help, get, lots of
Typical readerHeadteacher, council, newspaper readers, strangersFriends, family, classmates, your diary
Typical formatsformal letter, report, biography, news report, information textdiary, informal letter or email, some blogs

Formal: *"I would be grateful if you could provide more benches in the park."* Informal: *"Can you put some more benches in the park? That'd be great!"* Both say the same thing. Only one suits a letter to the council.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
get → receive, obtainI received your letter on Monday.
give → provideThe school provides free water.
ask → requestI would like to request a meeting.
tell → informI am writing to inform you that…
help → assist, supportVolunteers assisted the visitors.

Formal openers and linking phrases

  • I am writing to…
  • I would be grateful if…
  • In my opinion, … / I firmly believe that…
  • Furthermore, … / In addition, … / Moreover, …
  • However, … / On the other hand, …
  • As a result, … / Therefore, …
  • In conclusion, … / To sum up, …
  • Thank you for taking the time to read this.

Informal openers and linking phrases

  • You won't believe what happened!
  • Guess what?
  • Anyway, …
  • The best part was…
  • To be honest, …
  • I can't wait to…
  • Speak soon! / See you soon!

  1. Rewrite formally: "Can you help me with my project?"

    Show answer
    "Could you please assist me with my project?" (or "I would be grateful if you could help me with my project.")

  2. Rewrite formally: "I can't come to the match because I'm sick."

    Show answer
    "I am unable to attend the match because I am ill." (no contractions, more precise words)

  3. Rewrite formally: "There's loads of rubbish in the park and it's really bad."

    Show answer
    "There is a large amount of litter in the park, and the situation is very poor."

  4. Rewrite formally: "Sorry, I messed up the poster."

    Show answer
    "I apologise for the mistakes I made on the poster."

  5. Which register for: (a) a diary entry about your birthday, (b) a report for the headteacher about the school trip?

    Show answer
    (a) Informal: it is private and personal. (b) Formal: the reader is a person in authority and the purpose is to inform.

4. How to read a Paper 1 writing task

Checkpoint tasks rarely say "Write a persuasive article". Usually you get a short situation (a scenario), an instruction, and then some bullet points to help you. Read every part slowly and underline the key words before you plan.

An exam-style task (original)

Your school wants to start a new club that meets once a week after school. The headteacher has asked pupils to suggest ideas. Write to persuade the headteacher that your idea is the best one. Consider: • what the club would do • who could join • how it would help pupils • what equipment or help it would need.

Part of the taskWhat it tells youIn this task
The situationThe background and your roleA new weekly club. The headteacher wants ideas.
The instruction verbThe PURPOSE'persuade': one-sided, strong, convincing
The readerThe AUDIENCE and so the REGISTERthe headteacher: formal and polite
The format word (if any)The LAYOUTNo format named, so normal persuasive paragraphs with a title
The bullet pointsThe AREA OF CONTENT4 points: what it does, who joins, how it helps, what it needs
'Consider' vs 'Think' (the teacher's rule)
  • Consider = you MUST use at least three of the bullet points in your writing. You may add your own ideas as well.
  • Think about = the bullets are only suggestions. You may use them or your own ideas.
  • Safest habit for both: use every bullet, one paragraph each. You can never lose marks for covering them all.

5. The G.A.A.P. technique

Before you write a single sentence, spend the first few minutes breaking the task down with G.A.A.P.: Genre, Audience, Area of content, Purpose. (Some people call it GAP, but it has four parts, not three.) Each letter matches something the examiner checks, so G.A.A.P. is not extra work: it protects your marks.

G = Genre (two parts: layout + topic)

  1. Layout: what kind of text must you produce? If a format is named, list its features (for a diary: date, Dear Diary, first person, feelings). If no format is named, write normal paragraphs with a title.
  2. Topic: what exactly is the task about? Be precise. Not "school" but "a new after-school club". Not "the environment" but "litter in the school playground".

A = Audience

  • Where will it be read? (a school magazine, a newspaper, a letter to the headteacher, a private diary, a class display)
  • Who exactly will read it? (children your age, parents, a teacher, the council, yourself)
  • So which register? Formal for people in authority and the public. Informal for friends, family and your diary.

A = Area of content

  1. Underline every point the task gives you.
  2. Use all of them. Do not skip the hardest one: missing a point costs Creation of texts marks.
  3. Develop each point. One sentence per point is never enough. Add an example, a reason, a feeling or a detail.

P = Purpose

PurposeTools to use
Persuade'you', questions to the reader, strong adjectives, a personal example, a call to action at the end
Informfacts, numbers and dates, clear sub-headings, a neutral tone, third person
Explainreasons and results: because, so, as a result, this means that
Recounttime order, past tense, time connectives, feelings, a reflection at the end
Argue / discussboth sides, 'On the one hand… On the other hand…', a balanced conclusion
G.A.A.P. for the club taskAnalysis
G (layout)No format named → normal persuasive writing with a title. No 'Dear Headteacher'.
G (topic)My idea for a new weekly after-school club (for example, a chess club)
A (audience)The headteacher → formal, polite, no contractions, no slang
A (area of content)'Consider' → use at least 3 bullets. Use all 4: what it does · who can join · how it helps · what it needs
P (purpose)Persuade → questions to the reader, strong reasons, examples, a confident ending
G.A.A.P. letterWhat the examiner checksCriterion it protects
Genre (layout)Is it the right text type with the right features?Creation of texts, Structure of texts
Genre (topic) + Area of contentIs everything relevant? Are all points covered and developed?Creation of texts
AudienceAre the tone and words right for the reader?Creation of texts, Vocabulary and language
PurposeAre the right techniques used for the right reason?Creation of texts, Vocabulary and language

  1. Do a G.A.A.P. for: "Write a recount of a school trip for the class blog. Think about: where you went, what you did, the best moment, what you learned."

    Show answer
    G: recount for a blog → title, past tense, time order, first person (format named: blog, so a friendly, lively tone). Topic: one school trip. A: classmates and parents reading the blog → friendly but correct, little slang. A: four bullets ('Think about' = suggestions, but use them all). P: recount and entertain → time connectives, feelings, vivid details, a reflection at the end.

  2. Do a G.A.A.P. for: "Write a letter to the headteacher asking for longer lunch breaks. Consider: why pupils need more time, what they could do with it, how lessons would not suffer."

    Show answer
    G: formal letter (named) → Dear…, clear reason in paragraph 1, Yours sincerely, full name. Topic: longer lunch breaks. A: headteacher → formal, polite, no contractions. A: 'Consider' → use at least three points (there are three, so use them all). P: persuade politely → reasons, examples, answering the worry about lessons, a polite request at the end.

  3. Do a G.A.A.P. for: "Write a biography of a person from your town that you admire, for a school booklet."

    Show answer
    G: biography (named) → title, third person, past tense, sub-headings, dates, a quotation. Topic: one admired local person. A: readers of a school booklet (pupils, parents, teachers) → formal and clear. A: no bullets given, so use the biography structure (early life, education, work, achievements). P: inform (and show admiration) → facts, dates, achievements, why they will be remembered.

6. The 5-minute plan

Your plan is not marked, so keep it short: key words only, never full sentences. A good plan takes about five minutes and saves you from forgetting a bullet, repeating yourself or running out of ideas halfway.

MinuteWhat you do
0–1Read the task twice. Underline the purpose word, the reader, any format word and every bullet.
1–2Write G.A.A.P. in one line at the top of the planning space.
2–4Draw boxes: one for each paragraph. Put each bullet into its own box with 2 or 3 key words (an example, a feeling, a detail).
4–5Choose your title, your first sentence and your last idea (an ending that links back to the start). Pick two ambitious words to use.

Example plan for the club task: Title: *Checkmate! Why We Need a Chess Club.* P1 Hook: question → "What if one hour a week could make us better thinkers?" + my idea. P2 What it does: learn rules, puzzles, mini-tournament, older pupils teach younger. P3 Who joins: Years 4 to 6, beginners welcome, no cost. P4 How it helps: concentration, patience, friendship, maths. P5 What it needs: 10 chess sets, one classroom, a teacher to supervise, parents could donate sets. P6 Ending: "Give us one hour and we will give you a school of thinkers."

8. Paragraphing

A paragraph is a group of sentences about ONE main idea. In Checkpoint, good paragraphs earn marks in Structure of texts (7 marks): the examiner wants a clear opening, a body in a sensible order and an ending. Start a new paragraph when you change topic, time, place or person speaking.

Start a new paragraph when…Example
the topic changesfrom 'what the club does' to 'who can join'
the time changesLater that afternoon, …
the place changesWhen we reached the museum, …
a new person speaks (in direct speech)'Are you lost?' asked the guard.

The shape of a strong body paragraph

PartJobExample
Topic sentenceTells the reader what this paragraph is aboutChess would also help pupils in their lessons.
DetailGives an example, a fact or a descriptionEvery move needs you to think three steps ahead, just like a hard maths problem.
ExplanationSays why it mattersThis means pupils would learn to slow down and check their answers.
LinkLeads to the next idea or back to the purposeOf course, a club like this needs a few simple things to begin.
Weak vs strong

Weak: "Chess is good. It helps you think. It is fun." (three short facts, no development) Strong: "Chess would also help pupils in their lessons. Every move needs you to think three steps ahead, just like a difficult maths problem. As a result, players learn to slow down, plan and check, which are skills every teacher wants."

9. Strong sample (no format named)

Notice: this task names no format, so the answer has a title and paragraphs, with no greeting, no headline and no sign-off. Every bullet has its own paragraph.

Sample: 'Let's Grow Something Together' (persuasive, no format named)339 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school is starting a gardening club on the empty roof of the school building. Some pupils think it will be boring. Write to convince pupils in Years 5 and 6 to join the club. Think about: • what members will do • how gardening can help your health and mood • what the school will gain • why it is worth giving up one break a week.

Let's Grow Something Together

What if one break a week could give you fresh air, new friends and a basket of tomatoes that you grew yourself? That is exactly what our new rooftop gardening club offers, and I believe every pupil in Years 5 and 6 should join it.

First of all, members will not just water a few plants and go home. Every Tuesday, we will plant seeds, build wooden boxes for herbs and keep a growth diary for each plant. Mr Karim, our science teacher, will show us how to turn fruit peel from the canteen into compost. In other words, the club is a science lesson that you can touch, smell and even eat.

Gardening is also wonderful for your health and your mood. After hours of sitting at a desk, your body needs to move: digging, carrying and planting keep you active without you even noticing. Many people find that time with plants makes them feel calmer, too. Imagine stepping onto the roof after a difficult maths test. The sun is warm, the mint smells sweet and your worries slowly melt away.

Our school will gain a great deal as well. The roof is grey and empty at the moment; soon it could be green and full of life. The vegetables we grow could be cooked in the canteen, and our flowers could brighten the entrance for visitors. Wouldn't you feel proud to point at them and say, 'I helped to make that'?

Some of you might say, 'But break time is for playing!' I understand, because I love football as much as anyone. However, the club takes only one break a week, so you will still have four left. What you get in return is worth far more: a new skill, new friends and the joy of watching something grow because of you.

So, do not wait for someone else to take your place. Put your name on the list outside the library before Friday. Join the gardening club and help our school bloom.

Why this gets the marks
  • Format decision: no format was named, so there is a title and paragraphs only (no 'Dear…', no headline, no sign-off). This is the correct choice and protects Creation and Structure marks.
  • Creation of texts 5/5: all four bullets are covered and developed, one paragraph each: what members do (P2), health and mood (P3), what the school gains (P4), why it is worth one break (P5). The writer talks directly to the reader ('you', 'Imagine', 'Wouldn't you…?') all the way through and ends with a clear call to action.
  • Persuasive tools: an opening question, a list of three ('fresh air, new friends and a basket of tomatoes'), a teacher's support as evidence, a picture for the reader to imagine (P3), an objection answered ('But break time is for playing!') and imperatives at the end ('Put your name…', 'Join…').
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise, lively words ('compost', 'brighten', 'melt away', 'bloom'). The final word 'bloom' links back to gardening and to the title.
  • Structure of texts 7/7: a hook and clear position in P1. Topic sentences start each body paragraph ('First of all…', 'Gardening is also…', 'Our school will gain…', 'Some of you might say…'). The ending repeats the call to action and links back to the title.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7: a mix of questions, short and long sentences. A colon to explain (P3) and a colon before a list (P5). A semicolon joining two closely related sentences (P4). A pair of commas for extra information ('Mr Karim, our science teacher,'). Apostrophes ('Let's', 'Wouldn't') and correct quotation marks.
  • Word structure (spelling) 3/3: longer words spelt correctly: 'canteen', 'compost', 'difficult', 'brighten', 'visitors', 'gardening'.
  • Length: inside the 250–350 range a Year 6 pupil can write and check in about 30 minutes.

10. Common mistakes

  • Starting to write without reading the whole task, then missing a bullet point.
  • Inventing a format: writing 'Dear Headteacher' or a newspaper headline when the task never asked for a letter or a report.
  • Mixing two formats, for example starting like a letter and ending like a diary.
  • Using the wrong register: slang and 'gonna' in a formal piece, or stiff, formal words in a diary.
  • One long block of writing with no paragraphs.
  • Mentioning each bullet in one short sentence instead of developing it with details and examples.
  • No ending: the piece just stops when time runs out.
  • Writing too little: 60 words or fewer caps your mark at 7 out of 25.
Before-you-write checklist
  • I have underlined the purpose word, the reader, any format word and every bullet.
  • I have done G.A.A.P. in one line.
  • I know whether a format is named. If not, I will write a title and normal paragraphs.
  • I have chosen formal or informal language and I will keep it the same to the end.
  • My plan has one box per paragraph, with every bullet in a box.
  • I know my first sentence and my last idea.
  • I have chosen two or three ambitious words to use.
  • I will leave 5 minutes to check punctuation, tenses and spelling.

اقرأ السؤال كله قبل أن تكتب، وضع خطاً تحت كل نقطة. لا تخترع شكلاً لم يُطلب (لا تكتب Dear إلا إذا طُلب خطاب). اكتب فقرة لكل نقطة واشرحها بمثال أو سبب أو شعور. اجعل لكتابتك بداية قوية ونهاية واضحة، واترك خمس دقائق في النهاية للمراجعة. وتذكّر: إذا كتبت 60 كلمة أو أقل فلن تحصل على أكثر من 7 من 25.

11. Practice task

  1. Practice: "Many children spend most of their weekends indoors. Write to persuade pupils of your age to spend more time outdoors at the weekend. Consider: • what they could do outside • how it helps their bodies and minds • how families can join in • what they miss by staying inside." Write a short plan (G.A.A.P. + paragraph boxes).

    Show answer
    G: no format named → title + persuasive paragraphs, no greeting. Topic: spending weekends outdoors. A: pupils my age → friendly but correct, direct 'you'. A: 'Consider' → at least 3 bullets, use all 4. P: persuade → question opening, examples, imagine-picture, objection answered, call to action. Title: 'Switch Off, Step Out'. P1 Hook: 'When did you last feel grass under your feet?' + position. P2 What to do: cycling, football, planting, a picnic in the park, flying a kite. P3 Body and mind: stronger, better sleep, calmer, less screen tiredness. P4 Families: Friday walk by the Nile, grandparents' garden, family match. P5 What you miss: sunsets, real friends, adventures. Answer the objection 'it's too hot': go early morning or evening. P6 Call to action: 'This weekend, choose one hour outside.' Link back to the title.
