Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Fantasy Stories

How to build a magical world, a reluctant hero and a battle between light and darkness in 35 minutes — features, a six-stage plan, phrases, vocabulary and a full-mark 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is fantasy writing?

Fantasy is a story set in a world with its own rules: magic, mythical creatures, prophecies, enchanted objects and kingdoms that exist only in the writer’s imagination. The danger is usually supernatural (a curse, a shadow, a sorcerer, a dragon) and the central conflict is often a clear battle between good and evil — light against darkness.

Fantasy is NOT "anything goes". The best fantasy stories feel real because the magic follows rules. If a lantern can drive back the darkness, it must not suddenly be able to fly. A fantasy world can be completely invented (a kingdom called Thornmere) or a hidden magical layer inside our own world (a door in a Cairo library that opens onto another realm).

FantasyAdventureScience fiction
Danger comes from…magic, curses, creatures, evil powersnature, the clock, real peopletechnology, science, a controlling system
Settinginvented realm or magical placereal places: desert, sea, mountain, junglethe future, space, a futuristic city
The hero wins by…courage, goodness, a special gift or objectskill, strength, quick thinkingintelligence, questioning, technology

Purpose: to entertain and to amaze — to take the reader somewhere they could never go, while still showing something true about people (courage, friendship, honesty, sacrifice). Audience: the examiner. Write for a reader who wants wonder AND tension.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Paper 2 is the Fiction paper. It lasts 1 hour 10 minutes and is worth 50 marks: Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks).
  • Section B is one story task. It is usually linked by theme to the fiction extract you read in Section A, and it gives you bullet prompts (for example: who the character is, where the story happens, what goes wrong, how it ends).
  • The suggested time for Section B is about 35 minutes, and planning space is given. Planning is not marked, but it is where good stories are won.
  • The task will not always say "write a fantasy story". It gives you a theme. YOU decide that this genre is the best way to answer it — and then you must stay inside that genre from the first line to the last.
  • The same format is used in the Stage 8 Progression Test you sit at school, so everything on this page works for both.

Themes that suit a fantasy story: a gift or object that changes a life, a test of courage, a special place, a promise, a journey, a choice between right and easy, an unexpected hero, light and darkness, a secret passed down a family, home.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in this genre
Creation of texts5The story fits the theme and every bullet prompt. The magical world has clear rules and a real sense of wonder. The hero has an inner struggle (doubt, fear) as well as an outer enemy. The reader is held to the end.
Vocabulary and language3Rich but controlled language: one strong image per sentence. Genre vocabulary (ancient, luminous, ominous, enchanted) chosen to build mood — wonder in calm parts, dread in dark parts.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7The six-stage arc is clear (world → hero and quest → warning → confrontation → cost → echo). Paragraphs are linked. The ending returns to an image from the opening.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7Varied sentences used for effect (long flowing description, short punchy action). Dialogue correctly punctuated. Colon, semicolon, dashes and ellipsis used accurately.
Word structure (spelling)3Difficult words spelt correctly (ancient, mysterious, guardian, believe, extraordinary, silhouette, threshold).
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. A very short answer (about 21-60 words) can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example, a diary or an article instead of a story), Creation of texts is capped at 1 and Structure of texts is capped too.
  • If the story drifts away from the task's theme, you lose Creation marks even if the writing is beautiful — keep the bullet prompts in front of you.

2. Features of fantasy stories

FeatureWhat it meansExample sentence
Magic or supernatural powerSpells, curses or gifts that do not exist in the real world — with LIMITSThe flame could push back the Mist, but only as far as its light could reach.
An invented worldIts own places, history and customs, shown through small detailsIn Thornmere, every child learned the Lantern Song before they could read.
A heroic (often reluctant) protagonistAn ordinary person with a hidden gift who doubts themselves“Why me? I can’t even light a candle without burning my fingers.”
A clear battle of good and evilLight versus shadow. Kindness versus greedThe Hollow King wanted the village to sleep forever.
Mythical creaturesDragons, guardians, spirits, talking animalsA stone owl blinked open its amber eyes.
A powerful object (artefact)A sword, lantern, key, map or amulet that matters to the plotIts handle was warm, almost as if it had a heartbeat.
Prophecy or legendAn old saying or story that the hero must fulfilWhen the last lantern burns, the youngest hand must carry it.
A test or riddleThe hero proves worth through honesty or wisdom, not only strength“What do you fear most?”
Elevated, sensory languageDescription that creates wonder or dreadThe sky burned violet and gold above the silent forest.
Cost of victoryWinning takes something from the hero (energy, a friend, innocence)My arms shook; the lantern was cold for the first time.

Language and layout features (for Structure and Grammar marks):

FeatureHow to use itExample
A titleName the object, place or creatureThe Last Lantern / The Owl of Thornmere / The Silver Key
ParagraphsNew paragraph for time, place, speaker and mood shifts. A one-line paragraph for a turning pointThen, one by one, the lanterns began to die.
DialogueShort and meaningful. Reveals fear, wisdom or threat“Turn back,” the voice hissed, “or you will never leave.”
Italic-style voices or warningsA line set alone on its own paragraph for an eerie effectTurn back, little lantern girl.

Do not overwrite

Fantasy tempts students to pile up adjectives: "the dark, gloomy, shadowy, black, evil, terrifying forest". That loses Vocabulary marks because the words do not add meaning. Choose ONE precise image: "The forest was so dark that even the moonlight seemed afraid to enter."

3. How to structure a fantasy story

Plan with G.C.S.P.P. first: Genre (fantasy — what is the magic and what are its limits?), Characters (hero + guide/helper + evil force or creature, 2-4 only), Setting (one realm with one memorable place: a tower, a wood, a bridge), Point of view (first person for closeness, third person for a bigger "legend" feel), Plot (whole story, opening or continuation).

Then use the six-stage fantasy arc. It gives you room to build the world before the conflict grows.

StageParagraph(s)What to writeTip
1. Opening — set the tone of the world1-2Create atmosphere and scale immediately. Show ONE rule of the magic and what is under threat.A one-line paragraph after your opening ("Then the lanterns began to die.") is a powerful hook.
2. Hero and conflict3-4Introduce the protagonist with STEAL, the quest or gift, and a reason to doubt themselves. A guide figure (grandparent, wizard, creature) can give the task.Inner stakes (fear, doubt) matter as much as the outer enemy.
3. Rising action — build suspense5-6The journey begins. Add a warning, a mysterious voice, a test or a guardian. Each step costs more.Use the setting to frighten: sounds, cold, darkness.
4. Climax — the confrontation7-8Hero meets the evil force. A short exchange of dialogue, then a decisive action using the gift — and a quality the hero has learned (honesty, courage).Short sentences and strong verbs. Keep it clear: one action at a time.
5. Falling action — the cost9Show exhaustion, damage or change, not just a happy cheer.This is what separates a strong ending from a rushed one.
6. Conclusion — a wider echo10Return home or to an image from the opening, and hint that the magic or the world continues.End on an image, not on a moral like "and she learned to be brave".
MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph. Write the rule of your magic in one line.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). By about minute 17 you should have reached the warning or the test.
30–35Proofread: spelling of your ambitious words, full stops (no comma splices), speech punctuation, capital letters, tense consistency.
Make the magic follow a rule

Write the rule of your magic in your plan in one line: "The lantern’s light reveals what hides in the Mist — but it burns out if the carrier lies." Then make the climax USE that rule. The examiner sees a story that was planned, and that is a top-band Creation mark.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Swap the words in brackets to fit your own world and prompt. Use one or two phrases per stage, not more.

Openings

  • Legends spoke of a [kingdom / forest / city] cloaked in [mist / shadow / starlight], where [dragons / spirits / ancient magic] still ruled.
  • In the village of [Name], the [lanterns / bells / wells] had [burned / rung / sung] for a thousand years.
  • The air shimmered faintly, as though the wind itself remembered old spells.
  • Beneath the shadow of [the mountain / the citadel], a secret older than memory waited to be found.
  • It was a world where the impossible was not only possible, but expected.

Describing the magical setting

  • Spires rose like a jagged crown, piercing the clouds.
  • Fields of [silver grass / glowing flowers / floating stones] stretched as far as the eye could see.
  • Rivers of [liquid starlight / molten gold] cut through the valley, lighting the endless dusk.
  • The trees leaned together, whispering in a language older than words.

Introducing characters

  • [Name] was no ordinary [girl / boy / traveller]; [her/his] eyes carried a light no one could explain.
  • Small as [she/he] was, [Name]’s courage burned brighter than the [flame / blade] [she/he] carried.
  • Even [she/he] did not yet know the full weight of what [she/he] carried.
  • The old guardian’s voice ground like a millstone, slow and heavy.

Building tension

  • The once-peaceful [kingdom / valley] teetered on the edge of ruin as the [shadow / curse] spread.
  • “Turn back now,” the voice hissed from the trees, “or you will never leave.”
  • The [amulet / key / blade] hummed faintly, promising power at a price no one had named.
  • Time was running short, and the fate of [the realm] hung by a single thread.

Magic and climax

  • [Name] raised [her/his] hand, and the air itself began to [shimmer / crack / burn].
  • Light erupted from [the lantern / her hands] — gold, blinding, endless.
  • The [beast’s / storm’s] roar shook the earth, and for a heartbeat no one breathed.
  • The darkness shattered like a mirror struck by a stone.

Endings

  • The curse lifted, and the land began to heal — its scars a record of what it had survived.
  • She had won, but deep down she knew this was only the beginning.
  • [Name] looked once more at the [kingdom / forest], then turned towards whatever came next.
  • Some nights, when the wind blows from the north, [the flame / the owl] still watches.
Word / phraseMeaning / use
ancient (adj.)very old, from long ago — ancient stones carved with forgotten runes
enchanted (adj.)under a magic spell — an enchanted forest where paths changed every night
ethereal (adj.)delicate and otherworldly — an ethereal light spilled across the lake
luminous (adj.)glowing softly — luminous moths drifted like living lanterns
majestic (adj.)grand and impressive — the majestic towers of the citadel
ominous (adj.)suggesting something bad is coming — an ominous shadow crept over the walls
sinister (adj.)threatening, suggesting evil — a sinister laugh echoed beyond the firelight
desolate (adj.)empty and abandoned — the desolate ruins of the old capital
fabled (adj.)famous in old stories — the fabled guardians of the eastern pass
prophecy (n.)a prediction of the future, often magical — the prophecy named the youngest child
artefact (n.)an object of special importance — the lantern was the oldest artefact in the valley
realm (n.)a kingdom or magical world — the realm beyond the mountains
sorcerer (n.)a person who uses (often evil) magic — the sorcerer’s curse spread overnight
incandescent (adj.)glowing with intense light — an incandescent glow burst from the amulet
serene (adj.)calm and peaceful — the serene garden seemed untouched by the war

5. Strong sample answer

Fantasy story — The Last Lantern547 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: The extract you have read is about a girl who is given an important responsibility by an older relative. Write a story about a character who is given something that changes their life. In your story you should: describe the character and what they are given, explain why it is important and what they must do with it, and show how the character changes by the end.

In the village of Thornmere, the lanterns had burned for a thousand years, and for a thousand years the Mist had stayed outside the walls.

Then, one by one, the lanterns began to die.

By the night of the Harvest Moon, only one was left: a small brass lantern that hung above my grandmother’s door. Its flame was the colour of honey, and when I was little she told me it held a single spark stolen from the first sunrise. I had always thought that was just a story.

“Salma.” Grandmother’s voice was as thin as paper. She pressed the lantern into my hands, and its handle was warm, almost as if it had a heartbeat. “The light must reach the Bell Tower before midnight. If it burns there, every lantern in the valley will wake.”

“Why me?” I whispered. “I can’t even light a candle without burning my fingers.”

“Because it chose you,” she said simply. “It glows brighter every time you walk past.”

The Bell Tower stood on the far side of the Whispering Wood. As soon as I stepped between the trees, the Mist rose to meet me — grey, cold and hungry — curling round my ankles like grasping fingers. Voices hissed inside it.

Turn back, little lantern girl. Turn back, or you will never leave.

I kept walking. With every step, the lantern’s glow pushed the Mist back a little further, and slowly I began to understand: it was not afraid of the light. It was afraid of being seen.

At the foot of the tower, a stone owl blinked open its amber eyes. “No one passes,” it said, its voice grinding like a millstone, “unless they answer truly. What do you fear most?”

I thought of a dozen clever answers. Then I told the truth. “Failing everyone who trusts me.”

The owl bowed its great head. “Then you are ready.”

I climbed the spiral stairs as the bells began to toll midnight. On the twelfth stroke, the Mist surged up the tower in one enormous wave, and a shape formed inside it: a tall, hollow king with a crown of smoke and eyes like two dying embers.

“Give me the flame,” he breathed, “and I will let your village sleep forever in peace.”

For a moment, sleep sounded wonderful: no more fear, no more darkness creeping closer every night. Then I remembered Grandmother’s warm hands and the children of Thornmere waiting behind their shutters.

“Thornmere doesn’t need to sleep,” I said, raising the lantern high above my head. “It needs to wake up.”

Light erupted from the brass — gold, blinding, endless. The Hollow King screamed, and the Mist shattered like a mirror struck by a stone. Below me, across the whole valley, a thousand lanterns flickered back to life. Then my legs gave way. I sat on the cold stone with the lantern in my lap, its flame shrunk to a tiny blue bead, and I shook until the sky turned grey.

When I came home at dawn, Grandmother was waiting by the door. She did not ask what had happened; she simply smiled, as if she had always known.

The lantern still hangs above our door. Some nights, when the wind blows from the north, its flame leans towards the forest, watching.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — all three bullets: what Salma is given and why it matters (paragraphs 3-6), what she must do (reach the Bell Tower, 4 and 7-17), and how she changes — from "I can’t even light a candle" to raising the lantern and speaking for the village (16). The magic has a rule (the light pushes back the Mist and reveals what hides in it) and the climax uses that rule.
  • Creation — inner and outer conflict: the Hollow King is the outer enemy. Her doubt (5) and her temptation to let the village sleep (15) are the inner struggle, which makes the reader care.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — mood-building fantasy words: Harvest Moon, brass, ember, millstone, spiral stairs, toll, surged, erupted, shattered. Images are controlled — one per sentence: "as thin as paper", "like grasping fingers", "like a mirror struck by a stone".
  • Vocabulary — contrast as a key idea: sleep/wake, warm/cold, light/Mist, with the final line "It needs to wake up" turning it into the story’s message.
  • Structure 7/7 — the six-stage arc: world and threat (1-2), hero and gift (3-6), rising action with a warning voice and a guardian’s test (7-12), confrontation (13-16), victory and its cost: the flame shrinks to a tiny blue bead and Salma's legs give way (17), echo ending (18-19).
  • Structure — cohesion and pace: the one-line paragraph "Then, one by one, the lanterns began to die." is the hook. The warning voice stands alone for an eerie effect. The ending returns to the lantern above the door from paragraph 3, now "watching" — the magic continues.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — sentence variety: long complex description (paragraph 13), short sentences for decision ("Then I told the truth."), a fronted adverbial ("On the twelfth stroke,"), a rule of three ("gold, blinding, endless").
  • Grammar & punctuation — range: colons for explanation ("only one was left: a small brass lantern…", "I began to understand: it was not afraid…"), a colon for explanation of what sleep would bring ("wonderful: no more fear, no more darkness…"), a semicolon joining two closely related sentences ("She did not ask what had happened; she simply smiled…"), a pair of dashes for extra information ("— grey, cold and hungry —"), a single dash for emphasis ("— gold, blinding, endless"), interrupted speech correctly punctuated ("“Give me the flame,” he breathed, “and I will…”").
  • Spelling 3/3 — tricky words correct: lantern, Harvest, whispered, curling, grinding, millstone, spiral, surged, enormous, erupted, shattered, flickered. Apostrophes accurate (grandmother’s, lantern’s, Thornmere doesn’t).

6. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
Magic solves every problem instantly ("She waved her wand and everything was fine.")No tension. Weak climax (Creation, Structure)Give magic a limit and a cost. The hero must struggle.
Copying a famous film or book (wizard schools, a ring, a lion king)Not original. Examiners noticeInvent your own object, creature and place names.
Too many characters and creaturesConfusing in a 35-minute storyHero + helper + one evil force. One creature is enough.
Long battle scenes with no feelingAction without tension or meaningShort exchange of words, ONE decisive action, then show the cost.
Adjective piles ("the dark, gloomy, black, evil cave")Vocabulary band drops — words repeat meaningOne precise image per sentence.
Ending with a moral: "And she learned that courage is important."Telling, not showingEnd on an image that shows the change.
Speech errors: “Turn back”, the voice said.Grammar & punctuationPunctuation goes inside the speech marks: “Turn back,” the voice said.
Invented names spelt differently each timeSpelling and cohesionWrite your names in the plan and copy them exactly.
Checklist before you hand in
  • I answered the theme and every bullet prompt.
  • My magic has a clear rule and a limit, and the climax uses it.
  • My hero has a doubt or fear at the start and has changed by the end.
  • I used one striking image per sentence — no adjective piles.
  • I followed the six stages: world → hero → warning → confrontation → cost → echo.
  • I used a colon, a semicolon, dashes and correctly punctuated dialogue.
  • My ending returns to an image from the opening.
  • I checked the spelling of my invented names and ambitious words.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: The extract you read describes a character who finds a hidden door. Write a story about a special place and what a character discovers there. Write a G.C.S.P.P. plan with the rule of your magic.

    Show answer
    G: fantasy (hidden realm inside our world). C: Hamza (12, bored, lonely after moving house), the Keeper (an old fox who speaks), the Grey Tide (a creature that eats colour). S: a locked room at the back of his grandfather’s bookshop in Alexandria that opens onto a garden where stories grow on trees. P: third person, past tense. Magic rule: every story read aloud in the garden turns into colour. If nobody reads, the Grey Tide spreads. Plot — 1 hook: the key that hums. 2 the garden. 3 the Keeper explains the danger. 4 Hamza doubts himself (he hates reading aloud). 5 the Grey Tide reaches the gate. 6 climax: he reads his grandfather’s favourite story, voice shaking, and colour bursts back. 7 echo: back in the shop, he opens a book for a younger child.

  2. Practice task 2: Write the opening (about 150 words) of a fantasy story called "The Silver Key". What must your opening do?

    Show answer
    It must: (1) set the tone of the world with one sensory image. (2) introduce the hero with a STEAL detail. (3) show the magical object and one rule. (4) end with a hook (a question, a warning or a one-line paragraph). Example first line: "The key had been warm for three days, and on the fourth day it began to whisper."

  3. Rewrite to remove the adjective pile: "The huge, big, enormous, scary, frightening dragon came out of the dark, black, gloomy cave."

    Show answer
    Possible answer: "The dragon uncoiled from the cave like smoke made solid, so large that its shadow swallowed the whole valley." — One precise image (simile + exaggeration of the shadow) replaces six repeated adjectives.

  4. Look at the sample story. Why does the writer use the colon in "sleep sounded wonderful: no more fear, no more darkness creeping closer every night"? Give every possible answer.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to introduce an explanation of WHY sleep sounded wonderful. To give extra information about what the Hollow King’s offer would bring. To emphasise how tempting the offer is by pausing before it. To slow the reader down at Salma’s moment of temptation, building tension before her choice.

  5. Why does the writer use the pair of dashes in "the Mist rose to meet me — grey, cold and hungry — curling round my ankles"? And why the simile "like grasping fingers"?

    Show answer
    Dashes: to add extra information describing the Mist, so the reader pictures it clearly. Simile: to compare the Mist to fingers so it seems alive and threatening. To suggest it wants to grab and hold her back. To create a frightening, creepy atmosphere. To make the reader feel Salma’s fear and the danger she is in.

  6. Punctuate correctly: give me the key the sorcerer said or you will never see your village again

    Show answer
    “Give me the key,” the sorcerer said, “or you will never see your village again.” — The sentence is split by the reporting clause, so the second part starts with a small letter and the comma before "the sorcerer" goes inside the speech marks.
