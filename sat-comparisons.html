Faulty and Logical Comparisons

Compare like with like: that of / those of and possessives, like/unlike openers, than any other, comparative vs superlative and the right partner words — with worked examples and a 10-question practice quiz.

American Diploma › Digital SAT Reading and Writing — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Comparison questions sit in Standard English Conventions, the grammar-and-punctuation domain of the Reading and Writing section (about 26% of the scored questions, usually 11–15 of them). The official skill list does not name comparisons as a separate skill, but they are part of writing sentences whose form and sense are correct: a comparison that compares the wrong things is not logical, and the choices often differ only in a possessive (Alexandria / Alexandria's), a pronoun (that / those) or a word form (more / most). Grammar questions usually come after the reading questions in each module.

The stem is the usual one: "Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?" Look for comparison signals: than, as… as, like, unlike, compared with, similar to, different from, and words ending in -er / -est or starting with more / most.

Rule 1: Compare like with like

You can only compare things of the same kind: a population with a population, a price with a price, a person with a person. The most common mistake compares a feature of one thing with the whole of another thing. Ask: What exactly is being compared on the left side? Is the right side the same kind of thing?

  • ✗ The population of Cairo is larger than Alexandria. (population compared with a city)
  • ✓ The population of Cairo is larger than that of Alexandria. (that = the population)
  • ✓ Cairo's population is larger than Alexandria's. (Alexandria's = Alexandria's population)
  • ✗ The shells of desert snails are thicker than coastal snails. (shells compared with snails)
  • ✓ The shells of desert snails are thicker than those of coastal snails. (those = the shells)
  • ✗ Mona's handwriting is neater than her brother. (handwriting compared with a person)
  • ✓ Mona's handwriting is neater than her brother's.

Rule 2: That of, those of, or a possessive

To avoid repeating the noun, English uses three tools. Choose by number (singular or plural) and keep the sentence logical.

The compared noun is…Use…Example (✓)
singularthat ofThe cost of rice is higher than that of wheat.
pluralthose ofThe wings of bats are thinner than those of birds.
owned by a person / thinga possessive ('s or s')Karim's score was higher than Mona's. The lions' cage is larger than the tigers'.
  • ✗ The rules of chess are more complex than that of checkers. (rules is plural → those of)
  • ✗ Karim's score was higher than that of Mona's. (double possessive: use "that of Mona" or "Mona's", never both)
  • ✗ The number of visitors in 2025 was higher than those in 2020. (number is singular → that)

Tip: replace "that" or "those" with the noun it stands for and read the sentence again: "higher than the number in 2020" ✓.

Rule 3: Like, unlike, compared with — at the start of a sentence

An opener such as Like…, Unlike…, Compared with…, Similar to… is both a comparison and an introductory modifier. The noun right after the comma must be the same kind of thing as the noun in the opener.

  • ✗ Unlike the earlier model's battery, the new tablet can run all day. (battery compared with tablet)
  • ✓ Unlike the earlier model, the new tablet can run all day. (model vs model)
  • ✗ Like the ancient Egyptians, cats were kept in many Roman homes. (people compared with cats)
  • ✓ Like the ancient Egyptians, many Romans kept cats in their homes. (people vs people)
  • ✗ Compared with last year's exam, students found this year's easier. (exam vs students)
  • ✓ Compared with last year's exam, this year's was easier.

Rule 4: "Than any other" and "than anyone else"

When you compare one member of a group with the rest of the same group, add other or else. Without it, the sentence says the thing is bigger than itself.

  • ✗ Nour read more books than any student in her class. (Nour is a student in her class, so she read more than herself?)
  • ✓ Nour read more books than any other student in her class.
  • ✓ Nour read more books than any student in the other classes. (She is not in that group, so no "other" is needed.)
  • ✗ Our team scored more than anyone in the league. → ✓ more than any other team in the league.
  • ✓ The coach worked harder than anyone else.

Rule 5: Comparative or superlative?

Use the comparative (-er / more / less) for two things and the superlative (-est / most / least) for three or more. Never use both endings at once.

AdjectiveComparative (2 things)Superlative (3+ things)
short: fast, cheapfaster, cheaperthe fastest, the cheapest
ending in -y: easy, earlyeasier, earlierthe easiest, the earliest
long: practical, expensivemore practical, less expensivethe most practical, the least expensive
irregular: good / wellbetterthe best
irregular: bad / badlyworsethe worst
irregular: farfarther / furtherthe farthest / the furthest
  • ✗ Of the two designs, the judges chose the most practical one. → ✓ the more practical one (two designs)
  • ✗ Of the three proposals, the committee chose the cheaper one. → ✓ the cheapest one (three proposals)
  • ✗ The new train is more faster than the old one. → ✓ faster (never more + -er)
  • ✗ This is the most easiest route. → ✓ the easiest route

Rule 6: Complete the comparison with the right partner word

  • -er / more … than: taller than, more useful than — never "taller then" (then = time).
  • as … as: as tall as, as much rain as — never "as tall than".
  • different from: "Her method is different from mine" (the safest choice in formal writing).
  • the same as / similar to: "the same as last year", "similar to a lemon".
  • Finish the comparison: "The new phone is cheaper" is fine only if the text already says cheaper than what; otherwise add "than the old one".

The 3-step method for test day

  1. Spot the comparison signal: than, as… as, like, unlike, compared with, -er/-est, more/most.
  2. Name the two things being compared. Read the left side and find its head noun (population, shells, score). Then check that the blank gives the same kind of thing — and the same number (that for singular, those for plural).
  3. Check the form: two things → comparative; three or more → superlative; same group → any other; correct partner word (than / as / from).

Worked examples

Worked example 1: those of

Snails that live in the dry hills of Sinai must protect themselves from heat and from birds. In a recent survey, researchers measured hundreds of snail shells and found that the shells of snails collected in the hills were thicker than ______ collected near the coast.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) snails
  • B) those of snails
  • C) that of snails
  • D) the snail
Answer: B) those of snails
  • The sentence compares shells. The second side must also be shells: "those of snails" = the shells of snails. "Those" is plural because "shells" is plural (B).
  • A is wrong: it compares shells with snails (faulty comparison).
  • C is wrong: "that" is singular, but it replaces the plural "shells" (number error).
  • D is wrong: it compares shells with a snail, and it is singular (faulty comparison).

Worked example 2: than any other

Every year, students at a secondary school in Assiut take part in a reading challenge. This year, Nour read more books than ______ in her class, finishing 64 titles before the summer holiday.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) any student
  • B) all students
  • C) every student's
  • D) any other student
Answer: D) any other student
  • Nour is one of the students in her class, so she must be compared with the other students: "any other student" (D).
  • A is wrong: "any student in her class" includes Nour herself, so she read more than herself (illogical).
  • B is wrong: "all students" in her class also includes Nour.
  • C is wrong: the possessive "student's" leaves nothing for it to own; the comparison is between people, not their things.

Worked example 3: comparative or superlative

A youth council in Ismailia asked architects to suggest ideas for a new sports center. Of the two designs that reached the final round, the council chose the ______ one, a plan that uses natural light instead of expensive lamps.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) more practical
  • B) most practical
  • C) practicaler
  • D) more practicaler
Answer: A) more practical
  • Only two designs are compared, so we need the comparative: "the more practical one" (A). "Practical" is a long adjective, so it takes "more", not -er.
  • B is wrong: "most" is for three or more things.
  • C is wrong: "practicaler" is not a word; long adjectives use "more".
  • D is wrong: never use "more" and -er together.

Worked example 4: an unlike opener

The young painter Ahmed Samir trained for years in the studio of a well-known artist who used only soft browns and greys. Unlike the paintings of his teacher, ______ bright, almost electric colors.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  • A) Ahmed likes to use
  • B) the young artist Ahmed uses
  • C) Ahmed's paintings are full of
  • D) Ahmed's style is to use
Answer: C) Ahmed's paintings are full of
  • The opener compares paintings, so the noun after the comma must be paintings: "Ahmed's paintings" (C). Here the possessive is right because the head noun is "paintings".
  • A is wrong: it compares the teacher's paintings with Ahmed, a person.
  • B is wrong: same problem — paintings compared with "the young artist".
  • D is wrong: it compares paintings with a style (head noun "style").
Classic traps
  • Feature vs whole: the population of Cairo vs Alexandria, the shells of snails vs snails.
  • Wrong number: that for a plural noun or those for a singular one (the number… those ✗).
  • Double possessive: that of Mona's ✗ — use that of Mona or Mona's.
  • Missing "other": than any student in her class ✗ when she is in the class.
  • Two vs three: the most of two ✗, the -er of three ✗; and never more + -er or most + -est.
  • Wrong partner word: as tall than ✗, taller then ✗.
Fast strategy for test day
  • When choices differ only by that / those / a possessive / no word, it is a comparison question. Find the head noun on the left side first.
  • Replace that or those with the noun it stands for and read the sentence; if it sounds right, keep it.
  • Count the things: two → -er/more; three or more → -est/most.
  • For Like/Unlike openers, use the doer question from the Modifiers lesson: the noun after the comma must match the noun in the opener.
  • These are quick questions — 30–45 seconds.

Practice

Remember
  • Compare like with like: a feature with the same feature, a person with a person.
  • Use that of (singular), those of (plural) or a possessive — never "that of X's".
  • Like/Unlike openers: the noun after the comma must be the same kind of thing as the noun in the opener.
  • Compare one member with the rest of its group using any other / anyone else.
  • Two things → -er/more; three or more → -est/most; never both at once.
