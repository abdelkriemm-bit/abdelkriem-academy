Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Instruction Writing

How to write clear, precise instructions and 'how to' guides for Paper 1: features, the seven-part structure, phrases, vocabulary and a strong 350+ word sample with numbered steps.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is instruction writing?

Instruction writing tells a reader exactly how to carry out a task, step by step, so that they can complete it successfully without needing to ask a single question. Recipes, assembly guides, safety procedures, the method in a science experiment, game rules, directions and 'how to' guides on a website are all instruction writing.

Its purpose is not to entertain or to persuade but to enable the reader. The test of good instructions is simple: could a reader who knows nothing about the task follow them and get the right result? If the reader hesitates, guesses or has to read a step twice, the instructions have failed — however elegant the sentences are.

QuestionAnswer for instructions
PurposeTo explain how to do or make something, in order, so the reader can do it alone.
AudienceNamed in the task: a new Year 7 student, a younger brother or sister, a visitor, readers of the school website. The reader decides how much detail you give.
ToneClear, calm, neutral and economical. Friendly is fine for young readers, but no jokes, no long stories and no emotive language.
Person and tenseSecond person ('you', stated or understood) and the imperative: Measure… Attach… Check… Present tense throughout.
Where it appearsPaper 1 (Non-fiction), Section B Writing — and as a text type in Section A Reading (for example, questions on why the writer uses imperative verbs or numbered steps).

Where it sits in the exam

Year 8 students sit the Cambridge Lower Secondary Progression Test Stage 8 in school and later the Checkpoint (0861) test, in the same format: Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction, each with Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks), and each lasting 1 hour 10 minutes. About 35 minutes is suggested for the writing on recent papers.

How an instructions task is worded
  • The task rarely says 'write instructions'. It gives you a scenario and a reader and expects you to recognise the form: explain how…, describe how to…, write a guide…, tell your reader how…
  • Possible forms: a guide for the school website, a page in a welcome booklet, a 'how to' article for a magazine, or an email explaining to a friend how to do something.
  • Instructions are a non-fiction text type in the Lower Secondary curriculum. They have not been confirmed as a recent Checkpoint writing task, so treat them as a type that can appear — and they appear often as reading texts.
  • Watch for the reader named in the task (a younger student, a visitor). Writing for an adult expert when the task says 'a new Year 7 student' loses Creation marks.

How your instructions are marked (25 marks)

CategoryMarksWhat the top band needs in instructions
Creation of texts (content, purpose and audience)5Clearly a set of instructions that a real reader could follow. Every bullet in the task covered. Detail pitched at the named reader. Helpful extras that show you are thinking about the reader: warnings, tips, what success looks like.
Vocabulary and language3Precise verbs (attach, align, insert, measure, press) and exact quantities, sizes and times instead of 'some', 'a bit', 'until it's ready'.
Structure of texts (text structure)7Title, introduction, equipment list, safety section, numbered steps in strict order (grouped into stages), troubleshooting, conclusion. Clear sequencing words. Layout that lets the reader find their place.
Grammar and punctuation (sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7Correct imperatives, but also variety: fronted subordinate clauses (Once…, Before…, If…), sentences that explain why, a short sentence for a warning. Correct colons, semicolons, brackets, commas in lists, consistent bullet style.
Word structure (spelling)3Technical and ambitious words spelt correctly (equipment, separately, necessary, centimetre, temperature).
Do not lose easy marks
  • Wrong text type: a story about the day you planted a tree is not instructions. Creation of texts is capped at 1 and Structure is capped too.
  • Too short: very short answers are capped (21–60 words = 7 marks at most). A list of eight one-line steps is too thin; Year 8 should aim for at least 350 words including the introduction, equipment, safety, explanations and ending.
Sentence range when most sentences are commands
  • Begin some steps with a subordinate clause: Once the water boils, … / Before you switch on, … / If the lid sticks, …
  • Add a short 'why' sentence after important steps: This stops the pot from rotting.
  • Use a very short sentence for a warning: Never leave it unattended.
  • Write the introduction and conclusion in ordinary statements — they show you can do more than give orders.
Punctuation that fits instructions
  • Colon: to introduce a list (Before you begin, gather the following:) or an explanation (Wear gloves: compost can contain germs.).
  • Semicolon: only to join two closely related sentences (Planting takes thirty minutes; after that, care takes five minutes a day.).
  • Brackets: for extra information, e.g. Cut just below the leaf joint (the point where the leaves grow).
  • Single dash: for emphasis (— never pour it all at once).
  • Commas in lists and after fronted clauses (Once it boils, turn off the heat.).

3. How to structure instruction writing

PartWhat to writeTip
1. TitleName the task and the result exactly.'How to…' titles are clear and safe.
2. Introduction2–3 sentences: what the reader will achieve, how long it takes, any skill or adult help needed.Lets the reader decide whether to start now. Write it in full sentences.
3. What you will needBulleted list with exact quantities and sizes. Tools first, then materials.Nothing should have to be fetched halfway through.
4. SafetyAny hazard in its own short section — and again just before the step it applies to.Repeating a warning is good practice here.
5. Method (numbered steps)One action per step, in strict order, each starting with an imperative. Group steps into stages with subheadings. Add what the reader should see when the step is done correctly.Usually 6–10 steps. Break a long step into two.
6. Troubleshooting / tipsThe two or three things most likely to go wrong, and how to fix them.Optional but impressive: shows strong audience awareness.
7. Conclusion1–2 sentences: what success looks like, plus aftercare (storing, cleaning up, what next).End on an encouraging note for a young reader.
Numbered steps or paragraphs?

Use numbered steps unless the task asks for a different form. If the task says 'write an email to your friend explaining how to…', keep the email layout (greeting, short friendly opening, sign-off) and put clearly sequenced steps inside it — numbered, or one paragraph per stage with First, Next, Finally.

Plan with G.A.A.P. in the first 3–4 minutes
  • G — Genre: instructions / guide (layout: title, list, numbered steps, subheadings).
  • A — Audience: who follows them? A beginner needs every step, and a Year 7 student needs school words explained.
  • A — Area of content: every bullet in the task becomes a section (equipment, steps, care, safety…).
  • P — Purpose: to enable the reader to do the task alone and safely.

How to start

  1. Outcome first: By the end of this guide, you will have a working model volcano that really erupts.
  2. Why it is worth doing: Fresh mint makes a glass of tea taste wonderful, and growing it yourself is easier than you think.
  3. Time and difficulty: This guide explains how to set up your school email account in six steps. It takes about ten minutes.
  4. Speak to the reader's situation: Starting at a new school is confusing, but finding your way around the library does not have to be.

How to end

  1. Describe success: Your bird feeder is now complete; within a week, the first sparrows should arrive.
  2. Aftercare: Wash the brushes in warm water and store them upright to dry.
  3. What next: Once you are confident, try the more advanced version on page two of the booklet.
  4. Encourage: Enjoy your first homemade loaf — you made it yourself!

4. Useful phrases and sentence starters

Introducing the task

  • This guide explains how to [task] in [number] straightforward steps.
  • By the end of this guide, you will have [result].
  • The whole process takes approximately [time] and needs no special equipment.
  • You will need an adult's help for [stage].

Listing equipment

  • Before you begin, gather the following:
  • You will need [quantity] of [material], along with [tool].
  • Make sure everything listed below is within reach before you start.

Moving through the steps

  • To begin with, [verb] the [object].
  • Start by [verb + -ing] the [object] until [result].
  • Next, [verb] the [object] so that [result].
  • Once [previous step] is complete, [verb]…
  • While the [object] is [state], [verb] the [second object].
  • Repeat steps [x] to [y] for the second [object].

Being precise

  • Measure exactly [quantity] — accuracy matters more than speed here.
  • The [object] should now be [description]. If it is not, repeat step [number].
  • Press gently but firmly until you feel it click.

Warnings

  • Warning: never [action] while [condition].
  • Take care not to [hazard], as this may cause [consequence].
  • Ask an adult to supervise this step.
  • Under no circumstances should you [dangerous action].

Tips and alternatives

  • If [problem], simply [solution] and continue from step [number].
  • For a neater finish, you may wish to [optional action].
  • If you do not have [item], [alternative] works equally well.

Finishing

  • Finally, [verb] the [object] and leave it to [process] for [time].
  • Your [item] is now ready to use.
  • Store the finished [item] in [place] until you need it.

5. Strong sample

Sample: a 'how to' guide for younger students560 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school is holding a Green Week. Write a set of instructions for the school website explaining to younger students how to grow a herb, such as mint, in a pot at home. You should include: • what they will need • the steps to follow • how to look after the plant • at least one safety point.

How to Grow Your Own Mint: A Beginner's Guide

Fresh mint makes a glass of tea taste wonderful, and growing it yourself is far easier than most people think. This guide explains how to turn three shop-bought mint stems into a healthy plant in eight simple steps. Planting takes about thirty minutes; after that, your plant needs only five minutes of care each day. No gardening experience is needed, although you should ask an adult to help you with the scissors and with lifting the full pot.

What You Will Need

• one plastic or clay pot, about 20 cm wide, with drainage holes in the bottom

• a saucer or tray for the pot to stand on

• about three litres of potting compost

• three healthy mint stems, each about 10 cm long, from a fresh bunch

• a glass of clean water, a pair of scissors and a small watering can

Safety First

Ask an adult to supervise whenever you use scissors. Wear gloves when you handle compost and always wash your hands afterwards: compost can contain tiny germs. Never place the pot on top of a balcony wall, where a gust of wind could knock it onto someone below.

Stage 1: Growing Roots

1. Using the scissors, cut each stem just below a leaf joint (the point where the leaves grow out of the stem). Remove all the leaves from the bottom 5 cm of each stem.

2. Stand the stems in the glass of water so that the bare part is covered but no leaves are under water. Place the glass on a bright windowsill, out of strong afternoon sun.

3. Change the water every two days. After about ten days, thin white roots should appear. Wait until they are at least 2 cm long before you move on to Stage 2.

Stage 2: Planting

4. Place the pot on its saucer and fill it with compost to 3 cm below the rim. Press the compost down gently with your fingertips; do not pack it hard, or the roots will struggle to spread.

5. Make three holes, each as deep as your finger, evenly spaced around the pot. Lower one cutting into each hole, then firm the compost around it so that the stem stands upright.

6. Water slowly until drops appear from the drainage holes. Empty the saucer after ten minutes — mint hates sitting in a puddle.

Stage 3: Caring for Your Plant

7. Keep the pot where it receives four to six hours of morning sunlight. Every morning, push a finger 2 cm into the soil to test it: dry soil needs water, but damp soil should be left alone.

8. Once the plant is about 15 cm tall, pinch off the top two leaves of each stem. This encourages it to grow bushy rather than tall and thin.

Troubleshooting

If the leaves turn yellow, you are probably watering too much, so let the soil dry out a little between waterings. If the leaves droop every afternoon, move the pot to a shadier spot, because the summer sun can be too strong even for mint.

Within a month, you should have a healthy, bushy plant covered in bright green leaves. Pick a few whenever you need them, always from the top, and your mint will keep growing for many months. Enjoy your first glass of home-grown mint tea!

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: every bullet is covered — what they need (list), the steps (Stages 1–2), looking after the plant (Stage 3 and Troubleshooting), safety (Safety First). It is pitched at a young beginner: the leaf joint is explained in brackets, the finger test replaces technical language, and adult help is mentioned. The reader is told what success looks like at several points.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise verbs (pinch off, firm, lower, supervise, encourage) and exact measurements (20 cm, three litres, roots 2 cm long, every two days, four to six hours). Words chosen for a young reader (bushy, droop, puddle).
  • Structure of texts 7/7: the full seven-part shape — title, introduction (outcome, time, difficulty), equipment list, safety section, method grouped under three staged subheadings, troubleshooting, conclusion with aftercare. Steps are numbered in strict order, and sequence words link them (after about ten days, once, before you move on, within a month).
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7 (sentences): imperatives used correctly and varied with fronted clauses (Using the scissors, … / Once the plant is… / If the leaves turn yellow, …) and explaining sentences (This encourages it to…). A short sentence gives the warning force ('Never place the pot…').
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7 (punctuation): colon for explanation (wash your hands afterwards: compost can contain tiny germs / push a finger 2 cm into the soil to test it: dry soil needs water…). Semicolons joining two closely related sentences (Planting takes about thirty minutes; after that…). Brackets for extra information (the point where the leaves grow out of the stem). A single dash for emphasis (— mint hates sitting in a puddle). Consistent bullets. Apostrophe in Beginner's.
  • Word structure (spelling) 3/3: supervise, drainage, encourages, troubleshooting, evenly, afterwards, shop-bought, home-grown all correct.
  • Note that the answer is well over 350 words even though the steps are short: the introduction, safety section, explanations of 'why', troubleshooting and conclusion carry the length.

6. Common mistakes

  • Describing instead of instructing: 'The library is a lovely, quiet place' gives no action. Start steps with a verb.
  • Vague amounts: 'add some water', 'cook it for a while'. Give numbers.
  • Steps in the wrong order or a missing step (forgetting to switch the oven on until step 6).
  • Warnings after the step: by then the accident has happened. Put them before.
  • Two or three actions crammed into one step — split them.
  • Writing for the wrong reader: technical words for a seven-year-old, or explaining the obvious to an expert.
  • Only commands: no introduction, no reasons, no conclusion — too short and no sentence range.
  • Inconsistent layout: some steps numbered and some not, or bullets mixed with dashes.
Timing (about 35 minutes for writing)
  • 3–4 minutes: G.A.A.P., then list the stages and the equipment in your plan.
  • 25–27 minutes: write title, introduction, list, safety, steps, tips and conclusion (350+ words).
  • 4–5 minutes: check the order of steps, numbers and units, spelling and that every bullet is covered.
Checklist before you hand in
  • Title names the task and the result.
  • Introduction says what, how long and how difficult.
  • Equipment list with exact quantities comes before the steps.
  • Safety point placed before the risky step.
  • Numbered steps in strict order, one action each, starting with imperatives.
  • At least three steps begin with Once / If / Before / When / Using…
  • Exact numbers, sizes and times — no 'some' or 'a bit'.
  • Troubleshooting tip and a conclusion describing the finished result.

Practice tasks

  1. Task 1: Write a guide for new Year 7 students explaining how to get ready for school the night before, so that their mornings are calm. Include: • what to prepare • the order to do it in • one tip for when things go wrong. Write a plan.

    Show answer
    Title: 'How to Have a Stress-Free School Morning: A Night-Before Checklist'. Introduction: the result (leave home on time, nothing forgotten), takes 15 minutes each evening. What you need: timetable, school bag, planner, uniform, water bottle. Stage 1 — Check: 1. Look at tomorrow's timetable. 2. Tick off homework due in your planner. Stage 2 — Pack: 3. Pack books in timetable order. 4. Add PE kit if needed. 5. Fill the water bottle and put it in the fridge. Stage 3 — Prepare yourself: 6. Lay out your uniform. 7. Set your alarm and put the phone across the room. 8. Sleep by 10 p.m. Tip: if you forget something in the morning, check the list on your door before leaving. Conclusion: a calm morning and a better start to the day.

  2. Task 2: Your friend from abroad is visiting Egypt and wants to make a traditional Egyptian dish or drink. Write an email explaining how to make it. Include: • the ingredients • the method • one safety point. Write the opening and the first three steps.

    Show answer
    Example: 'Hi Adam,' (on its own line), then: 'I'm so glad you want to try making karkadeh — it's the perfect drink for hot weather, and it only takes twenty minutes. Here's exactly what to do. You will need: 50 g of dried hibiscus flowers, one litre of water, four tablespoons of sugar and a sieve. Be careful: boiling water can burn badly, so use oven gloves. 1. Rinse the hibiscus flowers in cold water to remove any dust. 2. Boil the litre of water in a large pan. 3. Once it is boiling, turn off the heat and add the flowers; leave them to soak for ten minutes (the water should turn deep red).' (Email greeting and friendly tone kept, and steps still precise and numbered.)

  3. Make these steps precise: 'Put some flour in a bowl. Add eggs and mix it for a while. Cook it until it's done.'

    Show answer
    Example: '1. Sift 200 g of plain flour into a large mixing bowl. 2. Make a small hollow in the centre and crack in two eggs. 3. Whisk for two minutes until the mixture is smooth and has no lumps. 4. Pour one ladle of batter into a hot, lightly oiled pan and cook for one minute on each side, until golden brown.'

  4. Rewrite this badly placed warning so that it is useful: '4. Pour the boiling water into the cup. Be careful because it is hot.'

    Show answer
    'Warning: boiling water can scald. Hold the cup by its handle and ask an adult to help if the kettle is heavy. 4. Pour the boiling water slowly into the cup, stopping 1 cm below the rim.' The warning now comes before the step and says exactly what to do.
