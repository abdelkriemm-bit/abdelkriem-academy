Checkpoint 8 · Reading: understanding texts

Language, Devices and Their Effects

Every figure of speech and literary device with all the effects you can write, tone and mood word banks, connotation, and a step-by-step method for “explain the effect” questions with model answers.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

Writers choose every word on purpose. Language questions in both papers ask you to spot a writer’s choice (a simile, a strong verb, a repeated phrase) and, more importantly, to explain its effect on the reader. This page gives you every figure of speech and literary device you need, the possible effects of each one, tone and mood word banks, connotation, and a reliable method for “explain the effect” questions with model answers.

How this topic is tested
  • Identify a device: “Give an example of personification from paragraph 3.” / “Tick the device used in line 7.”
  • Effect of a single word: “Explain the effect of the word ‘…’ in line 12.”
  • Effect of a phrase or device: “Why does the writer compare the boy to a machine?” / “What is the effect of the simile in line 5?”
  • Tone and mood: “Which phrase shows the mood changes?” / “What is the writer’s tone in the final paragraph?”
  • These appear in Paper 2 (fiction) most often, but Paper 1 also asks about the effect of rhetorical questions, emotive words, repetition and headlines.

1. Naming is not explaining

Naming the device is the doorway. The marks are behind it. Compare:

Identifying only (few or no marks)Explaining the effect (marks)
“The writer uses a simile.”“The simile ‘the wind howled like a wounded wolf’ makes the storm sound alive, wild and in pain, which makes the night feel dangerous.”
“There is personification.”“‘The house watched them leave’ makes the house seem alive and suspicious, so the reader feels the children are not safe even as they escape.”
“It is repeated.”“Repeating ‘waiting’ three times stretches the moment, so the reader feels how slow and frustrating the delay is.”

2. Comparisons: simile, metaphor, personification, pathetic fallacy

DeviceWhat it isOriginal example
SimileCompares two things using “like” or “as”The lake lay as flat and silver as a mirror.
MetaphorSays one thing IS another (no like/as)The classroom was a beehive before the bell.
PersonificationGives human actions or feelings to something non-humanThe old car coughed and complained up the hill.
Pathetic fallacyWeather or nature reflects a character’s feelings or the moodAs Ali read the letter, grey clouds swallowed the sun.
Why does the author use a simile / metaphor in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To help the reader picture something clearly by comparing it with something familiar.
  • To make the description more vivid and memorable.
  • To show a quality of the thing described (size, speed, colour, sound, strength) — say which quality.
  • To show how a character feels (e.g. “my heart was a drum” shows fear or excitement).
  • To create a mood (peaceful, threatening, joyful) through the image chosen.
  • To show the writer’s attitude: a positive comparison praises, a negative one criticises.
  • A metaphor is stronger than a simile: it says the thing really IS the other thing, so the image feels more powerful and certain.
Why does the author use personification in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To make an object, place or force of nature seem alive.
  • To make it seem threatening or powerful (a storm that “attacks”), creating fear or tension.
  • To make it seem friendly or comforting (a house that “welcomes”), creating a warm mood.
  • To help the reader picture movement or sound vividly.
  • To reflect the character’s feelings onto the setting.
  • To make the description more original and engaging than a plain statement.
Why does the author use pathetic fallacy in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To reflect the character’s feelings through the weather (storm = anger or fear, sunshine = happiness).
  • To create the mood of the scene (gloomy, tense, cheerful).
  • To foreshadow what will happen next (dark clouds before bad news).
  • To show a change: when the weather changes, the character’s mood or situation is changing too.

3. Sound devices

DeviceWhat it isOriginal example
AlliterationThe same sound at the start of nearby wordsThe silent, silver sea… / Big, brave and bold
AssonanceThe same vowel sound repeated inside nearby wordsThe low, slow moan of the boat
SibilanceRepeated “s” or “sh” sounds (a type of alliteration/consonance)The snake slid softly through the sand.
OnomatopoeiaA word that sounds like the noise it describescrash, sizzle, hiss, thud, buzz, crackle
Why does the author use alliteration / assonance / sibilance in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To draw the reader’s attention to that phrase and make it stand out.
  • To make the phrase memorable (useful in slogans, headlines and speeches).
  • To create a rhythm or sound that matches the meaning — hard sounds (b, d, k) feel harsh or forceful, soft sounds (s, l, m) feel calm, gentle or sinister.
  • Sibilance can suggest whispering, hissing, the sea or a quiet threat.
  • Long vowel sounds (assonance) can slow the line down and create a sad, calm or heavy mood.
  • To emphasise the words that carry the key idea.
Why does the author use onomatopoeia in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To let the reader hear the sound, making the scene feel real and immediate.
  • To make the description more vivid and dramatic.
  • A sudden loud word (“CRASH!”) can shock the reader or create tension.
  • A gentle sound (“the kettle hummed”) can create a calm, homely atmosphere.

4. Exaggeration, imagery and symbolism

DeviceWhat it isOriginal example
HyperboleDeliberate exaggeration, not meant literallyI’ve walked a thousand miles today.
ImageryDescription that appeals to the senses so the reader can imagine itthe sharp smell of lemons and the warm crunch of fresh bread
SymbolismAn object, colour or image that stands for a bigger ideaA locked gate = being shut out · a candle = hope · a white flag = surrender
SenseKind of imageryExample
SightVisualLanterns swung in the dark, throwing gold circles on the wall.
SoundAuditorySomewhere a dog barked, and the gate creaked.
SmellOlfactoryThe kitchen smelled of cumin and burnt sugar.
TasteGustatoryThe tea was sweet and bitter at the same time.
TouchTactileThe rope was rough and wet against her palms.
Why does the author use hyperbole / imagery / symbolism in line X? (all possible answers)
  • Hyperbole: to emphasise how extreme something is or feels · to show strong emotion (frustration, excitement) · to create humour · in persuasion, to make a problem seem urgent.
  • Imagery: to help the reader imagine the scene with their senses as if they were there · to create atmosphere and mood · to make the description realistic and vivid · to show what a character notices and feels.
  • Symbolism: to suggest a deeper meaning without stating it · to link an object with a feeling or theme (freedom, hope, loss) · to hint at what will happen · to make the reader think more deeply about the message.

5. Repetition, rhetorical questions and the rule of three

DeviceWhat it isOriginal example
RepetitionA word, phrase or sentence pattern used again for effectWe waited. We waited all night. We waited until the sky turned grey.
Rhetorical questionA question asked for effect, not for an answerHow many more trees must we lose before we act?
Rule of threeA list or pattern of three for impactOur park is cleaner, safer and greener than ever.
Direct addressSpeaking straight to the reader as “you”You can make a difference today.
Emotive languageWords chosen to make the reader feel strong emotionhelpless, starving puppies abandoned in the cold
Why does the author use repetition in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To emphasise a key idea so the reader cannot miss it.
  • To show a character’s strong feeling (panic, determination, obsession).
  • To slow the pace and stretch out a moment (waiting, boredom, dread).
  • To build tension or rhythm, especially just before a climax.
  • In persuasion and speeches, to make the message memorable and powerful.
Why does the author use a rhetorical question in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To make the reader stop and think about the issue.
  • To involve the reader directly, as if in a conversation.
  • To suggest that the answer is obvious, so the reader agrees with the writer.
  • To challenge the reader or make them feel responsible (in persuasive writing).
  • In fiction, to show a character’s confusion, doubt or worry and to create suspense about what will happen.
Why does the author use the rule of three in line X? (all possible answers)
  • To emphasise a point by giving several pieces of support at once.
  • To create a satisfying, memorable rhythm.
  • To make the writer sound confident and convincing.
  • To show how much of something there is (many problems, many benefits).
  • Often used to build up to the most important idea, placed last.

6. Contrast, oxymoron and foreshadowing

DeviceWhat it isOriginal example
Contrast (juxtaposition)Placing two very different things close togetherOutside, the street was bright and noisy. Inside, the hospital room was dim and silent.
OxymoronTwo opposite words side by sidea deafening silence, bittersweet, a cheerful goodbye
ForeshadowingAn early hint of what will happen laterNobody noticed the small crack in the dam that morning.
Why does the author use contrast / oxymoron / foreshadowing in line X? (all possible answers)
  • Contrast: to highlight the difference between two people, places or feelings · to make each one stand out more · to show a change (before and after) · to create tension or surprise.
  • Oxymoron: to show mixed or confused feelings · to describe something unusual or hard to put into words · to make the phrase striking and memorable · to make the reader think.
  • Foreshadowing: to create suspense, because the reader expects something to happen · to build a sense of unease or dread · to prepare the reader so the later event feels believable · to make the reader curious about how the hint will connect to the ending.

A mood change often comes with a change in the setting or the weather, a sudden short sentence, or a new character arriving. When asked which phrase shows the mood changing, look for words such as “Suddenly”, “But then”, “Without warning”, or a change from soft to harsh vocabulary.

8. Connotation: why this word and not another?

Denotation is the dictionary meaning of a word. Connotation is the feeling or idea the word carries. Writers choose words for their connotations. “The man walked” is neutral. “The man stormed” suggests anger. “The man crept” suggests secrecy or fear.

Single-word effect method
  • 1. Meaning: what does the word literally mean here?
  • 2. Connotation: what does it suggest or remind us of (danger, softness, power, an animal, a machine)?
  • 3. Effect: what does that make the reader picture or feel about this character, place or event?
  • 4. Compare (optional but strong): why is it better than a plain word? “‘Crawled’ rather than ‘drove’ suggests the traffic was painfully slow.”

9. The method for every effect question

  1. Identify the writer’s choice in the exact lines given (a word, a device, an image, a repetition). If the question names it, start there.
  2. Quote it briefly and exactly — one word or a short phrase.
  3. Say what it does: what is being compared, exaggerated, repeated or suggested.
  4. Explain the effect on the reader: what we picture, feel or understand about the character, setting or idea — be specific to this text.
  5. Match the marks: for 2 marks, one point fully explained or two shorter points. For more marks, develop more than one effect (picture + feeling + link to the character or mood).
Vague answers that earn nothing
  • “It makes the reader want to read on.” — true of every text. Instead say what the reader wants to know: “the reader is left wondering who knocked on the door at midnight.”
  • “It makes it more interesting / exciting / descriptive.” — say how and why.
  • “It creates a good image.” — say what the image is and what it suggests.
  • “It emphasises it.” — say what exactly is emphasised and why it matters.
  • Test: could you write this sentence without reading the text? If yes, it is too vague.

Starting your explanation

  • The word “…” suggests that…
  • The writer compares … to …, which shows…
  • This makes the reader picture…
  • This creates a feeling of…
  • The image of … highlights…

Developing the effect

  • This emphasises how … the character feels.
  • As a result, the setting seems…
  • This hints that something … is about to happen.
  • The connotations of “…” are…, so…
  • This contrasts with…, which makes … stand out.

10. Model answers

Text A (fiction): “The wind clawed at the tent all night. Inside, Tarek lay awake, listening to the canvas snap and shudder like a trapped bird.”

Question 1: Explain the effect of the word “clawed”. (1 mark)

Model: “Clawed” suggests the wind is a wild animal attacking the tent with sharp claws, which makes the storm seem violent and dangerous.

Question 2: Why does the writer compare the canvas to “a trapped bird”? (2 marks)

Model: The simile makes the reader picture the canvas flapping wildly and helplessly, like wings beating to escape. It also reflects Tarek’s own feelings: he is stuck inside, frightened and unable to get away from the storm.

Text B (non-fiction): “Every year, eight million tonnes of plastic pour into our oceans. Can we really call ourselves the guardians of this planet?”

Question 3: Explain the effect of the rhetorical question. (2 marks)

Model: The question challenges readers directly and makes them feel guilty, because the answer is clearly “no” after the shocking statistic. The word “guardians” reminds us that we are supposed to protect the planet, so the question pushes readers to take responsibility.

Model longer answer: how is mood created?115 words · 4/4 (three language choices quoted, each effect explained precisely)
Task: Read the paragraph: “The street had emptied by the time Laila turned the corner. A single lamp flickered above the bakery, its light trembling on the wet stones. Somewhere behind her, footsteps stopped when she stopped. She walked faster. So did they.” How does the writer create a tense mood in this paragraph? Refer to the writer’s use of language. (4 marks)

The writer creates tension first through the setting. The street had “emptied”, so Laila is alone, and the reader feels she is vulnerable because nobody could help her.

The image of a lamp that “flickered” with light “trembling on the wet stones” is personification: the light seems nervous, as if it is shaking with fear, and because it could go out at any moment, the reader worries Laila will be left in darkness.

Finally, the short sentences “She walked faster. So did they.” speed up the pace and copy her quick steps. The repetition of the pattern shows that the unseen follower is copying her exactly, which makes the reader fear she is being chased.

Why this gets the marks
  • Each point quotes a short, exact piece of evidence (“emptied”, “flickered”, “trembling”, “She walked faster. So did they.”).
  • Devices are named (personification, short sentences, repetition) but the focus is on the effect.
  • Effects are specific to this text (alone, darkness, being chased), never “it makes it exciting”.
  • Every point links back to the question’s focus: a tense mood.
  • Written in clear paragraphs, one language choice per paragraph.

11. Practice questions

  1. Name the device: “The traffic crawled along the bridge like a tired snail.” Give two possible effects.

    Show answer
    Simile. Effects: it helps the reader picture how slowly the traffic moved, and it creates a feeling of frustration and boredom (“tired” suggests the drivers are worn out).

  2. Name the device: “Her words were knives.” Why might the writer use it?

    Show answer
    Metaphor. It shows her words hurt deeply and sharply, as if they cut the listener. Saying the words ARE knives makes the hurt seem stronger than a simile would.

  3. “The sea swallowed the little boat.” Explain the effect of “swallowed”.

    Show answer
    “Swallowed” makes the sea seem like a huge, hungry creature that eats the boat completely, suggesting the boat disappeared suddenly and the sea is powerful and dangerous.

  4. “The sun smiled on the children as they ran out of school.” Name the device and give two possible effects.

    Show answer
    Personification. It makes the weather seem friendly and warm, and it creates a happy, carefree mood that matches the children’s feelings at the end of the school day.

  5. “Rain hammered on the roof as Nour read the hospital report.” What is the device and why is it used?

    Show answer
    Pathetic fallacy. The violent rain reflects Nour’s worry and shock and creates a gloomy, tense mood. It may also hint that the news is bad.

  6. “Crack! The branch gave way.” Why does the writer use onomatopoeia here?

    Show answer
    So the reader hears the sudden sound, which makes the moment feel immediate and shocking and builds tension about whether someone will fall.

  7. “Soft, silent snowflakes settled on the sleeping city.” Name the sound device and its effect.

    Show answer
    Sibilance / alliteration with the “s” sound. The soft hissing sound matches the quiet snowfall and creates a calm, peaceful mood.

  8. “I’m so hungry I could eat a camel!” Name the device and say why it is used.

    Show answer
    Hyperbole. It exaggerates to show how extremely hungry the speaker feels and adds humour.

  9. “We need cleaner air, safer streets and greener parks.” Name the device and give two effects.

    Show answer
    Rule of three. It emphasises how many improvements are needed and creates a memorable rhythm that makes the writer sound confident and persuasive.

  10. “Do we want our grandchildren to ask why we did nothing?” Why does the writer use this question?

    Show answer
    It is a rhetorical question that makes readers imagine being blamed in the future, so they feel guilty and responsible, and the obvious answer (“no”) pushes them to agree they must act.

  11. “It was a sweet sorrow to leave the village.” Name the device and explain.

    Show answer
    Oxymoron (“sweet sorrow”). It shows mixed feelings: sad to leave, but with happy memories or excitement about the future.

  12. “Nobody locked the back door that night.” What might this sentence do near the start of a story?

    Show answer
    It foreshadows trouble, such as a break-in. It creates suspense because the reader expects the unlocked door to matter later.

  13. Explain the connotations: “The children swarmed into the playground.”

    Show answer
    “Swarmed” suggests insects such as bees, so the children seem noisy, numerous and uncontrollable, moving all at once.

  14. Identify the tone: “Oh brilliant, the lift is broken again. Ten floors of stairs — just what I needed.”

    Show answer
    Sarcastic: the speaker says “brilliant” and “just what I needed” but means the opposite, showing annoyance.

  15. What mood is created? “Lanterns glowed along the river, and the smell of roasted corn drifted between the laughing families.”

    Show answer
    A warm, joyful and peaceful mood, created by soft light (“glowed”), pleasant smells and the sound of “laughing families”.

  16. A student answers: “The simile makes the reader want to read on.” Rewrite it for the simile “the corridor stretched ahead like a dark throat”.

    Show answer
    Example: The simile makes the corridor seem like the inside of a creature’s mouth, as if it could swallow the character. This creates fear, and the reader worries about what waits at the end of the corridor.

  17. “The head teacher’s voice cut through the noise.” Explain the effect of “cut”.

    Show answer
    “Cut” suggests something sharp and sudden, so her voice is clear, firm and powerful enough to stop the noise immediately, which shows her authority.

  18. Symbolism: In a story, a girl keeps her late grandmother’s key on a chain around her neck. What might the key symbolise?

    Show answer
    It may symbolise her connection to her grandmother, her memories, or a family home and belonging that she does not want to lose.
