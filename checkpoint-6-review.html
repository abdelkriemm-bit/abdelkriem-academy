Checkpoint 6 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Review Writing

How to review a book, film, toy or product, place or event for Paper 1 — a toy review has been set in a real paper — with features, a plan for every type, phrases, vocabulary and two strong samples.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

1. What is a review?

A review tells the reader what you think of something — a book, a film, a toy or product, a place or an event — and why. It gives your honest opinion, backs it up with specific examples, and ends with a recommendation, so the reader can decide whether to read it, watch it, buy it or visit it.

A review has two jobs at the same time: to inform (what is it? what is it like?) and to advise (is it worth it, and for whom?). It is not a retelling of the whole story and it is not an advert. A good review is fair: it praises the good points and politely mentions at least one weaker point.

QuestionAnswer for a review
PurposeTo inform the reader about something and give a fair, supported opinion and a recommendation.
AudienceUsually readers of a children's magazine, a school magazine or newsletter, or a website — often children your age and their parents.
ToneLively, friendly and personal, but in correct, clear English. No slang.
Person and tenseFirst person for your opinion (I loved…, I would recommend…). Present tense for what the thing is and does (The story follows…, The robot rolls…). Past tense for your own experience (When I opened the box…, We visited…).
Where it appearsPaper 1 (Non-fiction), Section B Writing, 25 marks.
Reviews are set in real Checkpoint papers
  • A review of a toy for a children's magazine has been set on a Checkpoint Paper 1. A toy or product review could appear again, so learn the product plan below very well.
  • The task may ask for a review of any type: a book, a film, a toy or game, a place (a museum, park, restaurant) or an event (a school trip, a sports day, a concert).
  • Read the bullet points in the task carefully. They may ask for three things: what it is / what it was like, what was good and not so good, and who you would recommend it to. Each one needs its own paragraph.
  • If the task says a toy you played with when you were younger, write about your memories in the past tense and say whether you would recommend it to children today.

Where it sits in the exam and how it is marked

Year 6 students sit Cambridge Primary Checkpoint English (0058). There are two papers: Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction. Each paper lasts 1 hour and has Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks). About 30 minutes is suggested for each section. The review is a Paper 1 task, because it is non-fiction.

Your writing is marked out of 25 in five categories. Here is what each category means for a review. The teacher's older names are in brackets.

CategoryMarksWhat the top band needs in this genre
Creation of texts (content, purpose and audience)5Clearly a review, not a story or an advert. Every bullet point in the task is covered. Opinions are developed with specific examples and reasons, and the viewpoint stays clear from start to finish. Written for the named reader (e.g. children who read the magazine).
Vocabulary and language3Precise evaluative words instead of good/bad/nice: gripping, clever, sturdy, fiddly, disappointing, value for money, hilarious. Language suits the reader.
Structure of texts (text structure)7A title, a hooking introduction, a short description or summary, strengths, a weakness, a verdict with a recommendation. Each paragraph does one job, and connectives such as However, In addition, Overall link them.
Grammar and punctuation (sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7A range of sentence types used for effect (a short punchy verdict, longer complex sentences for detail). Accurate tenses — present for the thing, past for your experience. Correct commas, apostrophes, colons, dashes and inverted commas for quotations.
Word structure (spelling)3Correct spelling of everyday and ambitious words: recommend, disappointing, character, definitely, favourite, colourful, instructions.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • Wrong text type: If the task asks for a review of a toy and you write a story about a birthday party, or an advert that only praises the toy, that is the wrong text type. If you write the wrong kind of text, Creation of texts can be capped at 1 mark and Structure of texts at 3 (or 2), however good your English is.
  • Too short: 20 words or fewer scores 0, and an answer of 21–60 words can earn no more than 7 marks out of 25. Train yourself to write 250–350 words in about 30 minutes.
  • Markers use best fit: they read the whole answer and choose the band that matches it best. One small mistake will not ruin a strong answer, but many careless ones will pull it down.

How to get full marks: checklist

  • Name the thing in the title and in the first paragraph (what it is, who made or wrote it, who it is for).
  • Hook the reader in the first sentence — a question, a memory or a bold opinion.
  • Describe or summarise briefly — one paragraph — and never give away the ending of a book or film.
  • Give at least two strengths and one weakness, each with a specific example and a reason.
  • Use at least five evaluative words that are better than good or bad.
  • Keep your tenses under control: present for the thing, past for your experience.
  • Finish with a clear verdict and a recommendation to a specific group (children aged 8–10 who love animals…).
  • A star rating at the end is a nice extra, but it never replaces the reasons.

Tense trap

Use the present tense for what the book, film or toy is and does (e.g. The robot flashes its eyes / The story moves quickly). Use the past tense for your own experience (e.g. It took me three afternoons to build it / We visited on a Friday). Switching tenses for no reason loses Grammar marks.

3. How to structure a review

Use this five-paragraph plan for every review. The opening and the verdict never change. Only the middle changes with the type. In 30 minutes aim for 250–350 words.

ParagraphJobWhat to write
TitleName itThe name of the book/film/toy/place/event + a catchy phrase.
1. IntroductionHook + key factsA hook. What it is, who made/wrote it, who it is for. Why you chose it or how you got it.
2. Description / summaryInformBook/film: the main character, the setting and the problem — no ending. Toy/product: what is in the box, what it does. Place/event: what is there, what happened.
3. StrengthsWhat you likedTwo or three good points. Each one: the point → a specific example → why it matters to you.
4. WeaknessWhat was not so goodOne or two weaker points, said politely and fairly, with an example. Start with However or On the other hand.
5. VerdictRecommendYour overall opinion in one sentence. Who exactly would enjoy it, and why. Optional star rating. A neat final line.

What changes with each type

TypeParagraph 1: key factsParagraph 2: describeParagraphs 3–4: judge
BookTitle, author, genre (adventure, mystery…), age groupMain character, setting, the problem — no spoilersCharacters, plot and pace, description, the ending's effect (without telling it)
FilmTitle, director, type of film, age rating if knownMain character and the problem — no spoilersActing, story, music, special effects, funny or exciting scenes
Toy / game / productName, maker, age group, how you got it (a present, saved up), price in words (quite expensive, good value)What is in the box, how it works, how long it takes to set up or learnFun, how long it keeps you interested, how strong it is, safety, instructions, value for money, batteries/parts
Place (museum, park, restaurant)Name, where it is, why you went, who you went withWhat there is to see and do, what it looks likeHighlights, staff, food, cleanliness, crowds, price, getting there
Event (school trip, concert, sports day)Name, date, place, who it was forWhat happened, in order but brieflyBest moments, organisation, anything that went wrong
Plan with G.A.A.P. in the first 3–4 minutes
  • G — Genre: a review (title, introduction, summary, strengths, weakness, verdict).
  • A — Audience: a children's magazine = readers your age, so friendly and lively. A school newsletter = pupils and parents, so a little more formal.
  • A — Area of content: underline every bullet point in the task and give each one a paragraph.
  • P — Purpose: inform + give an honest opinion + recommend.

How to start: openings that work

  • A question: Have you ever wanted to build a robot that really moves?
  • A memory: I will never forget the morning I unwrapped my first set of building blocks.
  • A bold opinion: This is, without doubt, the funniest film I have seen this year.
  • Imagine…: Imagine waking up alone on an island with only your dog for company.
  • A surprising detail: Inside one small box were more than two hundred pieces — and not a single one was wasted.

How to end: verdicts that work

  • Clear recommendation: I would recommend it to children aged eight to ten who love animals and adventure.
  • Who should not choose it: However, if you prefer quiet stories, this may not be the book for you.
  • Star rating: My rating: four stars out of five — it would be five if the batteries were included!
  • Final advice: Visit early in the morning, before the crowds arrive, and do not miss the dinosaur hall.
  • Link back to the hook: So, if you have ever dreamed of building a robot, this is the perfect place to start.

5. Strong sample 1: toy review

A toy review has been set on a real Checkpoint paper, so study this one closely. Then read the notes to see where each mark is earned.

Sample 1: toy review for a children's magazine300 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: A children's magazine has a page called Toy Talk, where young readers review toys and games. Write a review of a toy or game you have owned. In your review you should: • describe the toy and what it does • explain what you liked and did not like about it • say who you would recommend it to and why.

RoboBuild Junior: The Robot You Build Yourself

When I unwrapped my birthday present last year and saw a shiny blue robot on the box, I could hardly wait to open it. RoboBuild Junior is a building kit made by the toy company Brightspark, and it is designed for children aged eight to twelve. It is more expensive than most toys, so I wanted to find out whether it was really worth the money.

Inside the box, there are more than two hundred plastic pieces, a small motor, two wheels and a colourful instruction booklet. You follow the steps to build a robot that can roll forwards, turn its head and flash its eyes. When it is finished, it stands about thirty centimetres tall.

The best thing about RoboBuild Junior is how proud it makes you feel. It took me three afternoons to build, and when my robot finally rolled across the kitchen floor, my whole family clapped. The instructions are clear: every step has a large picture, and the pieces are sorted into numbered bags, so nothing gets lost. I also love that you can take it apart and build two other models, a racing car and a crane, which means the fun does not end after one day.

However, the toy is not perfect. The motor needs four batteries that are not included — a disappointing surprise on my birthday morning! In addition, some of the tiny pieces are fiddly to push together, and younger children might need help from an adult.

Overall, RoboBuild Junior is a clever, exciting kit that teaches you how machines work while you play. I would recommend it to patient builders aged nine and over who enjoy science and a challenge. My rating: four stars out of five. Just remember to buy the batteries first!

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: clearly a review, not a story or an advert. All three bullet points are covered: describe (paragraph 2), liked and did not like (paragraphs 3 and 4), recommend (paragraph 5). The voice is friendly and lively for children who read the magazine, and the opinion stays clear throughout.
  • Vocabulary 3/3: precise evaluative words — shiny, colourful, proud, clever, fiddly, disappointing, patient — and product words (motor, models, numbered bags). 'worth the money' shows the reviewer is thinking about value.
  • Structure 7/7: a title with the name + a catchy phrase. Five paragraphs, each with one job (hook and facts → description → strengths → weaknesses → verdict). However and In addition link the paragraphs. The last line links back to the battery problem with humour.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7: present tense for the toy (it stands, the instructions are clear) and past tense for the writer's experience (It took me three afternoons). Complex sentences (When it is finished, it stands… / …which means the fun does not end…). A colon introduces an explanation (The instructions are clear: every step…) and the rating. A pair of commas adds extra information (…two other models, a racing car and a crane, which…). A single dash gives emphasis (— a disappointing surprise…). One exclamation mark used for effect at the end of each of two paragraphs, not everywhere.
  • Spelling 3/3: colourful, centimetres, disappointing, instruction, patient, recommend — British spelling throughout.

Strong sample 2: book review

Sample 2: book review for the school magazine307 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school magazine wants pupils to recommend good books to each other. Write a review of a book you have enjoyed. Say what the book is about, what you liked and did not like, and who you would recommend it to.

Kensuke's Kingdom by Michael Morpurgo: An Island Full of Surprises

Have you ever dreamed of being stranded on a desert island? Kensuke's Kingdom, an adventure novel by Michael Morpurgo, made me feel as if I were there myself. I chose it from our school library because I had enjoyed another book by the same author, and I finished it in less than a week.

The story is told by Michael, a boy who sets off to sail around the world with his parents and his dog, Stella, on a small yacht called the Peggy Sue. One stormy night, he is swept overboard and wakes up on a lonely island. At first, he believes he is completely alone, but someone is secretly leaving him food and water. That someone is Kensuke, an old Japanese man who has lived on the island for many years. I will not spoil what happens next!

What I loved most was the friendship between the boy and the old man. At the beginning, Kensuke seems unfriendly and strict, yet he teaches Michael how to fish and how to paint. Slowly, the two learn to trust each other; their friendship is the heart of the book. The descriptions of the island are also wonderful. I could almost hear the gibbons calling in the forest and feel the burning sand under my feet.

On the other hand, the first few chapters, about life on the boat, move quite slowly, and some readers might want the adventure to start sooner. A few parts are also sad, so very young readers may find them upsetting.

Overall, Kensuke's Kingdom is a gripping and thoughtful adventure. I would recommend it to readers aged nine to twelve who enjoy stories about survival, animals and friendship. I give it five stars, and I am already looking for my next Morpurgo book.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: a real review of a real children's novel. The summary gives the main character, setting and problem but stops before the ending (I will not spoil what happens next!). The recommendation names a specific audience (readers aged nine to twelve who enjoy survival, animals and friendship).
  • Vocabulary 3/3: stranded, swept overboard, unfriendly, strict, gripping, thoughtful, upsetting — exact words that show feelings and judgement. Sensory description (hear the gibbons calling… feel the burning sand) shows why the setting is strong.
  • Structure 7/7: hook question → key facts (title, author, genre, why chosen) → summary → strengths → weaknesses (On the other hand…) → verdict. Paragraphs are linked and balanced.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7: present tense for the story (The story is told by…, he wakes up…), past tense for the writer (I chose it… I finished it…). Relative clauses (a boy who sets off… / an old Japanese man who has lived…). Pairs of commas add extra information (…, an adventure novel by Michael Morpurgo, …). A semicolon joins two related sentences (…learn to trust each other; their friendship is the heart of the book). An apostrophe shows possession (Kensuke's Kingdom).
  • Spelling 3/3: adventure, stranded, overboard, descriptions, survival, thoughtful, wonderful.
Compare the two samples

Both samples use the same five-paragraph plan. Only paragraph 2 changes: the toy review describes what is in the box and what it does. The book review summarises the story without the ending. Learn the plan once, and you can review anything.

6. Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it loses marksFix it
Retelling the whole storyA review is not a summary, so Creation of texts stays low.One short summary paragraph — the rest is your judgement.
Giving away the endingIt spoils the story for the reader and shows poor audience awareness.Stop the summary at the main problem.
Only praise, no weaknessIt reads like an advert, not a review.Add one fair weakness with an example.
'It was good. It was nice. It was fun.'Weak vocabulary and no reasons.Use evaluative words + a specific example + why.
No recommendationMissing a key part of the task.Name who would enjoy it and why.
Mixing tensesGrammar errors.Present for the thing, past for your experience.
Writing a story about getting the toyWrong text type.Keep the memory to one or two sentences in the introduction.
Title not marked or no titleWeaker structure.Write a title with the name of the thing.
Timing (about 30 minutes for writing)
  • 3–4 minutes: G.A.A.P. and a five-box plan (one box per paragraph).
  • about 22 minutes: write 250–350 words.
  • 4 minutes: check spelling, tenses, apostrophes and that every bullet point is covered.
Checklist before you hand in
  • My title names what I am reviewing.
  • My introduction has a hook and the key facts.
  • My description or summary is short and has no spoilers.
  • I gave at least two strengths and one weakness, each with an example.
  • I used at least five evaluative words better than good/bad.
  • My tenses are correct: present for the thing, past for my experience.
  • I gave a clear recommendation to a specific audience.
  • I covered every bullet point in the task.

Practice tasks

  1. Write a review of a toy you loved when you were younger for a children's magazine. Say what it was like, why you enjoyed it, and whether children today would enjoy it too.

    Show answer
    Title: My Wooden Train Set: Still on Track! P1: memory hook (my grandfather gave it to me when I was four) + what it is. P2 (past tense): the tracks, the bridge, the little carriages. How I played with it. P3: strengths — sturdy wood that never broke, endless ways to build the track, played with my cousins. P4: weakness — the tracks were heavy to tidy away, and the paint on the engine chipped. P5: verdict — yes, children today would still love it because it needs imagination, not a screen. Ages 3–6. Four stars.

  2. Write a review of a film you have watched for your school newsletter.

    Show answer
    Title + film name. Hook. Key facts (type of film, who it is for). Summary of the main character and problem with no ending. Strengths (acting, a funny or exciting scene, music). One weakness (too long in the middle). Verdict and recommendation with a rating.

  3. Write a review of a place you have visited with your family (a museum, a park, a restaurant) for a website.

    Show answer
    P1 name, where, why you went. P2 what there is to see and do. P3 highlights with specific examples (the best room, the food). P4 weakness (queues, price, crowds). P5 advice and recommendation (who should go, best time to visit).

  4. Improve this sentence for a review: 'The book was good and the characters were nice.'

    Show answer
    Example: The book is gripping from the first page, and the main character, a brave but lonely boy, is easy to care about. (Present tense for the book, evaluative words, extra information between commas.)

  5. Which tense should you use? (a) to say what happens in a book (b) to say when you read it.

    Show answer
    (a) the present tense, e.g. The story follows a girl who… (b) the past tense, e.g. I read it during the summer holiday.
