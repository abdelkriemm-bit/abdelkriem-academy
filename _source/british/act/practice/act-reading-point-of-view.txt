P: LITERARY NARRATIVE: This passage is adapted from an original memoir. The narrator remembers the summers she spent at her grandmother's house in a village in the Fayoum.

Every summer until I was thirteen, my parents sent me from Cairo to my grandmother's house in a village near Lake Qarun. I went each year with the same complaint. There was no television in the house, the nearest shop sold little besides soap and sugar, and my cousins, who had grown up there, seemed to speak a private language of jokes and games that I could never quite learn.

What I remember most, though, is the bread. Twice a week, before the sun was fully up, my grandmother lit the clay oven in the courtyard and baked enough round loaves to last the household for days. She wanted me beside her. I wanted to be asleep. At ten, I could not imagine anything more pointless than getting up in the dark to make something that every shop in Cairo sold ready-made.

"You'll thank me one day," she said, the first morning I complained, and she pressed a ball of dough into my hands. I did not thank her. I made loaves that were thick on one side and thin on the other, and once I let a whole tray burn while I watched a cat chase a lizard along the wall. My grandmother scraped the black loaves into a basket for the chickens and said only, "The chickens are grateful." I could not tell whether she was angry. I think now that she was laughing.

She was not a woman who praised easily. When my loaves finally came out round and even, in my third summer, she looked at them for a long moment, turned one over to check the bottom and handed it to my uncle without a word. At the time I was hurt. I had worked so hard, and she had said nothing. It took me years to understand that handing my bread to her eldest son, at her own table, was the highest compliment she knew how to give.

My grandmother died when I was at university. The house was sold, and the oven, I suppose, was broken up or left to crumble. I did not go back to see.

I live in a city flat now, with an electric oven that heats evenly and a timer that rings. Sometimes, on a weekend morning, I make bread anyway. It never tastes the way hers did, and I have stopped expecting it to. But when the dough is warm under my hands and the kitchen is still dark, I can almost hear her in the next room, telling the chickens how grateful they ought to be.

When my students hand in an essay these days, it almost always arrives as a typed document. The pages are neat, the spelling has been checked by the computer, and I never have to squint at a word and guess what it is meant to be. I would be lying if I said I missed the days of stained pages and crossed-out paragraphs. Typed work is easier to read, easier to correct and easier to share, and my students type far faster than they write by hand.

And yet something has been lost, or at least put at risk. Some educators argue that handwriting is now simply an old skill, like using a slide rule, and that schools should spend the time on keyboard skills instead. I understand the argument, and I agree with part of it. No student today will build a career on beautiful handwriting. But I think the argument moves too quickly.

Several studies have suggested that students who take notes by hand remember lessons somewhat better than students who type them. The usual explanation is that writing by hand is slower, so the writer cannot copy every word and must decide what matters. I would not want to build too much on these studies; the differences they report are modest, and not every study has found them. But they match what I see in my own classroom. When my students plan an essay on paper, with arrows and circles and half-sentences, their ideas seem more connected than when they begin by typing.

There is also a smaller, more personal loss. Last year, one of my students found, in an old library book, a note her grandfather had written in the margin decades earlier. She recognised his writing at once and brought the book to me as if she had found a letter addressed to her. A typed note could never have done that; it would have looked like everyone else's.

None of this means that schools should go back to long hours of copying out letters. That would be a waste of time, and it would not make anyone love writing. What I would like is something more modest: that young children still learn to write comfortably by hand, and that older students are encouraged, at least sometimes, to think on paper before they type.

The keyboard has won, and on most days I am glad it has. I would simply like it to win a little less completely.
