P: SOCIAL SCIENCE: This passage is adapted from an original article about a farming village that changed the way it waters its fields. The village and the people are fictional.

[1] Today the fields around the village of Nazlet Omar are green for most of the year, and the farmers there pay almost nothing to water them. Visitors from nearby villages come to see the rows of dark blue panels standing at the edge of almost every field, and some have begun to copy them. Fifteen years ago the situation was very different.

[2] At that time, the village's farmers watered their crops with pumps that ran on diesel fuel. When the price of diesel rose sharply, many farmers could no longer afford to run their pumps as often as their crops needed. Some left part of their land unplanted; others switched to grain, which needed less water but earned less money at the market. Many young men began leaving for jobs in the city.

[3] The first solar pump arrived almost by accident. A farmer named Gamal Rashed had a son who was studying engineering in Assiut, and the son persuaded his father to borrow money for a small solar system: a set of panels that turn sunlight into electricity to run a pump. The son installed the panels himself during his summer holiday. At first the neighbours laughed at the shiny panels. They stopped laughing after the first summer, when Gamal watered his fields every day and paid nothing for fuel.

[4] Within five years, more than half the farms in the village had installed solar pumps. Most farmers could not afford the panels alone, so groups of three or four neighbours shared one system and took turns using the water. A local bank, which had at first refused to lend money for the panels, began offering special loans once it saw that farmers were repaying their debts on time.

[5] The change brought benefits beyond lower costs. Diesel pumps were noisy and had to be watched; solar pumps work quietly during daylight hours without anyone watching them. Because watering became cheap, farmers returned to growing vegetables and fruit, which earn more than the grain they had switched to. Some of the young men who had left came back to work on their families' land.

[6] Cheap water also created a new problem. Because pumping no longer cost anything, some farmers began running their pumps all day, and the level of the underground water started to fall. Last year the village council introduced a rule limiting each farm's pumping hours. Unlike the diesel years, when money limited the amount of water farmers used, the limit now depends on an agreement among neighbours.

P: HUMANITIES: This passage is adapted from an original essay comparing old radio drama with modern audio drama.

[1] For several decades in the middle of the twentieth century, radio drama was one of the most popular forms of entertainment in many countries. Families gathered around a single set in the evening to follow serials that lasted for months. Then television arrived in homes, and within a generation most of those audiences had moved to the screen. In many places, radio drama survived only in a few late-night slots.

[2] In recent years, however, drama told through sound alone has returned, this time as audio series that listeners download or stream. The new form shares its basic tools with the old one: voices, music, sound effects and silence. Yet the two differ in ways that reveal how much the habits of listeners have changed.

[3] The first difference is time. A radio serial was broadcast at a fixed hour, and a listener who missed an episode usually missed it for good. Writers therefore repeated important information often, opening each episode with a summary and ending it with a question that would bring people back the following week. Because modern listeners can pause, rewind and play several episodes in a row, many audio series now cut these summaries and trust the audience to remember.

[4] The second difference is the room. Radio drama was usually heard by a family together, through a loudspeaker, in a room where people might also be eating or talking. Actors spoke clearly and fairly loudly so that they could be heard over the noise. Today most listeners use headphones, often alone, while walking or travelling. As a result, actors can speak almost in a whisper, and sound designers can place a noise close to one ear and far from the other. The effect is more intimate than anything the old loudspeakers could offer.

[5] The third difference is the making of sound itself. Many radio stations once employed sound-effects artists who stood beside the actors and produced footsteps, doors and storms by hand, with shoes, wooden boxes and sheets of metal. Modern producers usually build their effects from recordings on a computer, adding them after the actors have finished. The old method demanded perfect timing during a live performance; the new one allows endless correction but, some actors say, a little less excitement.

[6] What has not changed is the listener's part in the work. Because there are no pictures, every listener builds the scene in his or her own mind. Supporters of both forms agree that this shared act of imagination, rather than any piece of technology, is what makes drama in sound so powerful.

[3] The team's first step was to measure several hundred lizards on each island. The southern lizards had longer back legs, which allow faster sprinting, and they stayed close to bushes and rocks. [6] Dr Fawzy's team argues that the islands show two kinds of change working at different speeds.
