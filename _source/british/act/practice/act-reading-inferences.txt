[1] When Am Sayed went into hospital, he left me the key to the roof and a list written on the back of an electricity bill. [1] For thirty-four years, the painter Farid Mansour painted almost nothing but the view from his studio window: a short street in Shubra, a bakery on the corner, a lamp post and the upper floors of the building opposite. [2] Mansour had not always worked this way. As a young man, he had travelled widely, painting markets in Aswan, fishing boats in Alexandria and mountains in Sinai, and his early pictures sold well. Then, in his early forties, he rented the studio in Shubra, and he never painted anywhere else again.

[3] He rarely explained the change. In one of the few interviews he gave, he said only that he had "spent twenty years looking at many things quickly, and wanted to spend the rest looking at one thing slowly". When the interviewer asked whether he grew bored, he answered that the street had never once looked the same to him two days running.

[4] Looking at the canvases in order, a viewer begins to see what he meant. The light changes with the seasons and the hour. The bakery's awning goes from green to red and back again. A tree that appears as a thin stick in the early pictures is, by the final years, wide enough to hide half the lamp post. People appear only as small shapes, but the shapes change: children with school bags in one decade, a street crowded with cars in the next.

[5] Critics during his lifetime were divided. Some called the series an act of discipline. Others, less kindly, called it a failure of imagination, and one suggested that Mansour had simply run out of ideas. He never replied to them in public. [6] Today the series is more often seen as a kind of record: not only of one street but of a way of seeing. It asks viewers to do something that modern life rarely encourages, which is to look at the same thing long enough for it to become unfamiliar again.

P: NATURAL SCIENCE: This passage is adapted from an original article about how elephants communicate. The research team and its study are fictional.

[1] Anyone who has stood near a herd of elephants knows their trumpets and roars. Far fewer people notice the sound that elephants use most. Much of an elephant's communication takes place in deep rumbles, many of them too low for human ears to hear. These sounds can travel for several kilometres, especially in the cool, still air of early morning and evening.

[2] Researchers have known about the rumbles for decades, but recording them in the wild is difficult. A team led by biologist Dr Amr Shalaby placed sensitive microphones and ground sensors around a water hole in a national park in southern Africa and recorded every visit by elephants over two dry seasons. The sensors detected vibrations in the soil as well as sounds in the air.

[3] The recordings showed that family groups often produced a particular rumble shortly before leaving the water hole. Within a minute of the first call, other members of the group answered, and the whole family then moved off together. When the team played the same recorded rumble through a loudspeaker to a group that was drinking, the elephants stopped, raised their heads and, in most trials, began to move away within a few minutes. When the researchers played a different rumble, recorded from an unfamiliar group, the drinking elephants also stopped and listened, but they did not leave. Several adult females moved to stand between the loudspeaker and the young.

[4] The ground sensors produced a second finding. On several occasions, elephants at the water hole froze and leaned forward, placing more weight on their front feet, at moments when the air microphones had recorded nothing unusual. In each case, the ground sensors had picked up vibrations from a group still several kilometres away. Elephants' feet contain many sensitive nerve endings, and the team suggests that the animals were "listening" through the ground.

[5] Dr Shalaby is careful about what the results mean. Rumbles, he notes, are not words, and it would be a mistake to translate them as "let's go" or "danger". The calls seem instead to work as signals whose meaning depends on who makes them and when. He adds that the dry seasons of the study may have made long-distance signals especially important, since elephants then travel farther between sources of water.

[6] The findings have practical uses. Roads, mines and heavy machines create vibrations of their own, and the team now plans to study whether such noise interferes with the signals elephants send through the ground.
