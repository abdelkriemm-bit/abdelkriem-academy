P: NATURAL SCIENCE: This passage is adapted from an original article about how camels survive in the desert.

Ask a group of children what a camel keeps in its hump, and most of them will give the same confident answer: water. The idea is so common that it appears in cartoons, school jokes and even some old travel books. It is also wrong. A camel's hump is made mostly of fat, and it is the animal's food store, not its water tank.

Why has the mistake lasted so long? Part of the reason is that the real answer is less simple. A camel does not survive the desert because of one clever container. It survives because almost every part of its body has been shaped, over a very long time, to waste as little water as possible.

Consider the way a camel deals with heat. Most mammals, including humans, keep their body temperature almost constant and sweat as soon as they grow warm. A camel can let its temperature rise by several degrees during the hot day before it begins to sweat, and then lose that extra heat in the cool desert night. In effect, the animal stores heat the way a stone wall does: it soaks up warmth in the afternoon and releases it after sunset. Every degree it does not have to sweat away is water it keeps.

The camel's body saves water in other ways too. Its kidneys produce very concentrated urine, and its droppings are so dry that desert travellers have long used them as fuel. Its nose recovers some of the moisture in each breath before the air leaves its body. Even its blood is unusual: its red blood cells are oval rather than round, a shape that helps them keep flowing when the animal is badly dehydrated.

None of this means that camels do not need water. They do, and when they find it they can drink an enormous amount, many tens of litres, in a matter of minutes. Few other animals could take in so much so quickly without harm. The difference is that a camel can wait much longer than most animals before it must drink again.

The hump, then, is still important, but for a different reason than the cartoons suggest. When food is scarce, the camel draws on the fat stored there, and the hump slowly shrinks and leans to one side. Travellers who once saw a drooping hump and thought the animal was "running out of water" were noticing something real; they were simply explaining it wrongly. The true story is less tidy than the myth, but it is far more remarkable.

A few steps from the old gate of Bab Zuweila in Cairo, a narrow covered street is lined with tiny workshops. In each one, a craftsman sits with a large piece of cloth spread over his knees, sewing small shapes of coloured cotton onto a plain background with stitches almost too fine to see. Today, the panels have found new homes. Many Egyptians still know the designs best from the colourful tents put up for weddings, funerals and Ramadan gatherings, though most of those tents are now made from printed fabric that only copies the hand-sewn patterns. The printed copies are cheap and quick to produce, and it is easy to see why event organisers choose them. A printed tent can be ready in a day; a hand-sewn one would cost more than most families could pay. Yet the printed fabric has also made life harder for the real craft. The craft faces a second difficulty as well. The work is slow and the income uncertain, so it is not easy to persuade young people to spend years learning it. A craftsman may train an apprentice for a long time only to see him leave for a job that pays more regularly.

Others welcome visitors who want to try a few stitches themselves. These changes are small, but they suggest that the craft can adapt without losing what makes it valuable: the patience of a hand that places every stitch. P: SOCIAL SCIENCE: This passage is adapted from an original article about the experience of waiting. The clinic and the surveys described are fictional.

Nobody enjoys waiting, but not all waits feel the same. Ten minutes at a bus stop with a friend can pass almost unnoticed; ten minutes alone in a silent waiting room, watching a door that does not open, can feel like an hour. People who study waiting have reached a conclusion that sounds odd at first: the length of a wait often matters less than the way it is experienced.

Several features seem to make a wait feel longer. Unoccupied time drags more than occupied time. Uncertain waits feel longer than waits whose end is known. And a wait that seems unfair, as when someone who arrived later is served first, is remembered as longer and more irritating than the clock would suggest.

The experience of a busy health clinic in one provincial town illustrates the point. For years, patients had taken a paper number and then sat with no idea when they would be called. The clinic could not afford more doctors, so the average wait could not be shortened. Instead, the managers installed a screen showing which number was being served and roughly how many minutes each patient still had to wait. They also placed a few shelves of magazines and children's books in the room.

The actual waiting time did not change; it remained a little over forty minutes on average. Yet in surveys taken before and after the changes, the share of patients who described their wait as "too long" fell from about two-thirds to under half. Complaints at the reception desk dropped as well. The staff, who had once expected an angry crowd every Monday morning, reported that the room was noticeably calmer.

It would be easy to dismiss such changes as tricks: ways of making people accept poor service instead of improving it. That criticism deserves a serious answer. A screen does not cure a patient, and no amount of reading material makes up for a clinic that is badly understaffed. Organisations that use these methods instead of real improvement deserve the complaints they receive.

But the criticism misses something. For the patients in that room, the forty minutes were not only a number; they were an experience of worry, boredom and, often, the feeling of being forgotten. Reducing that worry is not a trick. It is a genuine improvement, even if it is not the only one that matters.

The lesson, then, is not that waiting can be made pleasant, nor that clinics should stop trying to shorten it. It is that the time a person spends waiting has two measures, the minutes on the clock and the minutes in the mind, and that those responsible for waiting rooms should pay attention to both.

Every exam season, the same scene repeats itself in homes across the world. A student sits at a desk long after midnight, surrounded by notes, determined to fit one more chapter into a tired brain. Sleep, in this plan, is the enemy: hours spent asleep are hours not spent studying. It is an understandable way of thinking. It is also, according to a large body of research, a poor strategy.

A common type of experiment makes the point clearly. Volunteers learn a list of word pairs or a simple skill, such as typing a short sequence on a keyboard. Half of them are tested after a night's sleep; the other half are tested after the same number of hours awake during the day. In many such studies, the group that slept remembers more or performs better, even though both groups spent the same amount of time away from the material.

This does not mean that sleep works like a magic recording machine. Students cannot learn a new language simply by playing lessons while they sleep, and a good night's rest will not rescue material that was never studied in the first place. The late-night student at the desk is not lazy or foolish; he is working hard in the way he believes will help. The problem is that the belief is out of date. Sleep is not the opposite of studying. In a very real sense, it is the last stage of it.

P: HUMANITIES: This passage is adapted from an original essay about reading the same book more than once.

When I was fourteen, I read a long novel about a family of cloth merchants in an old port city, and I thought it was the dullest book I had ever finished. Nothing happened, or so it seemed to me: people married, argued about money, grew old. I returned it to the library with relief. Twenty years later, a friend pressed the same novel into my hands, and I read it again over a single winter. It was, I discovered, a book about almost everything I had come to care about.

The book had not changed, of course. I had. That is the obvious explanation, and it is mostly right, but it is not quite complete. Something else happens when we reread, and it is worth trying to name.

There is a familiar argument against rereading, and it deserves a fair hearing. Life is short, the argument goes, and the number of books we can read in it is limited. Every hour spent on a book we already know is an hour taken from one we have never opened. Readers who return again and again to the same few favourites risk becoming like travellers who only ever visit the town where they were born.

I do not think this argument is wrong so much as incomplete. It treats a book as a place we visit once and then cross off a list. But a serious book is less like a place than like a conversation, and conversations with old friends are rarely a waste of time. We do not reread to find out what happens; we already know that. We reread to notice how it happens: the sentence that quietly prepares an ending, the minor character who was there all along.

An old saying holds that no one steps into the same river twice, because both the river and the person have changed. Rereading works in a similar way, with one difference. The river of the text stays still. Only the reader moves. And because the words stay fixed, rereading shows us, more clearly than almost anything else, how far we have travelled.

This is the part the obvious explanation misses. Rereading is not only a way of understanding a book better. It is also a way of measuring ourselves against something that does not change. The boy who found the merchants dull was not stupid; he had simply not yet lived through the things the book describes. Meeting his judgement again, all those years later, was one of the strangest pleasures of that winter.

So read new books, by all means, and many of them. But keep a shelf for the ones you mean to return to. They will be waiting, exactly as you left them, ready to show you who you have become.
