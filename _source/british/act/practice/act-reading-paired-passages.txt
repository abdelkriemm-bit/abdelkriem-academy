Older beginners rarely behave like this. A fourteen-year-old who makes a mistake in front of classmates may stay silent for the rest of the lesson.

This is why I believe that schools should introduce a second language as early as possible. Young children are natural mimics. They pick up the sounds of a new language, its rhythm and its music, in a way that older learners find much harder. Early teaching does not need to be heavy. Twenty minutes a day of songs, stories and games is enough. Of course, an early start is not magic: a child who stops hearing the language at eight will forget much of it. But a child who keeps going has a head start that is very hard to catch up with later. None of this means that early lessons are a waste. They can make children curious about other languages and comfortable with new sounds. What matters most in the classroom is not the age at which lessons begin but how many hours of good teaching follow, and whether learners have real reasons to use what they learn.

When students know their books will be in the exam room, some of them stop practising recall altogether. None of this means that teachers should return to exams of forty dates. The best tests may combine both: a short section answered from memory, and a longer one in which students use sources to argue and judge.

Passage A and Passage B are adapted from original essays by two historians of science. Both historians are fictional.

**PASSAGE A**

It has become fashionable to say that science advances through communities rather than individuals, and that the "lone genius" is a myth invented by people who write biographies. There is something to this. No scientist works alone, and every discovery rests on earlier work. Yet the fashion can be taken too far, until we forget that some moments in the history of science happened because one particular mind refused to accept what everyone else accepted.

Consider Ibn al-Haytham. The leading theories of vision he inherited, including those of respected Greek authorities, held that the eye sends out rays toward the objects it sees. Ibn al-Haytham argued instead that vision occurs when light from objects enters the eye, and he supported his account with careful experiments rather than with appeals to authority. He read his predecessors closely, but he read them in order to test them, and where they failed his tests, he set them aside.

Many scholars of his time had access to the same books. What they lacked was not information but the willingness to doubt it. That willingness cannot be produced by a library or a committee. It belongs to individuals, and when we tell the history of science only as the story of networks, we risk teaching students that their own doubts do not matter.

**PASSAGE B**

Consider what made his work possible. The Greek writings on optics that he tested so carefully were available in Arabic because, over the previous two centuries, scholars in Baghdad and elsewhere had translated them, discussed them and written commentaries on them. Patrons paid for copies; teachers taught them; earlier scholars had already begun to question some of their details. Ibn al-Haytham doubted brilliantly, but he doubted within a conversation that others had built.

Nor did the conversation end with him. His Book of Optics shaped later scholarship only because it was copied, studied and eventually translated into Latin, in which form it influenced generations of European scholars. A discovery that no one reads is not, in any useful sense, a discovery.

None of this makes Ibn al-Haytham less remarkable. It makes him more understandable. Students who are told that progress depends on rare geniuses may conclude that science is for other people. Students who are shown how a brilliant individual worked inside a community that preserved, challenged and passed on ideas may conclude something far more useful: that they, too, can join the conversation.
