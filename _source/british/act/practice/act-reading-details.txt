P: NATURAL SCIENCE: This passage is adapted from an original article about how the one-humped camel survives in the desert.

[1] Few animals are as closely linked to the desert as the dromedary, the one-humped camel of North Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. For thousands of years, people have relied on it to carry goods and travellers across land where water may be days away. Its ability to survive such journeys depends not on a single trick but on many small features working together.

[2] A common belief is that the camel's hump is full of water. In fact, the hump stores fat. When food is scarce, the camel can break down this fat for energy. Keeping its fat in one place, rather than spreading it under the skin over the whole body, also helps the animal lose heat more easily. A camel that has gone without food for a long time will have a hump that is smaller and may lean to one side.

[3] Water is handled in other ways. A thirsty camel can drink a very large amount of water in a few minutes, far more than most animals could take in so quickly. Its red blood cells are oval rather than round. This shape allows them to keep flowing when the animal is short of water, and to swell without bursting when it drinks a great deal at once.

[4] The camel also saves water by allowing its body temperature to change. During the day its temperature can rise by several degrees before it begins to sweat, and at night the temperature falls again as the animal releases the stored heat into the cool air. A person whose body temperature rose in the same way would soon be in danger.

[5] Other features protect the camel from the desert itself. Two rows of long eyelashes keep sand out of its eyes, and it can close its nostrils during a sandstorm. Its thick coat may seem strange in such heat, but the fur shields the skin from the sun and slows the rate at which heat reaches the body. Wide, padded feet spread its weight so that it does not sink into soft sand.

[6] Many of these features have been studied by scientists who are interested in how living things cope with heat and drought. Some have measured camels' temperatures through the day and night, while others have studied their blood. As many dry regions grow hotter, some researchers think that a better understanding of the camel could help farmers choose and care for animals that cope better with high temperatures. The animal that once carried traders between oases may now carry useful lessons for farmers as well.

[1] Walk through a date plantation in one of Egypt's oases in spring and you may see a farmer climbing a palm trunk with a bundle of cut flowers tucked into his belt. He is not decorating the tree. He is doing a job that, in the wild, would be left to the wind.

[2] Date palms are either male or female. Only female trees produce fruit, but their flowers must first receive pollen from a male tree. In nature, the wind carries the pollen, and male and female trees grow together in roughly equal numbers. For a farmer, however, every male tree takes up space that could hold a fruit-bearing female. Farmers therefore keep only a few male trees and pollinate the females by hand, cutting strands of male flowers and tying them inside the opening female flower clusters. Done this way, a single male tree can supply pollen for dozens of females.

[3] Hand pollination is effective, but it is slow and dangerous. Each female tree may need to be climbed more than once, because its flower clusters do not all open on the same day, and the trunks of older palms can be very tall. In many oases, the skilled climbers who do this work are growing older, and fewer young people are learning the job.

[4] For these reasons, a team of agricultural researchers led by Dr Nabil Ezzat set out to compare three methods on one farm over two seasons. The first group of trees was pollinated in the traditional way. The second was dusted from the ground with dry pollen mixed with flour, using a long pole fitted with a small pump. The third was sprayed from above by a drone carrying pollen mixed into water.

[5] The traditional method produced the highest share of developed fruit, but the pole method came close, reaching about nine-tenths of the traditional result while needing less than half the labour. The drone was by far the fastest of the three, yet its results varied widely. On calm mornings it performed almost as well as the pole; on windy afternoons much of the pollen drifted away, and some trees set very little fruit.

[6] The researchers also found that pollen lost much of its strength when it was stored at room temperature for more than a few weeks, while pollen kept cool and dry stayed useful for the whole season. Dr Ezzat's team now recommends the pole method for most farms, with drones used only in calm weather, and advises farmers to store their pollen carefully. Climbing by hand, they suggest, may soon be needed only for the tallest trees.
