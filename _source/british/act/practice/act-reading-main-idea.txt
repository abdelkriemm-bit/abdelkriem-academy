### Key Ideas and Details (main idea)
P: HUMANITIES: This passage is adapted from an original essay about the work of film subtitlers.

[1] Most cinema audiences notice subtitles only when they go wrong: when a line disappears before it can be read, or when a joke that made the actors laugh produces silence in the theatre. When subtitles work, they are invisible. That invisibility hides one of the most demanding forms of writing in the modern world, and it has led many people to imagine that subtitling is simply translation with a stopwatch.

[2] The stopwatch is real. A subtitle usually allows no more than two short lines on the screen, and it must stay there long enough to be read but disappear when the speaker stops. Viewers read more slowly than actors speak, so a subtitler can rarely include every word. A character who talks quickly for ten seconds may receive only a dozen words in the subtitle, and the subtitler must decide which of the original words those dozen should carry.

[3] Those decisions are where the real work begins. A literal translation might keep every fact in a sentence and lose its tone entirely, turning a sarcastic remark into a polite one. A good subtitler asks first what a line is doing in the scene. Is it giving information, revealing character or making the audience laugh? If a line exists to make people laugh, keeping the joke may matter more than keeping the exact words, even if that means replacing a pun with a different one that works in the new language.

[4] Culture adds another layer of difficulty. A reference to a famous local singer or a children's television programme may mean everything to one audience and nothing to another. Subtitlers must decide whether to keep the reference and trust viewers to follow, to replace it with something familiar, or to drop it. Each choice has a cost. Replacing a reference can make a film feel as though it were made somewhere it was not; keeping it can leave viewers confused at an important moment.

[5] Because of these choices, two subtitlers working on the same film will almost never produce the same text. Some critics see this as a weakness and call for strict, word-for-word rules. Yet the variety is better understood as evidence of skill. Like a translator of poetry, a subtitler interprets as well as transfers, and the best of them are artists working within very tight limits.

[6] Audiences, then, owe subtitlers more attention than they usually give. The next time a foreign film makes you laugh at exactly the right moment, it is worth remembering that someone decided, word by word and second by second, how that laughter would reach you.

[1] Each year, strong winds lift enormous quantities of dust from the Sahara and carry it high into the atmosphere. Much of it falls back to the ground within Africa, but a large share travels west over the Atlantic Ocean, sometimes forming clouds of haze that can be seen from space. For a long time, scientists treated this dust mainly as a nuisance. Today many of them see it as one of the threads that connect the planet's ecosystems.

[2] Some of the dust that travels farthest comes from an ancient lake bed in Chad known as the Bodélé Depression. Thousands of years ago, the area lay beneath a large lake, and the remains of tiny water organisms built up in its sediments. When winds now sweep across the dry basin, they pick up dust rich in phosphorus, a nutrient that plants need in order to grow.

[3] That nutrient matters on the far side of the ocean. The soils of the Amazon rainforest are surprisingly poor. Heavy rain washes nutrients away, and much of the forest's wealth is stored in living plants rather than in the ground. Researchers using satellite measurements have estimated that dust from Africa delivers phosphorus to the Amazon in amounts roughly comparable to what the forest loses each year through rain and floods. The forest, in other words, is partly fed by a desert.

[4] The ocean between the two continents benefits as well. Iron and other minerals in the dust that falls on the sea can encourage the growth of plankton, the tiny organisms at the base of the marine food chain. In some regions where iron is scarce, a dust storm can be followed by a visible bloom of plankton. Because plankton absorb carbon dioxide as they grow, these blooms also interest scientists who study the climate.

[5] Not every effect is welcome. When thick dust reaches the Caribbean, it can lower air quality, and it has been linked to breathing problems on some islands. Scientists are also studying whether dust layers affect the formation of storms over the Atlantic, and whether microbes carried in the dust may harm coral reefs. The same particles that feed one ecosystem may damage another.

[6] Studying the dust has therefore become a way of studying connections. A change in the winds over Chad, or in rainfall over the Sahara, could alter the supply of nutrients to a rainforest on another continent. For scientists, the lesson is that no ecosystem can be fully understood on its own; even a desert and a rainforest are parts of a single, linked system.

I could not have explained the difference, but I could hear it, and hearing it irritated me more than any of his corrections. I began to arrive early, to sit in his hallway and listen to the student before me, trying to catch the moment where the difference lay.

[4] In the spring, a cousin's wedding was held in the courtyard of our building, and the hired musicians arrived late. Someone remembered that I "played", and before I could protest an oud was put into my hands. I did not attempt anything from my list. I played a simple song my grandmother used to sing, the only one I knew well enough, one note at a time, letting each note finish. The courtyard went quiet. An old man near the front closed his eyes. Afterwards my aunt asked who had taught me, and for the first time I said my teacher's name without complaining.

[5] When I returned to Ustaz Fathi's flat the next week, I did not mention the wedding, and he did not ask. He simply handed me a new piece. It was not fast. I noticed that the table no longer rocked, and I understood, with some embarrassment, that my list had been doing useful work all along, though not the work I had planned for it.
