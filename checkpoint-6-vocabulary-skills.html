Checkpoint 6 · Reading: understanding texts

Vocabulary Skills: Context Clues, Word Parts, Spelling and Idioms

Work out unfamiliar words from context and word parts, answer 'find a word' and technical-term questions, and master the spelling rules and British idioms that matter in Checkpoint 6.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 English (Cambridge Primary 0058)

Why vocabulary matters in Checkpoint 6

Vocabulary is tested in both papers. In Section A Reading (25 marks in Paper 1 Non-fiction and 25 marks in Paper 2 Fiction), you will meet questions such as 'Find one word that means...', 'Match each technical term to its definition', or 'What does this phrase mean?'. In Section B Writing, two of the five marking areas are about words: Vocabulary and language (3 marks) and Word structure (spelling) (3 marks).

So this page teaches you two things: how to work out words you do not know when you read, and how to use and spell words correctly when you write.

SkillWhere it helps you

Spelling rulesWriting: the 3 spelling marks in every piece you write.
IdiomsReading: understanding figurative expressions. Writing: a natural voice in stories, diaries and informal letters.

1. Context clues: working out a word you don't know

A context clue is a hint in the words or sentences around a difficult word. Writers often help you, even in an exam. You do not need to know the word already. You need to be a detective.

Type of clueHow it worksOriginal example
Definition / restatementThe meaning is given straight after the word, often after 'which means', 'or', 'that is', or between commas, dashes or brackets.The desert fox is nocturnal (it sleeps all day and hunts at night).
SynonymA word with the same meaning is nearby.The path was narrow. It was so thin that we walked in a line.
Antonym / contrastA word with the opposite meaning is nearby, often after 'but', 'however', 'unlike'.Unlike his timid brother, Ziad was bold and loved trying new things.
ExampleExamples show what the word means.We packed provisions such as bread, cheese, dates and water.
Cause and effectThe result tells you the meaning.The road was hazardous, so the police closed it until the ice melted.
General senseThe whole sentence or paragraph tells you.After running for an hour, Sara was famished and ate three sandwiches.
Word partsBreak the word into prefix + root + suffix.unbreakable = un (not) + break + able (can be) = cannot be broken

Signal words tell you what kind of clue is coming:

Signal wordsClue to expect
but, however, although, unlike, whereas, insteadContrast: the word means the OPPOSITE of the clue.
so, because, as a result, thereforeCause and effect.
such as, for example, including, likeExamples.
which means, or, in other words, that is, a pair of dashes, bracketsDefinition / restatement (extra information explaining the word).
The 4-step method for 'What does this word mean?'
  • 1. Find the word and read the whole sentence plus the sentences before and after.
  • 2. Look for a clue: definition, synonym, contrast, example, cause and effect.
  • 3. Decide if the word is positive or negative, and what word class it is (noun, verb, adjective).
  • 4. Replace the word with your meaning and read the sentence again. If it makes sense, you are right.

Worked mini-example. Text: 'The kitten was so docile that it let the children carry it around all afternoon without a single scratch.' Question: What does 'docile' mean? (1 mark). Answer: calm and gentle / easy to handle. Why: cause and effect clue: because it is docile, it lets children carry it and never scratches. Test: 'The kitten was so gentle that...' makes sense.

2. 'Find a word or phrase that means...' questions

You are given a meaning and told where to look. You must copy the exact word or phrase from the text.

  1. Circle the instruction: 'one word' means ONE word only. Two words, even if correct, can score zero.
  2. Underline where to look: 'in paragraph 2', 'in lines 5-8'. Do not search anywhere else.
  3. Work out the word class of the meaning. If the meaning is 'very tired' (adjective), the answer is an adjective (exhausted), not a verb.
  4. Read the paragraph and test each possible word by putting the meaning in its place.
  5. Copy the word exactly, with the same spelling and ending (crept, not creep).

Worked mini-example. Paragraph 2: 'At the back of the cupboard, behind old jars, Hana found a tiny wooden box. It was ancient, its paint faded and cracked.' Question: Find one word in paragraph 2 that means 'very old'. Answer: ancient. ('faded' is a trap: it describes the paint, not the age.)

Common traps
  • Writing a whole phrase when the question says 'one word'.
  • Writing your own synonym instead of copying the word from the text.
  • Choosing a word from the wrong paragraph.
  • Choosing a word with a similar topic but a different meaning (faded vs ancient).

3. Matching technical terms to definitions

Information texts in Paper 1 (about science, nature, sport, history, technology) often contain technical terms: special words for that subject. A question can give you several terms and ask you to match each one to its definition, usually by drawing lines or writing letters.

Method: (1) Find each term in the text. (2) Read the sentence around it: the writer often explains it with a definition clue. (3) Match the terms you are sure of first. (4) The last one is usually the one left over, but still check it makes sense.

Worked mini-example. Text: 'Bees collect nectar, the sweet liquid inside flowers. As they move from flower to flower, they pollinate the plants, carrying tiny grains of pollen that help new seeds form. Back at the hive, the bees store honey in wax cells called a honeycomb.'

TermDefinition
nectarthe sweet liquid inside flowers
pollinatecarry pollen from one flower to another so seeds can grow
hivethe home where bees live
honeycombthe wax cells where honey is stored

Notice that every definition was in the text itself: the writer explained the words using commas and 'called'. That is why reading the sentence around the term is the key step.

Using word parts in a reading question. 'The old bridge was unrepairable.' un- (not) + repair (fix) + -able (can be) = cannot be fixed. Even if you have never seen the word, you can explain it.

Spelling tip for prefixes

Adding a prefix never changes the spelling of the root: dis + appear = disappear (one s), dis + satisfied = dissatisfied (two s), mis + spell = misspell (two s), un + necessary = unnecessary (two n).

5. Synonyms, antonyms and shades of meaning

A synonym means the same or almost the same (big / large / huge). An antonym means the opposite (big / small). Synonyms are rarely exactly the same: they differ in strength. Choosing the right strength is what makes writing precise.

'In your own words' questions

When a question says 'in your own words', you must not copy the key words from the text. Replace each key word with a synonym. Text: 'The villagers were terrified of the forest.' Own words: 'The people who lived in the village were very frightened of the woods.'

6. Spelling: the rules that save marks

Spelling is marked in every piece of writing under Word structure (spelling), 3 marks. These rules cover most of the mistakes Year 6 students make.

RuleExamplesWatch out
i before e when the sound is 'ee', except after cbelieve, piece, chief / receive, ceilingExceptions: weird, seize, protein. 'ay' sound = e before i: eight, neighbour, weigh.
Drop the silent e before a vowel suffix (-ing, -ed, -able)make + ing = making, hope + ed = hoped, love + able = lovableKeep the e before a consonant suffix: hopeful, movement, lovely.
Consonant + y: change y to ihappy + ness = happiness, carry + ed = carried, baby → babiesKeep y before -ing: carrying, studying. Vowel + y: just add (played, enjoys).
Double the last consonant (short vowel + one consonant) before a vowel suffixstop → stopping, swim → swimmer, begin → beginning, big → biggestNo doubling when there are two vowels or two consonants: rain → raining, jump → jumped.
Add -es after s, sh, ch, x, zbuses, wishes, churches, boxes, quizzes
-f / -fe plurals often change to -vesleaf → leaves, knife → knives, half → halvesExceptions: roofs, chiefs.
The suffix -ful has ONE lcareful, beautiful, hopeful, wonderful'full' on its own has two l's.
-able when the root is a whole word, -ible when it is notenjoyable, comfortable / possible, terrible, visibleCheck in a dictionary when unsure.
British spelling doubles l before a vowel suffixtravel → travelled, cancel → cancelled, label → labelledAmerican spelling uses one l.

7. Idioms (common British idioms)

An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning is different from the meaning of each word. 'It's raining cats and dogs' has nothing to do with animals: it means it is raining very heavily. In reading, use the context to work out the meaning. In writing, one well-chosen idiom in a story's dialogue, a diary or an informal letter adds a natural voice. Do not use idioms in formal reports.

How to answer an idiom question

Never explain the words literally. Ask: what is happening in the story at this moment? 'When Adam saw the mess, he hit the roof.' Adam is clearly not climbing anything: from the context (a mess), 'hit the roof' must mean he became very angry.

To reach 3/3 in Vocabulary and language, choose words that are precise and match the mood and text type. Here are useful upgrades.

Common mistakes

  • Giving more than one word when the question says 'one word'.
  • Using your own word in 'find a word' questions instead of copying from the text.
  • Copying the text in 'own words' questions.
  • Explaining an idiom literally.
  • Using American spelling (color, favorite) in a British exam.
  • Writing 'carefull', 'beautifull' (the suffix -ful has one l).
  • Mixing up there/their/they're and its/it's.

Checklist

  • I can use 7 types of context clue and the signal words.
  • I always check 'one word' or 'phrase', and which paragraph.
  • I can match technical terms using the sentence around them.
  • I know the main prefixes, suffixes and roots.
  • I choose synonyms with the right strength.
  • I know the spelling rules, homophones and British spellings.
  • I can work out an idiom from the context.

Practice questions

All texts are short and original. Try each one before opening the answer.

  1. 'The cave was so dim that we could hardly see our own hands.' What does 'dim' mean? (1 mark)

    Show answer
    dark / not bright. Cause and effect clue: we could hardly see our hands.

  2. 'Unlike the noisy city, the village was tranquil.' What does 'tranquil' mean? Which clue helped you? (2 marks)

    Show answer
    calm / quiet / peaceful (1). Contrast clue: 'Unlike' tells us it is the opposite of 'noisy' (1).

  3. 'Camels can survive in arid places – areas that get almost no rain – where most animals would die.' What does 'arid' mean? (1 mark)

    Show answer
    very dry. Definition clue: the pair of dashes adds extra information explaining the word.

  4. Paragraph: 'The explorers were weary after climbing all day. Their legs ached and they could barely keep their eyes open.' Find ONE word that means 'very tired'. (1 mark)

    Show answer
    weary

  5. Paragraph: 'The vast desert stretched in every direction. There was nothing but sand for hundreds of kilometres.' Find ONE word that means 'very large'. (1 mark)

    Show answer
    vast

  6. Match each term to its meaning. Text: 'Volcanoes form where hot, melted rock called magma rises from deep underground. When it bursts out of the volcano, the rock is called lava. The explosion itself is an eruption. The opening at the top is the crater.' Terms: magma, lava, eruption, crater. (2 marks)

    Show answer
    magma = melted rock underground / lava = melted rock that has come out of the volcano / eruption = when the volcano explodes / bursts out / crater = the opening at the top of the volcano. (4 correct = 2 marks, 2-3 correct = 1 mark.)

  7. What does 'misjudge' mean? Use the prefix to explain. (1 mark)

    Show answer
    To judge wrongly. The prefix 'mis-' means wrongly.

  8. Add a prefix to make the opposite: (a) possible (b) legal (c) honest (d) regular (2 marks)

    Show answer
    (a) impossible (b) illegal (c) dishonest (d) irregular.

  9. Change the word into a noun using a suffix: (a) kind (b) enjoy (c) decide (d) dark (2 marks)

    Show answer
    (a) kindness (b) enjoyment (c) decision (d) darkness.

  10. Explain the meaning of 'telescope' using its parts. (1 mark)

    Show answer
    tele (far) + scope (look) = an instrument for looking at things that are far away.

  11. Put in order from weakest to strongest: furious, annoyed, angry. (1 mark)

    Show answer
    annoyed, angry, furious.

  12. Give an antonym for each: (a) ancient (b) generous (c) arrive (d) victory (2 marks)

    Show answer
    (a) modern / new (b) selfish / mean (c) depart / leave (d) defeat.

  13. In your own words, explain: 'The children were astonished by the enormous whale.' (2 marks)

    Show answer
    Example: The young people were very surprised by the huge whale. ('astonished' replaced by 'very surprised', 'enormous' by 'huge'. 1 mark for each key word changed correctly.)

  14. Correct the spelling: (a) hopeing (b) carryed (c) stoped (d) beautifull (2 marks)

    Show answer
    (a) hoping (drop the e) (b) carried (y to i) (c) stopped (double the consonant) (d) beautiful (-ful has one l).

  15. Choose the correct word: (a) The dog wagged (its / it's) tail. (b) (Their / There / They're) going to the beach. (c) I need to (practice / practise) my lines. (2 marks)

    Show answer
    (a) its (b) They're (c) practise (British verb).

  16. Write the British spelling: color, center, organize, traveled. (2 marks)

    Show answer
    colour, centre, organise, travelled.

  17. 'When Sami heard he had won the trip, he was over the moon.' What does the idiom mean? (1 mark)

    Show answer
    He was extremely happy.

  18. 'Laila had butterflies in her stomach as she walked on stage.' What does this tell us about Laila? (1 mark)

    Show answer
    She felt very nervous.

  19. 'Don't let the cat out of the bag about Mum's present!' What is the speaker asking? (1 mark)

    Show answer
    Not to tell the secret about the present by mistake.

  20. Which idiom means 'very rarely'? A) a piece of cake B) once in a blue moon C) on the ball D) cold feet (1 mark)

    Show answer
    B) once in a blue moon.
