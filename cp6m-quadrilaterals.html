Quadrilaterals: Properties and Classification

Name, describe, sort and sketch quadrilaterals using their sides, angles, parallel sides, lines of symmetry and diagonals.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 6 › Checkpoint 6 Maths — Complete Course

In the curriculum
  • Identify and name quadrilaterals: square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, trapezium, kite. [6Gg.01]
  • Describe them using their sides, angles, parallel sides, lines of symmetry and diagonals. [6Gg.01]
  • Classify (sort) quadrilaterals by their properties — for example in Venn and Carroll diagrams — and understand that some shapes are special types of others. [6Gg.01]
  • Sketch quadrilaterals with given properties, on plain paper or on a grid. [6Gg.01]

A quadrilateral is any flat (2D) shape with four straight sides and four vertices (corners). Some quadrilaterals are so useful that they have their own names. To get full marks you must know the properties of each one so well that you can name a shape from a description, sort shapes into groups, and sketch a shape that fits a list of clues.

The arrowhead is a "kite pushed in": it has the same equal sides as a kite but one corner points inwards. Any other four-sided shape with no special properties is simply called an irregular quadrilateral (or just a quadrilateral).

3. Lines of symmetry

Imagine folding the shape along a line. If the two halves fit exactly on top of each other, the fold is a line of symmetry. Use tracing paper in the test if it helps — it is allowed.

Watch out: the diagonals of a rectangle are NOT lines of symmetry, and the diagonals of a rhombus ARE. Test it by folding a paper rectangle corner to corner — the halves do not match. A general trapezium and an irregular quadrilateral have no lines of symmetry. A kite and an arrowhead each have one.

Some shapes also look the same after a turn (rotational symmetry — you study this fully in the symmetry lesson): a square looks the same 4 times in a full turn (order 4); a rectangle, rhombus and parallelogram 2 times (order 2); kites and trapeziums only once (order 1).

4. Diagonals

Draw both diagonals and ask three questions: Are they equal in length? Do they cut each other in half (bisect each other)? Do they meet at a right angle (are they perpendicular)?

Notice the square says yes to everything: it has all the properties of a rectangle AND all the properties of a rhombus.

5. Classifying: the quadrilateral family

A shape can belong to more than one group. A square has 2 pairs of parallel sides, so it IS a parallelogram. It has 4 right angles, so it IS a rectangle. It has 4 equal sides, so it IS a rhombus. We say a square is a special rectangle and a special rhombus.

  • Every square is a rectangle — but not every rectangle is a square (its sides need not all be equal).
  • Every square is a rhombus — but not every rhombus is a square (it need not have right angles).
  • Every rectangle and every rhombus is a parallelogram.
  • A rhombus is also a special kite (its two pairs of adjacent sides are equal).
  • In this course a trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel sides, so a parallelogram is not counted as a trapezium.

6. Sketching quadrilaterals

  1. Read every clue and decide which shape it is first (use the tables above).
  2. On a square grid, use the grid lines for parallel and perpendicular sides. To make a side parallel to another, move the same number of squares across and up.
  3. Use a ruler. Mark equal sides with ticks, parallel sides with arrows and right angles with a small square — this shows the examiner you know the properties.
  4. Check every clue again against your sketch before moving on.

7. Worked examples

Worked example 1 (1 mark) I am a quadrilateral. I have two pairs of parallel sides. All my sides are equal. I have no right angles. What am I?

  1. Two pairs of parallel sides → parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus or square.
  2. All sides equal → rhombus or square.
  3. No right angles → not a square.
  4. Answer: rhombus.

Worked example 2 (2 marks) Write each shape in the correct place in the Carroll diagram: square, rectangle, rhombus, kite, trapezium, irregular quadrilateral.

diagonals meet at 90°diagonals do not meet at 90°
has parallel sidessquare, rhombusrectangle, trapezium
no parallel sideskiteirregular quadrilateral

Working: check each shape for both properties. Kite: diagonals perpendicular ✓, no parallel sides. Rectangle: parallel sides ✓, diagonals equal but not at 90°. Marks: 2 marks for all six correct; 1 mark for four or five correct.

Worked example 3 (1 mark) P, Q and R are three vertices of a parallelogram PQRS. Find the position of S.

  1. Opposite sides of a parallelogram are parallel and equal, so PS must be the same "move" as QR.
  2. Q → R is 1 right, 3 up. So P → S is also 1 right, 3 up.
  3. Answer: S is 1 square right and 3 squares up from P (at (2, 4) if P is (1, 1)). Check: SR is 4 squares long, the same as PQ. ✓

Worked example 4 (1 mark) Zain says: "A square is not a rectangle because its sides are all the same length." Is he right? Explain.

Answer: No. A rectangle is a quadrilateral with 4 right angles (and opposite sides equal). A square has 4 right angles and its opposite sides are equal, so it meets every rule for a rectangle. It is a special rectangle whose sides happen to be all equal.

Worked example 5 (1 mark) ABCD is a parallelogram. Angle A = 65° and angle B = 115°. Write the sizes of angle C and angle D.

  1. In a parallelogram, opposite angles are equal.
  2. Angle C is opposite angle A, so angle C = 65°.
  3. Angle D is opposite angle B, so angle D = 115°.
  4. Answer: C = 65°, D = 115°.

Common mistake: thinking all four angles of a parallelogram are equal. That is only true for a rectangle (all 90°).

Worked example 6 (1 mark) Sketch a quadrilateral with exactly two lines of symmetry whose diagonals are not equal. Name it.

Two lines of symmetry → rectangle or rhombus. Diagonals not equal → not a rectangle. Answer: a rhombus — sketch 4 equal sides (ticks) with no right angles and draw the two diagonals as the dashed lines of symmetry. Common mistake: drawing a rectangle — its diagonals are equal.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking a rhombus is just a "tilted square". A square turned on its corner is still a square. A rhombus has no right angles (unless it is a square).
  • Saying a rectangle has 4 lines of symmetry. It has 2 — its diagonals are not lines of symmetry.
  • Saying a parallelogram has lines of symmetry. A (non-rectangular) parallelogram has none.
  • Saying "a square is not a rectangle". It is — a special one.
  • Mixing up trapezium (1 pair of parallel sides) and parallelogram (2 pairs).
  • Sketching freehand without a ruler, so that "parallel" sides clearly are not parallel. Use the grid and a ruler.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Name the shape from a list of clues — go through the clues one at a time and cross out shapes that fail.
  • Sort shapes into a Carroll or Venn diagram. Every shape must go somewhere; check each shape against BOTH properties.
  • Tick true or false: "All rhombuses are squares". Look for one counter-example to show a statement is false.
  • Draw or complete a shape on a square grid: copy the "moves" of one side to draw the side parallel to it.
  • Join dots or draw a line to split a shape into two named quadrilaterals.
  • "Explain" questions: name the property that decides it (e.g. "it has no right angles") — vague answers like "it looks different" get no mark.

Practice

Remember
  • Describe a quadrilateral using sides, angles, parallel sides, lines of symmetry and diagonals.
  • Square: everything equal, 4 right angles, 4 lines of symmetry, diagonals equal and perpendicular.
  • Rectangle 2 lines of symmetry; rhombus 2 (its diagonals); parallelogram 0; kite 1; isosceles trapezium 1.
  • A square is a special rectangle AND a special rhombus; rectangles and rhombuses are special parallelograms.
  • Parallelogram: 2 pairs of parallel sides. Trapezium: 1 pair. Kite: none, but 2 pairs of equal adjacent sides.
  • On a grid, a parallel side copies the same "moves" (squares across and up).
