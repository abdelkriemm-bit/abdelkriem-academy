Checkpoint 8 · Paper 1 writing (non-fiction)

Review Writing

How to review a book, film, place, event or product for Paper 1: features, a paragraph-by-paragraph plan for every type, phrases, vocabulary and a strong 350+ word sample.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is a review?

A review is a piece of writing that gives your personal, evaluative opinion of something — a book, a film, a place, an event or a product — and supports that opinion with specific examples, so that the reader can decide whether it is worth their time or money. To evaluate means to judge: what is good, what is weak, and how good it is overall.

A review has a double purpose: to inform (the reader learns what the thing is) and to persuade or advise (the reader learns whether to try it). It is not a retelling of the story and it is not an advert. A good review is honest: it praises what deserves praise and criticises what deserves criticism, politely and with reasons.

QuestionAnswer for a review
PurposeTo inform the reader about something and give a balanced, supported opinion and a recommendation.
AudienceUsually readers of a school newsletter, school magazine, website or blog — often students your age and their parents.
ToneLively and personal, but clear and controlled. Semi-formal for a school newsletter. Slightly chattier for a blog.
Person and tenseFirst person for your opinion (I found, I would recommend). Present tense for the book/film/product (the plot follows, the screen is bright). Past tense for your own visit or experience (we arrived, the queue was long).
Where it appearsPaper 1 (Non-fiction), Section B Writing.

Where the review sits in the exam

Year 8 students sit the Cambridge Lower Secondary Progression Test Stage 8 in school, and later the Checkpoint (0861) test. Both have the same format: Paper 1 Non-fiction and Paper 2 Fiction. Each paper has Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks), and each paper lasts 1 hour 10 minutes. On recent papers about 35 minutes is suggested for the writing section.

Has the review really come up?
  • Yes. A review for a school newsletter has been set as the writing task on a Stage 8 progression test, so this genre deserves serious practice.
  • A review can also be tested in Section A Reading: for example, a question asking you to name the features of a review in a text, or the writer's opinion with evidence.
  • The task may name any type: a book, film, place, event or product. Learn the basic shape once, then adapt the middle paragraphs to the type (see the table in section 3).

How your review is marked (25 marks)

Every Paper 1 writing task is marked out of 25 in five categories. The teacher's older names are in brackets.

CategoryMarksWhat the top band needs in a review
Creation of texts (content, purpose and audience)5Clearly a review, not a story or advert. Every bullet in the task covered. Opinions are developed with specific examples and reasons. The reader is kept interested and the viewpoint is clear and consistent from start to finish.
Vocabulary and language3Precise evaluative words chosen for effect (gripping, predictable, well-paced, value for money) instead of good/bad/nice. Language suits the reader of a newsletter or magazine.
Structure of texts (text structure)7Title, hooking introduction, short summary, strengths, weaknesses, verdict. Clear paragraphs that each do one job. Smooth links between paragraphs (However, In addition, Overall). An ending that gives a clear recommendation.
Grammar and punctuation (sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7A range of sentence types used for effect (a short punchy verdict, a long complex sentence of detail). Accurate verb tenses. Varied, correct punctuation: commas, apostrophes, colon, semicolon, dashes, inverted commas.
Word structure (spelling)3Correct spelling of everyday and ambitious words (recommend, disappointing, atmosphere, character, definitely).
Two rules that cost a lot of marks
  • Wrong text type: if you write a story about going to the cinema instead of reviewing the film, Creation of texts is capped at 1 and Structure is capped too. Read the task and write a review.
  • Too short: very short answers are capped (21–60 words can earn no more than 7 marks in total). For Year 8, train yourself to write at least 350 words in the time.
How to get full marks — the quick version
  • Name the thing in the title and the first paragraph (what it is, who made it, where it is).
  • Summarise briefly — no more than one paragraph — and never spoil the ending.
  • Give at least two strengths and one weakness, each with a specific example and a reason.
  • Use evaluative vocabulary and at least one comparison with something similar.
  • Finish with a clear verdict and a recommendation to a specific group of people.
  • Vary your sentences on purpose: long complex sentences for detail, a short one for a verdict.
  • Use a range of punctuation correctly on purpose: colon, semicolon, dashes, apostrophes.

2. Features of a review

FeatureExample
Engaging title naming the subjectThe Grand Escape: Is This Year's Big Adventure Film Worth the Hype?
Hook + first opinion in the introductionFrom the very first scene, I knew this was not going to be an ordinary superhero film.
Basic facts (who, what, where, when, price)Written by…, published in…. Open daily from 9 a.m.. Costs 450 pounds
Brief summary without spoilersThe story follows a young inventor who… but I will not reveal how it ends.
Evaluation of key elementsBook/film: plot, characters, pace, setting, ending. Place: atmosphere, service, cleanliness, price. Product: design, quality, battery, value for money.
Specific examples as evidenceThe battle in the flooded city, filmed almost in silence, is the most powerful scene.
Balanced view: strengths and weaknessesAlthough the food was delicious, we waited forty minutes for a table.

Comparison with similar thingsUnlike the first film in the series, this one gives the villain a real reason for his anger.
Direct address to the readerIf you love puzzles, you will not be able to put it down.
Recommendation to a specific audienceI would recommend it to families with children over eight.
Verdict / rating (optional)Verdict: 4 out of 5 stars.

Tense trap

Use the present tense for what the book, film or product is and does (The plot moves quickly. The phone feels light.). Use the past tense for your own experience (When we visited, the museum was crowded). Mixing them randomly loses Grammar marks.

3. How to structure a review

Use this five-part plan for every review. The opening and closing never change. Only the middle content changes with the type.

PartWhat to writeLength
TitleName the subject + a hint of your opinion. Example: Holes by Louis Sachar: A Desert Full of Secrets.1 line
Paragraph 1 — IntroductionHook (question, 'Imagine…', bold statement). Say what it is: genre, author/director/maker, place. Why you chose it. A first hint of your opinion.3–4 sentences
Paragraph 2 — Summary / descriptionBook or film: the situation and main character's problem, no ending. Place or event: getting there, what is on offer. Product: buying it, design and main features.4–5 sentences
Paragraph 3 — What you likedTwo or three strengths, each with a specific example and why it worked for you.5–6 sentences
Paragraph 4 — What you did not likeOne or two weaknesses, explained fairly. A comparison with something similar fits well here.3–4 sentences
Paragraph 5 — Verdict and recommendationSum up your overall opinion. Who should try it and why? Who might not enjoy it? Optional star rating.3–4 sentences

Plan with G.A.A.P. in the first 3–4 minutes
  • G — Genre: a review (layout: title, paragraphs, verdict).
  • A — Audience: who reads it? School newsletter = students and parents, a website = the public.
  • A — Area of content: list every bullet point in the task and give each one a paragraph.
  • P — Purpose: inform + give an honest, supported opinion + recommend.

How to start: five openings that work

  1. 'Imagine…': Imagine a museum where you are allowed to touch almost everything. That is the promise of the new science centre in our city.
  2. A question: Can a two-hour film about a lost football really keep a whole family glued to the screen?
  3. A bold opinion: This is, without doubt, the most surprising book I have read this year.
  4. A short scene: The lights went down, the drums began, and the whole school hall fell silent.
  5. Expectation vs reality: After months of adverts, I expected great things from these headphones. What I got was a pleasant surprise and one big disappointment.

How to end: five closings that work

  1. Clear recommendation: I would strongly recommend it to anyone aged eleven and over who enjoys a good mystery.
  2. Balanced verdict: Despite its slow start, it is well worth your time.
  3. Who should avoid it: However, if you dislike loud music, you may prefer to stay at home.
  4. Short punchy sentence: Go. You will not regret it.
  5. Star rating: Verdict: four stars out of five — nearly perfect.

4. Useful phrases and sentence starters

Introducing the subject

  • From its intriguing title to its striking cover, [book] caught my attention immediately.
  • Having heard so many recommendations, I was eager to try [product/place] for myself.
  • With [director]'s reputation for exciting films, I had high expectations for [film].
  • Set in [place], [book/film] tells the story of…

Summarising (without spoilers)

  • The story follows [character], who…
  • Without giving away too much, I can say that…
  • The central question of the story is whether…
  • I will not spoil the ending, but…

Describing strengths

  • One of the greatest strengths of [X] is its [feature], which…
  • What impressed me most was…
  • A particular highlight was [example], because…
  • [X] really comes to life when…
  • I was particularly struck by…

Describing weaknesses (politely)

  • Nevertheless, [X] is not without its flaws.
  • Although [X] is strong in many ways, its [weakness] could have been better.
  • At times, the [pace/service] felt uneven, which…
  • One aspect that let it down was…
  • Unfortunately, [X] falls short when it comes to…

Comparing

  • Compared with [similar thing], [X] is far more…
  • Unlike [similar thing], [X] manages to…
  • Fans of [similar thing] will find plenty to enjoy here.

Verdict and recommendation

  • Overall, [X] is a [adjective] [type] that I would recommend to…
  • Despite a few weaknesses, it is well worth [reading/watching/visiting].
  • It is a must-[read/see] for anyone who enjoys…
  • If you prefer…, however, you might want to look elsewhere.
  • My verdict: [number] out of five stars.

5. Strong sample: book review (full answer)

Sample: book review for the school newsletter455 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: Your school newsletter is starting a 'Book Corner' page. Write a review of a book you have enjoyed. In your review you should say: • what the book is about • what you liked and did not like about it • who you would recommend it to and why.

Holes by Louis Sachar: A Desert Full of Secrets

Imagine being sent to a camp where the only activity is digging a hole in the burning desert, every single day, whatever the weather. That is exactly what happens to Stanley Yelnats, the unlucky hero of Holes, a mystery-adventure novel by the American author Louis Sachar. I chose it from our class library because the strange title made me curious, and I finished it in three evenings, which says a great deal about how gripping it is.

Stanley is wrongly accused of stealing a pair of trainers that belonged to a famous baseball player. Instead of going to prison, he is sent to Camp Green Lake, which, ironically, has no lake at all: it dried up more than a hundred years ago. Every boy there must dig a hole five feet wide and five feet deep each day, supposedly to 'build character'. Before long, Stanley begins to suspect that the Warden is not interested in character at all — she is searching for something buried in the ground. Meanwhile, the novel tells two older stories, one about Stanley's great-great-grandfather and a broken promise, and one about a gentle schoolteacher who became a feared outlaw.

The greatest strength of Holes is its clever structure. At first, the three stories seem completely unrelated. However, small details — a jar of peaches, a pair of old trainers, a mountain shaped like a thumb — keep reappearing until the pieces click together like a jigsaw. I found myself turning back the pages to check clues I had missed. The characters are equally memorable. Stanley begins as a lonely boy with no friends, but his loyalty to Zero, a silent boy whom everyone else ignores, made me admire him more with every chapter. Sachar's humour also stops the book from becoming too dark: the narrator describes the cruel adults so dryly that I laughed out loud more than once.

Nevertheless, the novel is not perfect. The opening chapters move slowly, and some readers may give up before the mystery really begins. In addition, a few of the adult characters, such as the counsellor Mr Pendanski, are rather one-sided; they exist mainly to be unpleasant, and I would have liked to understand them better. Compared with fast-paced adventure series, Holes asks for more patience.

Overall, Holes is an intelligent, funny and surprisingly moving book that rewards patient readers. I would recommend it to students aged eleven to fourteen who enjoy mysteries, especially anyone who likes solving puzzles before the characters do. If you prefer non-stop action from the first page, be prepared to keep reading for a few chapters. Trust me: it is worth it.

My rating: four and a half stars out of five.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5: every bullet is covered — what it is about (P2), likes (P3), dislikes (P4), who and why (P5). The opinion is consistent and every judgement has an example (the jar of peaches, Zero's loyalty, Mr Pendanski). The writer talks to the reader ('Imagine…', 'Trust me') so the newsletter reader stays interested.
  • Vocabulary and language 3/3: precise evaluative words — gripping, ironically, memorable, dryly, one-sided, rewards patient readers, surprisingly moving — instead of 'good' and 'nice'. Tone suits students and parents.
  • Structure of texts 7/7: title naming book and author. Five paragraphs, each with one job (hook → summary → strengths → weaknesses → verdict). Clear links between paragraphs (Meanwhile, The greatest strength, Nevertheless, In addition, Overall). A comparison with fast-paced adventure series. The ending gives a specific audience (aged eleven to fourteen who like solving puzzles) and a rating.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7 (sentences): a range of sentence types used for effect — long complex sentences carrying detail in P2, a short sentence for impact ('The characters are equally memorable.'), and a brief, punchy ending ('Trust me: it is worth it.'). Present tense for the book and past tense for the writer's own reading experience are kept accurate.
  • Grammar and punctuation 7/7 (punctuation): colon for explanation (no lake at all: it dried up…) and for emphasis (Trust me: it is worth it.). Semicolon joining two closely related sentences (…are rather one-sided; they exist mainly to be unpleasant…). A pair of dashes for extra information (— a jar of peaches… a thumb —). A single dash for emphasis (— she is searching for something buried…). Inverted commas for words not meant literally ('build character'). Apostrophes for possession (Stanley's, Sachar's).
  • Word structure (spelling) 3/3: ambitious words spelt correctly — supposedly, ironically, counsellor, character, recommend, unpleasant, mystery-adventure, great-great-grandfather.
  • No spoilers: the reader learns the situation and the mystery, but not how the three stories connect or how the book ends.

6. Common mistakes

  • Retelling the whole story — the summary takes half the answer and there is no evaluation. Keep the summary to one paragraph.
  • Spoiling the ending — a reader who has not seen the film will be annoyed. Stop at the main problem.
  • Opinions with no evidence — 'The acting was amazing.' Which actor? Which scene? Why?
  • Only praise — a review with no weakness sounds like an advert. Add at least one fair criticism.
  • Weak vocabulary — good, bad, nice, boring repeated. Use the vocabulary list above.
  • Vague recommendation — 'Everyone should read it.' Name a specific group and say why.
  • Wrong tense — mixing past and present without a reason.
  • Forgetting a bullet point — every bullet in the task carries content marks.
Timing (about 35 minutes for writing)
  • 3–4 minutes: G.A.A.P. and a five-box plan (one box per paragraph).
  • 25–27 minutes: write about 350–450 words.
  • 4–5 minutes: check spelling, tenses, apostrophes and that every bullet is covered.
Checklist before you hand in
  • Title names the book/film/place/event/product.
  • Introduction has a hook and says what it is and why I chose it.
  • Summary is short and has no spoilers.
  • At least two strengths and one weakness, each with a specific example.
  • At least one comparison with something similar.
  • Five evaluative words that are better than good/bad.
  • Colon, semicolon, dash and apostrophe used correctly at least once.
  • Clear verdict and recommendation to a specific audience.

Practice tasks

  1. Task 1 (place): Your school's website has a page called 'Weekend Ideas'. Write a review of a restaurant or café you have visited. Include: • where it is and why you went • the food, service and atmosphere • who you would recommend it to. Write a five-paragraph plan.

    Show answer
    Title: 'Breakfast with a View: A Review of [café name], [area]'. P1: hook ('Few things beat breakfast by the Nile'), then location, opening hours, price range and why you went (a cousin's birthday). P2: arriving, booking, first impressions of the terrace and decoration. P3 liked: fresh bread and ful, quick friendly waiters, calm atmosphere in the morning, with an example of one dish. P4 did not like: noisy after 11 a.m., small portions for the price, and a comparison with another café. P5: recommend for families and early risers, advise booking a terrace table, 4/5.

  2. Task 2 (event): Write a review of a school trip or concert for your school magazine. Include: • what happened • the best and worst parts • whether it should be repeated next year. Write a five-paragraph plan.

    Show answer
    Title: 'Lights, Music, Action: The Winter Concert Reviewed'. P1: short scene opening (lights dim, choir begins), then date, place, who organised it. P2: running order — choir, orchestra, drama sketch, Year 9 band. P3 best parts: the Year 9 band's final song, clear organisation, colourful stage design, with one specific moment. P4 worst parts: started thirty minutes late, sound problems in the drama sketch. P5: verdict — should definitely be repeated, with a sound check and a printed programme. Recommend that parents come early for seats.

  3. Task 3 (product): A teenage technology magazine asks readers to review a gadget they use every day. Write the opening paragraph and the final paragraph only.

    Show answer
    Opening (example): 'When my old phone finally gave up in the middle of a revision session, I needed a replacement that was cheap, fast and tough. After weeks of reading comparisons, I chose the [model], which costs around [price]. Two months later, I can honestly say it has surprised me — mostly in a good way.' Closing (example): 'Overall, the [model] is excellent value for money. Its battery and camera beat phones that cost twice as much, although the slow charging is a real drawback. If you are a student who needs a reliable phone for study and photos, I would recommend it without hesitation. My verdict: four stars out of five.'

  4. Improve this weak review sentence: 'The film was very good and the actors were nice and I liked it.'

    Show answer
    Example: 'The film is gripping from start to finish, and the lead actor's quiet, nervous performance in the exam-hall scene makes the character feel completely real.' (Present tense for the film, precise vocabulary, a specific example and a reason.)
