Checkpoint 8 · Paper 2 writing (fiction)

Adventure Stories

How to write a fast, tense adventure story set in the real world — features, a five-part arc, action phrases, vocabulary and a full-mark 350+ word sample set in Egypt’s White Desert.

British & IGCSE › Checkpoint 8 English (Cambridge Lower Secondary 0861)

1. What is adventure writing?

Adventure writing follows a brave, active character through real physical danger — a storm, a cliff, a flood, a jungle, a desert, a race against time — while they try to reach a clear goal: to survive, to rescue someone, to escape, or to discover something. The world is recognisably OUR world (a mountain, the sea, the Sinai, ancient ruins). There is no magic: the danger comes from nature, the clock and sometimes other people.

Adventure is the most natural genre for many Paper 2 tasks, because so many themes involve a journey, a trip, a challenge or something that goes wrong. A good adventure story is not just action, though. The examiner rewards a story where the reader feels the danger through the senses and sees the character change because of what they survive.

Adventure is…Adventure is NOT…
Real-world danger: weather, terrain, injury, time pressureMagic, dragons or spells (that is fantasy)
One clear goal that drives every paragraphA list of fun holiday activities
Fast pace in action, slower in quiet momentsNon-stop fighting with no feelings
A character who solves the problem using skill and courageA character who is simply rescued by luck
Believable events (a jeep breaks down, a rope is too short)Impossible coincidences

Purpose: to thrill and entertain — to make the reader’s heart beat faster. Audience: the examiner. Write as if your reader is sitting on the edge of their seat.

Where it sits in the exam
  • Paper 2 is the Fiction paper. It lasts 1 hour 10 minutes and is worth 50 marks: Section A Reading (25 marks) and Section B Writing (25 marks).
  • Section B is one story task. It is usually linked by theme to the fiction extract you read in Section A, and it gives you bullet prompts (for example: who the character is, where the story happens, what goes wrong, how it ends).
  • The suggested time for Section B is about 35 minutes, and planning space is given. Planning is not marked, but it is where good stories are won.
  • The task will not always say "write an adventure story". It gives you a theme. YOU decide that this genre is the best way to answer it — and then you must stay inside that genre from the first line to the last.
  • The same format is used in the Stage 8 Progression Test you sit at school, so everything on this page works for both.

Themes that suit an adventure story: a trip that goes wrong, an unexpected event in a remote place, a challenge, a rescue, getting lost, a race against time, working as a team, facing a fear, a discovery, learning a new skill under pressure, a journey home.

How your story is marked (25 marks)

Mark categoryMarksWhat the top band looks like in this genre
Creation of texts5The story fits the theme and every bullet prompt. The danger feels real and believable. There is a clear goal and stakes. The main character solves the problem and changes. The reader is gripped throughout.
Vocabulary and language3Strong, precise verbs (crumbled, hauled, lunged, scrambled), setting vocabulary that fits the place (dunes, ridge, gorge, current), sensory words that put the reader inside the danger.
Structure of texts (Text structure)7A clear five-part arc (hook → build-up → rising action → climax → resolution). Pace is controlled — short paragraphs in action, longer ones in description. The ending links back to the opening.
Grammar and punctuation (Sentence structure, punctuation and grammar)7Sentence lengths deliberately varied for pace (fragments and short sentences in action, complex sentences for setting). Speech correctly punctuated. A range of punctuation used accurately.
Word structure (spelling)3Ambitious words spelt correctly (horizon, desperately, kilometres, emergency, exhausted, rescue, cousin, trainers). No tense slips that cause spelling errors (crept, swept, clung).
Total25Best fit: the examiner reads the whole story and matches it, row by row, to the band that describes it best. Planning is not marked.
Rules that can cap your mark
  • 20 words or fewer scores 0. A very short answer (about 21-60 words) can get no more than 7 marks out of 25.
  • If you write the wrong text type (for example, a diary or an article instead of a story), Creation of texts is capped at 1 and Structure of texts is capped too.
  • If the story drifts away from the task's theme, you lose Creation marks even if the writing is beautiful — keep the bullet prompts in front of you.

2. Features of adventure stories

FeatureWhat it meansExample sentence
A bold, active protagonistBrave and resourceful — but allowed to be scaredI wanted to say that I was only thirteen. Instead, I nodded.

Constant physical dangerWeather, terrain, injury, time pressureThe chalk crumbled under my trainers.

Immersive sensory detailSound, smell, touch — especially during dangerSand screamed against the windows.
Fast pace in actionShort sentences, strong verbs, little descriptionMy arms burned. My heart hammered.

Change in the characterThe survivor is not the same person who set out…the place where I had stopped being a child who waited for help.

FeatureHow to use itExample

The pace rule

Slow down for SETTING (longer sentences, senses, imagery). Speed up for ACTION (short sentences, strong verbs, almost no adjectives). Switching between the two is what makes an adventure story feel alive — and it is exactly what the examiner looks for under sentence variety and structure.

3. How to structure an adventure story

Plan with G.C.S.P.P.: Genre (adventure — what is the danger and what is the goal?), Characters (a protagonist + 1-2 companions. Give each one a job), Setting (one real place you can describe with three senses — choose somewhere you know, like Sinai, the Red Sea or the White Desert), Point of view (first person makes the danger personal), Plot (whole story, opening or continuation of the extract).

Then use the five-part arc. Treat it as a spine, not a cage.

PartParagraph(s)What to writeTip
1. Hook1-2Drop the reader into a charged moment: dialogue, action or a line that hints at what will go wrong.Ironic hooks work well: “Ten minutes,” he said. (It was not ten minutes.)
2. Build-up — character, setting, stakes3-4Introduce the companions with contrast, let the setting press in through the senses, and make the goal clear.Start the clock here (a time, a sunset, a tide).
3. Rising action — complications multiply5-9Each obstacle is worse than the last: the weather turns, equipment fails, someone is injured, time runs out. The protagonist makes a plan.Every complication must make the goal harder, not just add noise.
4. Climax — the decisive danger10-14One concentrated, high-stakes moment. Sentences shorten, verbs carry the weight, the protagonist ACTS.Add a moment of near-failure just before success ("Nothing.").
5. Resolution — cost and change15-17Show what survival cost and how the character has changed. Link back to the opening.End on a changed character or an echo of the first line, not a summary.
MinutesWhat you do
0–5Read the task twice. Underline the theme and every bullet prompt. Plan with G.C.S.P.P. (Genre, Characters, Setting, Point of view, Plot) and jot a short note for each paragraph.
5–30Write. Aim for at least 350 words (strong answers are often 400–550 words). By about minute 17 you should have reached the first complication.
30–35Proofread: spelling of your ambitious words, full stops (no comma splices), speech punctuation, capital letters, tense consistency.
Make your plan believable

Ask yourself: Could this really happen? Would a 13-year-old really be able to do this? A believable solution (torches and SOS from a high point, a rope used in two stages, following a river downhill) earns more Creation marks than a superhero escape.

4. Useful phrases and vocabulary

Swap the words in brackets to match your setting. Use one or two per stage.

Openings

  • “[Ten minutes / Just one more photo / It’s completely safe],” [Name] said. That was the first mistake.
  • The [rope / map / water bottle] had [forty metres / one route / half a litre] left, and that was not enough.
  • The [jungle / desert / coastline] pressed in on every side, alive with sounds I couldn’t name.
  • Somewhere beyond the [ridge / reef / dunes], the real danger was only just beginning.

Describing the setting

  • The [desert / ocean / ice field] stretched endlessly in every direction, offering no shelter and no mercy.
  • A [biting wind / thick fog / relentless sun] swept across the [plateau / valley].
  • The sky to the west had turned the colour of a bruise.
  • Every [step / wave / gust] reminded us how far we were from safety.

Characters and teamwork

  • [Name]’s hands trembled around the [rope / torch], though nothing in [her/his] voice gave it away.
  • Practical as ever, [Name] rechecked the [knots / supplies / route] one final time.
  • “We’re not turning back,” [Name] said, though [her/his] voice betrayed the fear underneath.
  • “Focus,” [Name] said sharply, hauling me up by the arm. “We’re almost there.”

Physical action

  • The rope snapped tight as I swung across the [gap / current], my pulse hammering in my ears.
  • My boots slid on the [wet rock / loose scree], and for a heartbeat there was nothing beneath me at all.
  • With a final, desperate lunge, I caught the [ledge / branch] just as the ground gave way.
  • I scrambled, slipped, scrambled again.

Climax

  • There was no more time to think — only to move.
  • Nothing. Then, far to the east, [a light / a horn / a voice] answered.
  • For a heartbeat, the storm, the roar and my own heartbeat merged into one sound.
  • I didn’t remember deciding to act. Only that, somehow, I had.

Endings

  • We stood in silence, exhausted and barely recognisable from the people who had set out.
  • I looked back once at the [mountain / dunes / shoreline], then turned towards home.
  • Whatever came next, I knew now exactly what I was capable of.
  • “[Repeat the opening line],” [Name] said, and this time everyone laughed.
Word / phraseMeaning / use
perilous (adj.)full of danger — the perilous path along the cliff edge
treacherous (adj.)dangerous in a hidden way — treacherous sand that slid under every step
relentless (adj.)never stopping — the relentless sun
uncharted (adj.)not on any map, unexplored — uncharted canyons beyond the ridge
forbidding (adj.)looking threatening — the forbidding cliff face
vast (adj.)very large — the vast, empty desert
harrowing (adj.)extremely distressing — a harrowing night on the mountain
exhilarating (adj.)thrilling — the exhilarating final descent
tenacious (adj.)refusing to give up — tenacious to the last, she held the rope
resilient (adj.)able to recover from hardship — the team stayed resilient
trepidation (n.)fearful worry about what will happen — I approached the edge with trepidation
vantage point (n.)a high place with a clear view — from our vantage point on the ridge
scramble (v.)to climb or move quickly using hands and feet — we scrambled up the dune
haul (v.)to pull with great effort — he hauled himself onto the ledge
stagger (v.)to walk unsteadily — we staggered into camp just before nine

5. Strong sample answer

Adventure story — Ten Minutes523 words · Strong answer — Creation 5/5 · Vocabulary 3/3 · Structure 7/7 · Grammar & punctuation 7/7 · Spelling 3/3
Task: In the extract you have read, a family trip into the countryside does not go as planned. Write a story about a journey that suddenly becomes dangerous. In your story you should: describe where the characters are and why they are there, explain what goes wrong, and show how the main character deals with the danger and how the story ends.

“Ten minutes,” Uncle Hany said, slamming the jeep door. “We’ll photograph the Mushroom Rock and be back at camp before the tea is cold.”

That was at four o’clock. By half past four, the sky to the west had turned the colour of a bruise.

My cousin Laila noticed it first. She lowered her camera and pointed at the horizon, where a wall of orange dust was rolling towards us, swallowing the chalk towers of the White Desert one by one.

“Sandstorm,” Uncle Hany muttered. “Into the jeep. Now!”

The storm hit us like a fist. Sand screamed against the windows, and the world outside vanished into a churning, choking cloud. For an hour we sat with scarves wrapped over our mouths, listening to the jeep rock and groan. When the wind finally dropped, the engine coughed twice and died. Uncle Hany climbed out to check it, slipped on the loose sand beside a rock and fell heavily. I heard the crack before I heard him cry out.

His ankle was already swelling. His phone had no signal, the radio crackled uselessly, and the sun was sinking fast.

“Karim,” he said through gritted teeth, “listen carefully. The camp is east of here — perhaps three kilometres. When the rangers come looking, they’ll follow the old track. You need to make sure they see us.”

Three kilometres. In the dark. I wanted to say that I was only thirteen, that I had never been anywhere without a map on a screen. Instead, I nodded.

Laila and I gathered everything useful: two torches, a bottle of water and the silver emergency blanket from the first-aid kit. Then I looked at the tallest chalk tower, a crooked white giant standing alone above the dunes.

“From up there,” I said, “they’ll see a light for miles.”

The climb was harder than it looked. The chalk crumbled under my trainers, and twice my foot slipped, leaving me hanging by my fingertips while Laila shouted directions from below. My arms burned. My heart hammered. Halfway up, I made the mistake of looking down, and the ground seemed to tilt beneath me.

Don’t stop, I told myself. Don’t think. Climb.

At the top, I spread the silver blanket over the rock so that it would catch the light of the rising moon. Then I switched on both torches and swept them across the desert in the only pattern I knew: three short flashes, three long, three short. SOS. Over and over, until my wrists ached and my eyes stung with sand.

Nothing. Only the wind and the endless, silver dunes.

Then, far to the east, two headlights blinked back.

The rangers reached us just before nine. Later, sitting by the campfire with Uncle Hany’s ankle strapped and a cup of sweet tea in my shaking hands, I looked up at the chalk tower glowing pale in the moonlight. That afternoon, it had been just a rock to photograph; tonight, it was the place where I had stopped being a child who waited for help.

Uncle Hany raised his cup towards me and smiled. “Ten minutes,” he said. Everyone laughed — even me.

Why this gets the marks
  • Creation of texts 5/5 — every bullet: where and why (a short trip to photograph the Mushroom Rock in the White Desert, paragraphs 1-3), what goes wrong (sandstorm, engine failure, injury, sunset — 3-6), how Karim deals with it and how it ends (8-17). The solution is believable for a 13-year-old: torches, an emergency blanket and SOS from a high point.
  • Creation — viewpoint and character: first person lets us hear Karim’s doubt ("I wanted to say that I was only thirteen") and his growth ("the place where I had stopped being a child who waited for help"). Uncle Hany’s injury raises the stakes and gives Karim the responsibility.
  • Vocabulary 3/3 — strong verbs (slamming, swallowing, screamed, crumbled, swept, blinked) and setting words that fit a real Egyptian desert (chalk towers, dunes, track, rangers). Imagery: simile ("like a fist"), metaphor ("a crooked white giant"), personification ("Sand screamed"), colour image ("the colour of a bruise").
  • Structure 7/7 — five-part arc: ironic hook (1) → build-up and setting (2-3) → rising action with multiplying problems (4-9) → climax on the chalk tower, including a near-failure ("Nothing.") before success (10-15) → resolution showing the cost and the change (16-17).
  • Structure — cohesion and pace: time connectives track the ticking clock (four o’clock, half past four, for an hour, just before nine). One-line paragraphs mark turning points. The last line repeats "Ten minutes" from the opening, now as a joke — a circular ending.
  • Grammar & punctuation 7/7 — sentence variety for pace: a long complex sentence for the storm (paragraph 5), fragments for fear ("Three kilometres. In the dark."), short main clauses in action ("My arms burned. My heart hammered."), and inner-voice commands ("Don’t think. Climb.").
  • Grammar & punctuation — range: a colon for a list ("everything useful: two torches, a bottle of water and…") and for explanation ("the only pattern I knew: three short flashes…"), a semicolon joining two related independent clauses in the final reflection ("…just a rock to photograph; tonight, it was the place…"), a single dash for emphasis ("— perhaps three kilometres", "— even me"), speech correctly punctuated and split by a reporting clause (“Karim,” he said through gritted teeth, “listen carefully.”).
  • Spelling 3/3 — ambitious and tricky words correct: photograph, horizon, swallowing, churning, uselessly, kilometres, emergency, crumbled, fingertips, directions, strapped. Apostrophes and contractions correct (o’clock, We’ll, they’ll, Don’t, Uncle Hany’s).

6. Common mistakes and final checklist

Common mistakeWhy it loses marksFix
A long, slow start: breakfast, packing, the car journeyWeak hook and wasted words (Structure)Start at the moment just before things go wrong.
A holiday diary: "Then we swam. Then we ate. Then we went to the market."No conflict, so it is barely a story (Creation)One goal and one danger that grows.
The character is rescued by luck or by adultsWeak climax. No change in the characterThe protagonist makes the plan and takes the risk.
Unbelievable events (fighting a lion with bare hands)Loses credibility and Creation marksKeep it plausible: weather, terrain, injury, time.
The same sentence length all the way throughGrammar & punctuation stays in the middle bandLong sentences for setting, short ones for action.
Overusing fragments and exclamation marks (!!!)Looks childish. Punctuation used without controlA few fragments at the key moment. One exclamation mark at most.
Weak verbs: went, got, moved, didVocabulary band dropsscrambled, hauled, lunged, staggered, plunged
Switching tense in the excitement (I run… I ran…)Grammar and spelling errorsPast tense all the way through.
Checklist before you hand in
  • I answered the theme and every bullet prompt.
  • My hook drops the reader into a moment — no breakfast, no packing.
  • There is a clear goal and a ticking clock.
  • Each complication makes things harder, and there is a near-failure before the climax.
  • My main character solves the problem and has changed by the end.
  • I slowed down for setting and sped up for action.
  • I used a colon, a semicolon and a dash correctly, and my dialogue is punctuated properly.
  • I checked the spelling of my setting words and stayed in the past tense.

Practice tasks

  1. Practice task 1: The extract you read is about a character who has to be brave in a difficult situation. Write a story about someone who faces a challenge in a remote place. You should describe the place, the challenge and how the character overcomes it. Write a G.C.S.P.P. plan.

    Show answer
    G: adventure. C: Youssef (12, afraid of deep water), his older sister Mariam (strong swimmer, injured), their diving instructor on the boat. S: a snorkelling trip off a reef near Dahab, Red Sea. P: first person, past tense. Plot — 1 hook: "Stay with the group," the instructor said. 2 setting: turquoise water, coral, the boat a white dot. 3 problem: a strong current drags the siblings away from the boat. 4 complication: Mariam cuts her foot on coral and panics. 5 plan: Youssef remembers the instructor’s rule — swim across the current, not against it. 6 climax: he tows Mariam sideways, lungs burning, waves breaking over his snorkel, until the current lets go. 7 resolution: they reach a buoy, wave the orange safety tube, and the boat turns. Ending: Youssef, who had never gone past the shallow end, looks back at the deep blue and is not afraid.

  2. Practice task 2: Rewrite this weak action paragraph to improve pace and vocabulary: "We went up the mountain and it was very hard and then the rock broke and I nearly fell but I got the rope and I was OK."

    Show answer
    Example: The path narrowed to a ledge no wider than my foot. I edged along it, cheek pressed to the cold rock. Then the stone beneath my left boot cracked. Gave way. For a heartbeat there was nothing below me but air — until my fingers closed round the rope. I hung there, gasping, while pebbles clattered into the gorge. — Why it is better: strong verbs (edged, cracked, closed, clattered), a fragment at the moment of danger, sensory detail, and the long sentence slows the moment after the fall.

  3. Look at the sample story. Why does the writer use the fragments "Three kilometres. In the dark."? Give every possible answer.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to show Karim’s thoughts as he takes in the danger, word by word. To emphasise how far and how frightening the walk would be. To slow the pace and create a pause of shock. To build tension and suspense before his decision. To make the reader feel his fear.

  4. Why does the writer use the simile "The storm hit us like a fist"? Give every possible answer.

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to compare the storm to a punch so the reader feels how sudden and violent it is. To make the storm seem alive and aggressive, like an attacker. To create a frightening, tense atmosphere. To show how powerless the characters are against nature.

  5. Why does the writer use the colon in "everything useful: two torches, a bottle of water and the silver emergency blanket"?

    Show answer
    Possible answers: to introduce a list of the items they gathered. To give the reader extra information about what "everything useful" means. To show how little they have to work with, which raises the tension.
