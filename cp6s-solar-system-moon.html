The Solar System and the Phases of the Moon

The Sun, the eight planets and the Moon — where they are, how they move, and why the Moon seems to change shape during its monthly cycle.

1. The Solar System

The Sun is at the centre of the Solar System. It is a star — a huge ball of very hot gas that gives out its own light and heat. Eight planets travel around the Sun in paths called orbits. The Sun's gravity pulls on the planets and keeps them in their orbits.

  • Order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
  • A sentence to help you remember: My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming.
  • Inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are small and rocky.
  • Between Mars and Jupiter is the asteroid belt — millions of rocky lumps that also orbit the Sun.
  • Outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are giants made mostly of gas (and ice); all four have rings, Saturn's are the easiest to see.

How the planets move

  • Every planet orbits the Sun. All the planets orbit in the same direction and in nearly the same flat plane, like runners on a giant flat track.
  • The orbits are nearly circular (very slightly oval).
  • The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer it takes to complete one orbit. The time for one orbit is that planet's year.
  • Each planet also spins on its axis. The Earth spins once every 24 hours — this gives us day and night.
  • The Earth orbits the Sun once every 365¼ days — this is one Earth year.
  • Moons orbit planets. The Moon orbits the Earth about once a month, and the Earth carries the Moon with it as it orbits the Sun.

Science changes with evidence

For a very long time many people believed that the Earth was at the centre and that the Sun moved around it. In the 1500s and 1600s, astronomers such as Copernicus and Galileo used careful observations (Galileo used one of the first telescopes) to show that the planets, including the Earth, orbit the Sun. In 2006 scientists agreed to call Pluto a dwarf planet, which is why there are now eight planets, not nine. New evidence can change scientific ideas.

2. The Moon and its phases

The Moon does not make its own light. We see it because it reflects light from the Sun. At every moment, one half of the Moon is lit by the Sun (the half facing the Sun) and the other half is dark — just like day and night on Earth.

As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see different amounts of its lit half. That is why the Moon seems to change shape. The shapes are called the phases of the Moon. One full cycle of phases, from new moon to the next new moon, takes about 29½ days — about a month. (The word 'month' comes from 'Moon'.)

  • Waxing = the lit part we see is growing (from new moon to full moon).
  • Waning = the lit part we see is shrinking (from full moon back to new moon).
  • The Moon spins once on its axis in the same time that it takes to orbit the Earth, so we always see the same side of the Moon.
Observing over time

Draw the Moon at the same time every evening (or whenever you can see it) for a month, and write the date under each drawing. This is an observing over time enquiry. Your drawings will show the lit part growing until full moon and then shrinking. In the Islamic (Hijri) calendar each month begins when the first thin crescent (هلال) is seen just after new moon — this is how the start of Ramadan is decided.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Name the four planets nearest to the Sun, in order. [2 marks]

  • Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars (2 marks for all four in the correct order; 1 mark for the correct four in the wrong order).

Worked example 2 — Use the table of planets. (a) Describe the pattern between a planet's distance from the Sun and the time it takes to orbit the Sun. [1] (b) A dwarf planet orbits the Sun at about 5 900 million km. Predict whether it takes more or less than 165 years to orbit the Sun. Explain. [1]

  • (a) The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer it takes to orbit.
  • (b) More than 165 years, because it is further from the Sun than Neptune (4 500 million km), and Neptune takes 165 years.

Common mistake: describing only one planet ('Neptune takes 165 years'). A pattern must link both things: further → longer.

Worked example 3 — Explain why we can see the Moon at night even though it does not give out light. [2 marks]

  • Light from the Sun hits the Moon (1 mark).
  • The Moon reflects the light and some of it travels to our eyes on Earth (1 mark).

Worked example 4 — Tonight Nour sees a first quarter Moon (right half lit). (a) What will the Moon look like in about one week? [1] (b) Explain why the Moon seems to change shape. [2]

  • (a) A full moon (about 7 days after first quarter).
  • (b) Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun (1 mark). As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see different amounts of the lit half (1 mark).

Common mistake: saying the Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon. That only happens during a lunar eclipse, not in the monthly phases.

Worked example 5 — Describe the movements of the Earth and the Moon. [3 marks]

  • The Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours (giving day and night) (1 mark).
  • The Earth orbits the Sun once every 365¼ days / one year (1 mark).
  • The Moon orbits the Earth about once a month (about 27 days; the phases repeat every 29½ days) (1 mark).
Common mistakes
  • Saying the Sun orbits the Earth. The Earth orbits the Sun; the Moon orbits the Earth.
  • Saying the Moon gives out its own light. It reflects sunlight.
  • Explaining phases with the Earth's shadow. Phases happen because we see different amounts of the Moon's sunlit half.
  • Mixing up spin (on its axis → day and night) and orbit (around the Sun → a year).
  • Mixing up waxing (growing) and waning (shrinking). Tip: from Egypt, a waxing Moon is lit on the right.
  • Thinking the planet nearest the Sun must be the hottest. Venus is hotter than Mercury.
How Checkpoint asks this
  • Order the planets, or name the planet in a given position.
  • Label a diagram — Sun, Earth, Moon, orbit; draw an arrow to show the direction of an orbit.
  • Read a data table of planets and describe a pattern (distance → time to orbit) or make a prediction for a new object.
  • Moon phases: draw or recognise the next phase, put phases in order, say about how many days between phases.
  • Explain why the Moon shines (reflection) and why its shape seems to change (the half lit by the Sun + the Moon's orbit).
  • Check the words in the question: 'orbit' and 'spin' have different answers.

Practice

Remember
  • The Sun (a star) is at the centre. Planets in order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.
  • All planets orbit the Sun in the same direction; the further from the Sun, the longer the orbit (the longer the year).
  • The Earth spins once in 24 hours (day and night) and orbits the Sun once in 365¼ days (a year). The Moon orbits the Earth about once a month.
  • The Moon reflects sunlight. Half of it is always lit; as it orbits the Earth we see different amounts of the lit half — the phases.
  • Phases: new → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full → waning gibbous → third quarter → waning crescent → new (about 29½ days).
