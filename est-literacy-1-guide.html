EST Literacy Test 1: Writing and Language

The skills, a pacing plan for 44 questions in 35 minutes, the 15 rules to know by heart and a passage-style practice quiz.

American Diploma › EST I Literacy — Complete Guide

Literacy Test 1 is the Writing and Language part of EST I. You act as an editor: you read texts and choose the version of a sentence, a punctuation mark, a word or a transition that makes the writing correct, clear and logical. This guide shows exactly which skills to master, how to pace yourself, the 15 rules to know by heart, and where each rule is fully taught on this site.

What this test tests

As published by schools, Literacy Test 1 has 44 multiple-choice questions in 35 minutes. Every question has four choices and asks you to improve the writing. Typical question stems (in our own words) look like this:

  • "Which choice completes the text so that it follows the rules of Standard English?" (grammar and punctuation)
  • "Which choice is the most concise and still keeps the meaning?" (concision)
  • "Which choice gives the most logical transition?" (transitions)
  • "Where is the most logical place for this sentence?" or "Should the writer add this sentence?" (organisation and relevance)
  • "Which choice best fits the tone of the passage?" (word choice)
Important

These figures are as published by schools; check the latest official EST candidate guide on est.moe.gov.eg before your test. The official question layout is not described in the published school information, so this page trains you on both formats you may meet: short texts with a blank and longer passages with numbered, underlined parts.

Strategy: 44 questions in 35 minutes

35 minutes ÷ 44 questions ≈ 48 seconds per question. That is tight, but grammar questions are fast once you know the rules. The secret is not to read faster — it is to recognise the rule quickly.

CheckpointWhere you should be
8 minutesQuestion 11 finished
16 minutesQuestion 22 finished
25 minutesQuestion 33 finished
33 minutesQuestion 44 finished — 2 minutes left to check flagged questions
  1. Look at the choices first. What changes between them? Commas only → punctuation. Verb forms → agreement or tense. Whole sentences → concision or organisation. The choices tell you the topic.
  2. Read the whole sentence, not only the underlined part or the blank — the rule usually depends on words before and after it.
  3. Name the rule in your head ("two complete sentences", "subject is singular", "extra information needs a pair").
  4. Eliminate every choice that breaks the rule. Usually two go immediately.
  5. Prefer the shortest correct choice. If two choices are both grammatical and mean the same, the shorter one is usually right.
  6. Plug it back in and read the sentence once more. Then move on — no second-guessing.
  7. Do not get stuck. If a question takes more than about a minute, choose your best answer, flag it and come back at the end if time allows.

The 15 rules to know by heart

As general guidance, these are the rules that appear again and again in writing-and-language tests. Learn each one with its ✓/✗ pair. The teacher's punctuation rules used on this site match what the test expects.

#Rule✗ Wrong✓ Right
1Two complete sentences cannot be joined by a comma alone (comma splice). Use a full stop, a semicolon, or a comma + and/but/so.The bus was late, we missed the start.The bus was late; we missed the start. / The bus was late, so we missed the start.
2Never put a single comma between a subject and its verb, or between a verb and its object.The students in my class, wrote a play.The students in my class wrote a play.
3Extra (non-essential) information needs a matching pair: comma…comma, dash…dash or bracket…bracket.My uncle — a pilot, flies every week.My uncle, a pilot, flies every week.
4Essential information (it tells you which one) takes no commas.Students, who finish early, may leave.Students who finish early may leave.
5A colon comes after a complete sentence and introduces a list, an explanation, emphasis or extra information.We need: flour, eggs and milk.We need three things: flour, eggs and milk.
6A semicolon joins two independent, closely related clauses — both sides must be complete sentences.She trained hard; hoping to win.She trained hard; she hoped to win.
7A verb agrees with its real subject. Ignore phrases like "of the…" between them.The box of old letters were found.The box of old letters was found.
8A pronoun agrees with the noun it replaces. A company, team or club is singular: it / its.The team lost their first match.The team lost its first match.
9its = belonging to it; it's = it is. their = belonging to them; they're = they are; there = a place.The cat licked it's paw.The cat licked its paw.
10Plural = -s, no apostrophe. Possessive = 's (singular) or s' (plural).The teacher's gave us books.The teachers gave us books. / The teachers' room is upstairs.
11Keep verb tenses consistent unless the time really changes.Yesterday she opens the door and walked in.Yesterday she opened the door and walked in.
12Every sentence needs a finite main verb. An -ing word or "to + verb" alone is not enough.The scientists studying the river for years.The scientists have studied the river for years.
13A modifier at the start of a sentence must describe the subject that comes right after the comma (no dangling modifiers).Walking to school, the rain started.Walking to school, I felt the rain start.
14Be concise: do not say the same thing twice.Each and every student must return the forms back.Every student must return the forms.
15Choose the transition that shows the real relationship: addition, contrast, result or example.The road was closed; therefore, we drove on it.The road was closed; therefore, we took another route.
Semicolons — the rule for this site

The semicolon has one main job: it joins two independent, closely related clauses. It never "shows contrast" by itself; the contrast comes from words such as however. (The SAT/ACT lessons also teach one exam-specific extra use for lists whose items already contain commas — see Colons, Dashes and Semicolons (open lesson).)

Organisation, relevance and word choice

Not every question is about a rule. Some ask you to think like the writer:

  • Add or delete? Keep a sentence only if it supports the paragraph's main point. A true but off-topic detail should be deleted. Read the reason in each choice — the right answer gives the right reason.
  • Sentence order. Look for pointing words: this, these, such, however, then, also. A sentence beginning "These changes…" must come after the changes are described.
  • Introductions and conclusions. The best first sentence introduces the paragraph's topic; the best last sentence sums it up or links back to the opening.
  • Word choice and tone. Match the passage: a formal article needs formal words (considerable, not huge); a precise word beats a vague one (increased by half, not changed a lot).

Worked examples

Example 1. Passage: "The volunteers cleaned the beach for six hours ______ by sunset, they had filled more than forty bags with rubbish." Question: Which choice completes the text so that it follows the rules of Standard English? A) hours, B) hours C) hours; D) hours, and,

Answer: C. Both sides are complete sentences ("The volunteers cleaned the beach for six hours" / "by sunset, they had filled…"), so a semicolon joins them correctly. A is a comma splice. B is a run-on (no punctuation at all). D has the right idea (comma + and) but adds a wrong comma after "and".

Example 2. Passage: "The new library app __allows students to borrow books and also lets them borrow books__ from their phones." Question: Which choice is most concise? A) NO CHANGE B) allows students to borrow books C) allows and permits students to borrow books D) allows students, in addition, to borrow books

Answer: B. The original says "borrow books" twice. C repeats the same idea with two verbs (allows and permits). D adds "in addition", which adds nothing. B keeps the full meaning in the fewest words — the classic "shortest correct choice" pattern.

Example 3. Passage: "[1] Our neighbourhood bakery opens at five every morning. [2] These loaves are usually sold out before seven. [3] By then, the baker has already made two hundred loaves of bread." Question: For the most logical order, sentence 2 should be placed: A) where it is now B) before sentence 1 C) after sentence 3 D) at the start of a new paragraph

Answer: C. "These loaves" must come after the loaves are mentioned, and they are first mentioned in sentence 3. A leaves "these loaves" pointing at nothing. B is worse — the reader does not yet know about the bakery. D separates the sentence from the idea it depends on.

Example 4. Passage: "Many people believe that camels store water in their humps. ______, the humps store fat, which the animal can use for energy when food is scarce." A) For example B) In fact C) Similarly D) As a result

Answer: B. The second sentence corrects a common belief, and "In fact" introduces the real situation. A (example) and C (similarity) do not fit a correction. D (result) says the belief caused the fat — illogical.

Classic traps
  • The comma splice: a comma between two complete sentences looks natural but is always wrong.
  • The lonely comma: one comma between subject and verb ("The girl in the red coat, waved").
  • The mismatched pair: extra information opened with a dash and closed with a comma.
  • The distractor noun: "The quality of the photos are…" — the subject is quality, so is.
  • The longer-sounds-smarter trap: wordy choices with repeated ideas are almost always wrong.
  • The right answer with the wrong reason: in add/delete questions, Yes/No is only half the answer; check the reason.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Read the choices first to find the rule.
  • Two complete sentences? Full stop, semicolon or comma + and/but/so.
  • Extra information? Matching pair. Essential? No commas.
  • Same meaning, all correct? Choose the shortest.
  • Hit your checkpoints: Q11 at 8 min, Q22 at 16, Q33 at 25.

Practice

Two short passages with numbered, underlined parts. For each question, choose the best version of the underlined part, or answer the question about the passage. "NO CHANGE" means the original is best. Untimed — but try to stay near 48 seconds per question.

Remember
  • Literacy Test 1 = Writing and Language, 44 questions in 35 minutes as published by schools (check the official candidate guide): about 48 seconds each.
  • Read the choices first — they tell you which rule is being tested.
  • Two complete sentences need a full stop, a semicolon or a comma + and/but/so; never a comma alone.
  • Extra information = matching pair; never a single comma between subject and verb.
  • When all choices are correct, pick the shortest one that keeps the meaning.
