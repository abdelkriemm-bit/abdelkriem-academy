American Diploma · ACT · Reading: Craft and Structure

Purpose, Function and Text Structure

How to explain why an author included a word, sentence or paragraph and how a whole ACT Reading passage is built, with the text-structure vocabulary the answer choices use, worked examples and a full practice passage.

American Diploma › ACT English and Reading — Complete Course

What this question type tests

Purpose and structure questions belong to the Craft and Structure category (26–33% of the scored ACT Reading questions, about 7 to 9 of the 27 scored questions). They do not ask what the passage says. They ask why the author put something there and how the parts fit together. Think of the author as a builder: every word, sentence and paragraph was placed to do a job.

LevelTypical stem (in our own words)What you must decide
Whole passage"The main purpose of the passage is to:"Why the author wrote the text: explain, argue, describe, reflect, compare
Paragraph"The third paragraph mainly functions to:"The job of the paragraph in the whole: example, objection, concession, extension, conclusion
Sentence or phrase"The last sentence of paragraph 2 mainly serves to:" / "The author mentions X in order to:"The job of that sentence where it sits: qualify, illustrate, emphasize, link
Organization"Which choice best describes how the passage is organized?"The order of the moves the author makes
Relationship"How does paragraph 5 relate to paragraph 4?"How one part responds to another: answers, extends, contradicts, gives an example of

The key idea: name the job, not the topic. "Paragraph 4 is about roots and water" is a topic. "Paragraph 4 admits the practical problems with trees" is a job. ACT answers almost always describe jobs.

Text-structure vocabulary

The answer choices are written with a small set of verbs. If you know exactly what each one means, half the question is done.

Word / phraseMeaning / use
introducesstarts a topic or an idea that the rest develops

elaborates on / developsadds more detail to the same idea

qualifieslimits a claim so it is less absolute (although, in some cases, depends on)

The method, step by step

A. For a part (a word, phrase, sentence or paragraph)

  1. Find the part and read its neighbours. Read the sentence before and the sentence after; for a paragraph, read the last line of the paragraph before and the first line of the paragraph after.
  2. Look for signal words. They tell you the job directly (see the table below).
  3. Ask: what would be missing if this part were deleted? If the reader would lose an example, the job is to illustrate. If the claim would become too strong, the job is to qualify.
  4. Say the job with a verb from the vocabulary list before you look at the choices.
  5. Check the WHOLE choice. Many wrong choices name a correct job for the wrong part, or add a wrong second half.
SignalUsual job
for example, for instance, consider, a story, one caseillustrates
although, while, in some cases, depends on, perhapsqualifies
of course, admittedly, none of this means, it is true thatconcedes
but, yet, however, still, these objections do notturns back to the author's view (rebuttal)
also, in addition, there is also, moreoveradds or extends
like, in the same way, as … as, resemblescompares (analogy)
so, therefore, this is why, in this respectdraws a conclusion

B. For the whole passage (purpose and organization)

  1. Make a 3-word map as you read: one short label per paragraph ("story: trees cut", "claim: infrastructure", "objections").
  2. Purpose: the right answer must fit the first AND the last paragraph and the author's attitude. Choose the verb carefully: "argue" needs a position; "describe" and "explain" are neutral; "reflect on" is personal.
  3. Organization: read your map from top to bottom, then test each choice step by step. One wrong step anywhere = wrong choice.

Right reasoning vs wrong reasoning

  • ✓ "The story of the cut trees introduces the topic with a concrete example." ✗ "The story of the cut trees is about road widening." (topic, not job)
  • ✓ "The sentence qualifies the claim." ("although the difference depends on…") ✗ "The sentence rejects the claim." (too strong: a qualification limits, it does not reject)
  • ✓ "The paragraph concedes that planting is costly." ✗ "The paragraph proves that planting programmes fail." (extreme, and conceding is not proving)
  • ✓ "The purpose is to argue that trees are infrastructure." ✗ "The purpose is to describe trees." (the author takes a position, so "describe" is too weak)
  • ✓ "The analogy to sweat clarifies a process." ✗ "The analogy to sweat shows that trees are alive like humans." (reads the comparison too literally)
  • ✓ "The final paragraph returns to the opening example." ✗ "The final paragraph introduces a new example." (it is the same street)

Worked examples

Excerpt 1 (natural science): [1] For most of the twentieth century, biologists believed that octopuses, being solitary hunters, had little reason to develop complex intelligence. [2] Studies over the past few decades have challenged this view. [3] Octopuses have been observed opening jars, escaping from tanks and recognizing individual human keepers. [4] Not every researcher agrees on what these behaviours mean, however, and some warn against reading human motives into an animal so different from us.

Worked example 1 — function of a sentence

Question: Sentence 3 mainly serves to:

  • A) provide examples that support the challenge described in sentence 2.
  • B) state the traditional view of octopus intelligence.
  • C) qualify the claim made in sentence 2.
  • D) introduce the disagreement among researchers.

Answer: A. Sentence 2 says studies have "challenged" the old view; sentence 3 lists the behaviours that make up that challenge. Job: illustrate / support.

  • B is wrong — Wrong place: that is sentence 1.
  • C is wrong — Sentence 4 qualifies ("Not every researcher agrees… however"), not sentence 3.
  • D is wrong — The disagreement starts in sentence 4, after the examples.

Excerpt 2 (social science, middle of an essay that argues for four-day school weeks): [3] It is true that a longer school day can tire younger children, and some parents will struggle to arrange care for the free day. These are real costs, and supporters of the plan should not pretend otherwise. [4] Still, the schools that have tried the system report higher attendance and…

Worked example 2 — function of a paragraph

Question: Paragraph 3 mainly functions to:

  • A) prove that the four-day week harms children.
  • B) describe the results of schools that tried the plan.
  • C) concede some disadvantages of the plan before the author returns to its benefits.
  • D) summarize the whole argument of the essay.

Answer: C. "It is true that…" and "real costs" are concession signals, and paragraph 4 begins with "Still", the turn back. Job: concession.

  • A is wrong — Too extreme and reversed: the author supports the plan; admitting costs is not proving harm.
  • B is wrong — Wrong place: results come in paragraph 4.
  • D is wrong — It covers only the costs, not the whole argument (too narrow).

Excerpt 3 (humanities): A translator of poetry faces a choice that a translator of instructions never does. A washing-machine manual must be clear; nobody cares whether it sounds beautiful. A poem, however, is like a piece of music played on a different instrument: the notes can stay the same, but the sound inevitably changes.

Worked example 3 — purpose of a detail (analogy)

Question: The author mentions a washing-machine manual mainly to:

  • A) criticize manuals for being badly translated.
  • B) show that poetry is harder to understand than technical writing.
  • C) argue that all translation is impossible.
  • D) contrast a kind of writing where only meaning matters with poetry, where sound matters too.

Answer: D. The manual is set against the poem ("however"): in a manual only clarity counts; in a poem sound counts too. Job: contrast.

  • A is wrong — Off-topic: the author never says manuals are badly translated.
  • B is wrong — Wrong idea: the contrast is about translating, not about understanding.
  • C is wrong — Too extreme: the author says the sound "changes", not that translation is impossible.

Use Excerpt 1 (octopuses) above.

Worked example 4 — organization

Question: Which choice best describes the way Excerpt 1 is organized?

  • A) A problem is presented and a solution is proposed.
  • B) Events are narrated in the order in which they happened to one scientist.
  • C) An older view is described, challenged with evidence, and then a note of caution is added.
  • D) Two competing theories are compared and one is proved correct.

Answer: C. Map: [1] old view, [2] challenge, [3] evidence, [4] caution. C follows every step.

  • A is wrong — There is no problem or solution in the excerpt.
  • B is wrong — No single scientist and no story in time order.
  • D is wrong — Too extreme: nothing is "proved", and sentence 4 shows the question is still open.
Classic traps
  • Topic instead of job. "It describes roots" names a topic; the answer you want says what the description DOES (concedes a problem).
  • Right job, wrong part. The choice correctly describes another sentence or paragraph (wrong place).
  • Qualify ≠ reject, concede ≠ agree completely. Watch the strength of the verb.
  • Too extreme. proves, always, all, impossible, completely: ACT authors rarely go that far.
  • Half-right structure. The first three steps of the organization choice fit, but the last step does not. Test every step.
  • Wrong purpose verb. "Describe" for an author who clearly argues, or "argue" for a neutral explanation.
Fast strategy for test day
  • Label each paragraph with 3 words while you read; you will need the map for structure questions.
  • Circle signal words in your mind: for example, although, it is true, still, also, like.
  • Ask: what would be lost if this part were deleted? Then say the job with a verb.
  • For organization questions, check the choice step by step against your map.
  • Answer purpose and organization questions last in the set: by then you know the passage well.

Practice

Now try a full social science passage (about 700 words) with 9 questions on purpose, function and structure. It is untimed, but aim for about 10 minutes. Make a 3-word map as you read, and for every question name the job with a verb before you look at the choices.

Remember
  • Purpose and structure questions ask WHY a part is there and HOW the parts fit together, not what they say.
  • Name the job with a verb: introduces, illustrates, qualifies, concedes, refutes, contrasts, extends, concludes.
  • Signal words reveal the job: for example → illustrates; although → qualifies; it is true → concedes; still/yet → rebuttal.
  • Main purpose must fit the first and last paragraphs and the author's attitude.
  • Organization choices must match every step of your 3-word map.
